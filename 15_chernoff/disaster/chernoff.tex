\section{concentration of mass and chernoff's inequality}

\subsection{example: binomial distribution}

consider the binomial distribution $\distbinom{n}{1/2}$ for various
values of $n$ as depicted in \figref{binomial:distribution} -- here we
think about the value of the variable as the number of heads in
flipping a fair coin $n$ times. clearly, as the value of $n$ increases
the probability of getting a number of heads that is significantly
smaller or larger than $n/2$ is tiny. here we are interested in
quantifying exactly how far can we divert from this expected
value. specifically, if $x \sim \distbinom{n}{1/2}$, then we would be
interested in bounding the probability $\Prob{ x > n/2 + \delta}$,
where $\delta = t \sigma_x = t \sqrt{n}/2$ (i.e., we are $t$ standard
deviations away from the expectation). for $t >2$, this probability is
roughly $2^{-t}$, which is what we prove here.

more surprisingly, if you look only on the middle of the distribution,
it looks the same after clipping away the uninteresting tails, see
\figref{binomial:distribution:middle}; that is, it looks more and more
like the normal distribution. this is a universal phenomena known the
\emphi{central limit theorem} -- every sum of nicely behaved random
variables behaves like the normal distribution. we unfortunately need
a more precise quantification of this behavior, thus the following.








\subsection{a restricted case of chernoff inequality via %
   games}
\subsubsection{chernoff games}

\paragraph*{the game.}

consider the game where a player starts with $y_0=1$ dollars. at every
round, the player can bet a certain amount $x$ (fractions are
fine). with probability half she loses her bet, and with probability
half she gains an amount equal to her bet.  the player is not allowed
to go all in -- because if she looses then the game is over. so it is
natural to ask what her optimal betting strategy is, such that in the
end of the game she has as much money as possible.


\paragraph*{is the game pointless?}

so, let $y_{i-1}$ be the money the player has in the end of the
$(i-1)$\th round, and she bets an amount $\psi_i \leq y_{i-1}$ in the
$i$\th round.  as such, in the end of the $i$\th round, she has
\begin{align*}
    y_{i} = \begin{cases}
        y_{i-1} - \psi_{i} & \text{\textsc{lose}: probability half}\\
        y_{i-1} + \psi_i & \text{\textsc{win}: probability half}
    \end{cases}
\end{align*}
dollars.  this game, in expectation, does not change the amount of
money the player has. indeed, we have
\begin{align*}
    \Ex{y_i\sep{y_{i-1}}}%
    =%
    \frac{1}{2}\pth{ y_{i-1} - \psi_i} + \frac{1}{2}\pth{y_{i-1} +
       \psi_i}%
    =%
    y_{i-1}.
\end{align*}
and as such, we have that
$\Ex{\bigl. y_i} = \Ex{\Ex{y_{i}\bigl| {y_{i-1}} } \bigl.\! } = \Ex{
   \bigl. y_{i-1}} = \cdots = \Ex{y_0 \bigl.} = 1\big.$. in
particular, $\Ex{y_n} = 1$ -- namely, on average, independent of the
player strategy she is not going to make any money in this game (and
she is allowed to change her bets after every round). unless, she is
lucky\footnote{``i would rather have a general who was lucky than one
   who was good.'' -- napoleon bonaparte.}...




\paragraph*{what about a lucky player?}

the player believes she will get lucky and wants to develop a strategy
to take advantage of it.  formally, she believes that she can win,
say, at least $(1+\delta)/2$ fraction of her bets (instead of the
predicted $1/2$) -- for example, if the bets are in the stock market,
she can improve her chances by doing more research on the companies
she is investing in\footnote{``i am a great believer in luck, and i
   find the harder i work, the more i have of it.'' -- thomas
   jefferson.}. unfortunately, the player does not know which rounds
she is going to be lucky in -- so she still needs to be careful.


\paragraph*{in a search of a good strategy.}

of course, there are many safe strategies the player can use, from not
playing at all, to risking only a tiny fraction of her money at each
round.  in other words, our quest here is to find the best strategy
that extracts the maximum benefit for the player out of her inherent
luck.

here, we restrict ourselves to a simple strategy -- at every round,
the player would bet $\beta$ fraction of her money, where $\beta$ is a
parameter to be determined.  specifically, in the end of the $i$\th
round, the player would have
\begin{align*}
    y_{i} = \begin{cases}
        (1-\beta) y_{i-1}  & \text{\textsc{lose}}\\
        (1+\beta) y_{i-1} & \text{\textsc{win}}.
    \end{cases}
\end{align*}
by our assumption, the player is going to win in at least
$m=(1+\delta)n/2$ rounds.  our purpose here is to figure out what the
value of $\beta$ should be so that player gets as rich as
possible\footnote{this optimal choice is known as kelly criterion,
   see \remref{kelly}.}.  now, if the player is successful in $\geq m$
rounds, out of the $n$ rounds of the game, then the amount of money
the player has, in the end of the game, is
\begin{align*}
    y_n%
    &\geq%
    (1-\beta)^{n-m} (1+\beta)^m%
    =%
    (1-\beta)^{n/2 -(\delta/2)n} (1+\beta)^{n/2 +(\delta/2)n}%
    =%
    \pth[]{\big. (1-\beta)(1+\beta)}^{n/2 -(\delta/2)n}
    (1+\beta)^{\delta n}%
    \\&%
    =%
    \pth[]{\big. 1-\beta^2 }^{n/2 -(\delta/2)n} (1+\beta)^{\delta n}%
    % \\&%
    \geq%
    {\big. \Exp\pth{ -2\beta^2} }^{n/2 -(\delta/2)n}
    {\Exp\pth{\beta/2}}^{\delta n}%
    % \\&%
    =%
    \Exp\pth{ \pth[]{ -\beta^2 +\beta^2 \delta + \beta \delta /2 } n
    }.
\end{align*}
to maximize this quantity, we choose $\beta = \delta/4$ (there is a
better choice, see \lemref{maximize}, but we use this value for the
simplicity of exposition).  thus, we have that $\ds y_n \geq \Exp\pth{
   \pth[]{ -\frac{\delta^2}{16} + \frac{\delta^3}{16} +
      \frac{\delta^2}{8} } n } \geq \Exp\pth{ \frac{\delta^2}{16} n
}$, proving the following.

\begin{lemma}%
    \lemlab{chernoff:games}%
    % 
    consider a chernoff game with $n$ rounds, starting with one
    dollar, where the player wins in $\geq (1+\delta)n/2$ of the
    rounds. if the player bets $\delta/4$ fraction of her current
    money, at all rounds, then in the end of the game the player would
    have at least $\Exp\pth{n \delta^2 / 16}$ dollars.
\end{lemma}


\begin{remark}
    note, that \lemref{chernoff:games} holds if the player wins any
    $\geq (1+\delta)n/2$ rounds. in particular, the statement does not
    require randomness by itself -- for our application, however, it
    is more natural and interesting to think about the player wins as
    being randomly distributed.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
    \remlab{kelly}%
    %
    interestingly, the idea of choosing the best fraction to bet is an
    old and natural question arising in investments strategies, and
    the right fraction to use is known as
    \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/kelly_criterion}{\emphi{kelly
          criterion}}, going back to kelly's work from 1956
    \cite{k-niir-56}.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{chernoff's inequality}

the above implies that if a player is lucky, then she is going to
become filthy rich\footnote{not that there is anything wrong with that
   -- many of my friends are filthy,}. intuitively, this should be a
pretty rare event -- because if the player is rich, then (on average)
many other people have to be poor. we are thus ready for the kill.

\begin{theorem}[chernoff's inequality]
    let $x_1,\ldots, x_n$ be $n$ independent random variables, where
    $x_i=0$ or $x_i=1$ with equal probability. then, for any $\delta
    \in (0,1/2)$, we have that
    \begin{equation*}
        \probltr \bigl[ \sum_i x_i \geq (1+\delta)\frac{n}{2} \bigr]%
        \leq%
        \Exp\bigl( -
        \frac{\delta^2}{ 16} n \bigr).
    \end{equation*}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    imagine that we are playing the chernoff game above, with
    $\beta=\delta/4$, starting with $1$ dollar, and let $y_i$ be the
    amount of money in the end of the $i$\th round.  here $x_i=1$
    indicates that the player won the $i$\th round.  we have, by
    \lemref{chernoff:games} and markov's inequality, that
    \begin{equation*}
        \probltr \bigl[{ \sum_i x_i \geq (1+\delta)\frac{n}{2}} \bigr]%
        \leq %
        \probltr \bigl[{ y_n \geq \Exp \bigl( {\frac{n \delta^2}{16}}
           \bigr)
        } \bigr] %
        \leq%
        \frac{\Ex{y_n}\bigl. }{\bigl. \Exp\pth{n \delta^2 / 16}}%
        =%
        \frac{1}{\bigl.  \Exp\pth{n \delta^2 / 16}} =
        \Exp \bigl( -\frac{\delta^2}{ 16} n  \bigr).
    \end{equation*}
\end{proof}

\medskip%
\noindent%
\textbf{this is crazy -- so intuition maybe?} %
if the player is $(1+\delta)/2$-lucky then she can make a lot of
money; specifically, at least $f(\delta)=\Exp\pth{n \delta^2 / 16}$
dollars by the end of the game. namely, beating the odds has
significant monetary value, and this value grows quickly with
$\delta$. since we are in a ``zero-sum'' game settings, this event
should be very rare indeed. under this interpretation, of course, the
player needs to know in advance the value of $\delta$ -- so imagine
that she guesses it somehow in advance, or she plays the game in
parallel with all the possible values of $\delta$, and she settles on
the instance that maximizes her profit.

\paragraph*{can one do better?}
no, not really. chernoff inequality is tight (this is a challenging
homework exercise) up to the constant in the exponent. the best bound
i know for this version of the inequality has $1/2$ instead of $1/16$
in the exponent. note, however, that no real effort was taken to
optimize the constants -- this is not the purpose of this write-up.



\subsubsection{some low level boring calculations}

above, we used the following well known facts.
\begin{lemma}
    \begin{enumerate*}[label=(\alph*)]
        \item markov's inequality. for any positive random variable
        $x$ and $t > 0$, we have $\Prob{x \geq t} \leq \Ex{x}/t$.
        
        \item for any two random variables $x$ and $y$, we have that
        $\Ex{\bigl. x} = \Ex{ \bigl. \Ex{ x \left| \bigl. {y}
              \right. }}$.
        
        \item for $x \in (0,1)$, $1+x \geq e^{x/2}$.
        
        \item for $x \in (0,1/2)$, $1-x \geq e^{-2x}$.
    \end{enumerate*}
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}
    \lemlab{maximize}%
    % 
    the quantity $\Exp\pth{ \pth[]{ -\beta^2 +\beta^2 \delta + \beta
          \delta /2 } n }$ is maximal for $ \beta =
    \frac{\delta}{4(1-\delta)}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    we have to maximize $f(\beta) = -\beta^2 +\beta^2 \delta + \beta
    \delta /2$ by choosing the correct value of $\beta$ (as a function
    of $\delta$, naturally).
    \begin{math}
        f'(\beta) = -2 \beta +2\beta \delta + \delta/2 = 0%
        \quad\iff\quad 2(\delta-1)\beta = - \delta/2 \quad\iff\quad
        \beta = \frac{\delta}{4(1-\delta)}.
    \end{math}

\begin{fragment}{\deflabel{c_special}}
    \begin{theorem}
        \thmlab{chernoff:special}%
        % 
        let $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ be $n$ independent random variables,
        such that $\Prob{x_i =1} =\Prob{x_i=-1} = \frac{1}{2}$, for
        $i=1,\ldots, n$. let $y = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i$. then, for any
        $\delta >0$, we have
        \begin{align*}
            \Prob{\bigl. y \geq \delta} \leq \Exp \pth{-\delta^2/2n}.
        \end{align*}
    \end{theorem}%
    % 
\end{fragment}
% 
\begin{proof}
    clearly, for an arbitrary $t$, to specified shortly, we have
    \begin{align*}
        \Prob{y \geq \delta} = \Prob{\Exp \pth{ t y } \geq \Exp
           \pth{t\delta}} \leq \frac{\Ex{\Exp \pth{t y}}}{\Exp
           \pth{t\delta}},
    \end{align*}
    the first part follows by the fact that $\Exp(\cdot)$ preserve
    ordering, and the second part follows by the markov inequality.
    
    observe that
    \begin{align*}
        \Ex{ \Exp\pth{t x_i} }%
        &=%
        \frac{1}{2} e^{t} + \frac{1}{2}e^{-t} =
        \frac{e^t + e^{-t}}{2}\\
        &=%
        \frac{1}{2} \pth{1 + \frac{t}{1!} + \frac{t^2}{2!} +
           \frac{t^3}{3!} + \cdots}
        \\
        &+%
        \frac{1}{2}\pth{1 -\frac{t}{1!} + \frac{t^2}{2!} -
           \frac{t^3}{3!}
           + \cdots}\\
        &=%
        \;\; \;\pth{1 +\;\;\;\;\; + \frac{t^2}{2!} + \;\;\;\; +\cdots
           + \frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}  + \cdots},
    \end{align*}
    by the taylor expansion of $\Exp(\cdot)$. note, that $(2k)!\geq
    (k!) 2^k$, and thus
    \begin{align*}
        \Ex{ \Exp \pth{ t x_i } } = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}
        \frac{t^{2i}}{(2i)!} \leq \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}
        \frac{t^{2i}}{2^i(i!)} = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{i!}\pth{
           \frac{t^2}{2}}^i = \Exp \pth{t^2/2},
    \end{align*}
    again, by the taylor expansion of $\Exp(\cdot)$. next, by the
    independence of the $x_i$s, we have
    \begin{align*}
        \Ex{\Exp \pth{t y}}%
        =%
        \Ex{\Exp \pth{\sum_i t x_i}}%
        =%
        \Ex{\prod_i \Exp\pth{t x_i}}%
        =%
        \prod_{i=1}^{n} \Ex{\Exp\pth{t x_i}} \leq%
        \prod_{i=1}^{n} e^{t^2/2}%
        =%
        e^{n t^2/2}.
    \end{align*}
    we have
    \begin{math}
        \ds \Prob{y \geq \delta} \leq \frac{\Exp\pth{n t^2/2}}{\Exp
           \pth{t\delta}} = \Exp \pth{n t^2/2 - t\delta}.
    \end{math}
    
    next, by minimizing the above quantity for $t$, we set $t =
    \delta/n$. we conclude,
    \begin{align*}
        \Prob{ y \geq \delta } \leq \Exp
        \pth{\frac{n}{2}\pth{\frac{\delta}{n}}^2 -
           \frac{\delta}{n}\delta} = \Exp \pth{- \frac{\delta^2}{2n}}.
    \end{align*}
\end{proof}

by the symmetry of $y$, we get the following:
\begin{corollary}
    \corlab{chernoff:special}%
    %
    let $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ be $n$ independent random variables, such
    that $\Prob{x_i =1} =\Prob{x_i=-1} = \frac{1}{2}$, for
    $i=1,\ldots, n$. let $y = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i$. then, for any $\delta
    >0$, we have
    \begin{math}
        \probltr[ \cardin{y} \geq \delta ] \leq
        2\Exp\pth{-\delta^2/2n}.
    \end{math}
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
    before going any further, it is might be instrumental to
    understand what this inequalities imply.  consider then case where
    $x_i$ is either zero or one with probability half. in this case
    $\mu = \Ex{y} = n/2$. set $\delta = t\sqrt{n}$ ($\sqrt{\mu}$ is
    approximately the standard deviation of $x$ if $p_i = 1/2$).  we

\end{remark}

        have
        \begin{equation*}

               \nicefrac{e^\delta}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}}^\mu.
        \end{equation*}

\begin{proof}
    we have $\displaystyle \Prob{ x > (1+\delta)\mu } = \Prob{ e^{t x}
       > e^{t(1+\delta)\mu}}$.  by the markov inequality, we have:
    \begin{align*}
        \Prob{\big. x > (1+\delta)\mu } < \frac{ \Ex{e^{t
                 x}}}{e^{t(1+\delta)\mu}}
    \end{align*}
    
    on the other hand,
    \begin{align*}
        \Ex{e^{t x} } = \Ex{ e^{t(x_1 + x_2 \ldots + x_n)} } =
        \Ex{e^{t x_1}} \cdots \Ex{e^{t x_n}}.
    \end{align*}
    namely,
    \begin{align*}
        \Prob{ x > (1+\delta)\mu } < \frac{\prod_{i=1}^n \Ex{e^{t
                 x_i}}}{e^{t(1+\delta)\mu}} = \frac{\prod_{i=1}^n
           \pth{ (1-p_i)e^0 + p_i e^{t }}} {e^{t(1+\delta)\mu}} =
        \frac{\prod_{i=1}^n \pth{ 1+ p_i(e^{t} -1 ) }}
        {e^{t(1+\delta)\mu}}.
    \end{align*}
    let $y = p_i (e^{t} - 1 )$. we know that $1+y < e^y$ (since $y >
    0$). thus,
    \begin{align*}
        \Prob{ x > (1+\delta)\mu }%
        &<%
        \frac{\prod_{i=1}^n { \Exp( p_i(e^{t} -1 )) }}
        {e^{t(1+\delta)\mu}}%
        =%
        \frac{\Exp \pth{ \sum_{i=1}^n p_i(e^{t} -1 ) }}
        {e^{t(1+\delta)\mu}}%
        \\&%
        =%
        \frac{\Exp \pth{ (e^{t} -1 )\sum_{i=1}^n p_i }}
        {e^{t(1+\delta)\mu}} = \frac{\Exp \pth{ (e^{t} -1 )\mu }}
        {e^{t(1+\delta)\mu}} = \pth{\frac{\Exp \pth{ e^{t} -1 }}
           {e^{t(1+\delta)}}}^\mu%
        \\&%
        =%
        \pth{\frac{\Exp \pth{ \delta }}
           {(1+\delta)^{(1+\delta)}}}^\mu,
    \end{align*}
    if we set $t = \log ( 1+\delta)$.    
\end{proof}

\subsection{the lower tail}

we need the following low level lemma.
\begin{lemma}
    \lemlab{exp:lower:bound:2}%
    %
    for $x \in [0,1)$, we have $(1-x)^{1-x} \geq \Exp( -x + x^2/2)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    for $x \in [0,1)$, we have, by the taylor expansion, that
    $\ln (1-x) = - \sum_{i=1}^\infty ({x^i}/{i})$. as such, we have
    \begin{equation*}
        (1-x)\ln(1-x) %
        =%
        - (1-x) \sum_{i=1}^\infty \frac{x^i}{i}
        =%
        -  \sum_{i=1}^\infty \frac{x^i}{i}
        +  \sum_{i=1}^\infty \frac{x^{i+1}}{i}
        =%
        -x + \sum_{i=2}^\infty \pth{\frac{x^i}{i-1} -\frac{x^i}{i}}%
        =%
        -x + \sum_{i=2}^\infty \frac{x^i}{i(i-1)}.
    \end{equation*}
    this implies that $(1-x)\ln(1-x) \geq -x + x^2/2$, which implies
    the claim by exponentiation.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
    \thmlab{chernoff:2}%
    % 
    let $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ be $n$ independent random variables, where
    \begin{math}
        \Prob{ \bigl. x_i = 1 } =p_i,
    \end{math}
    \begin{math}
        \Prob{ \bigl. x_i = 0 } =q_i = 1-p_i, 
    \end{math}
    for all $i$.  for $x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$, its expectation is
    $\mu = \Ex{\bigl. x} = \sum_i p_i$.  we have that
    \begin{equation*}
        \Prob{\bigl. x < (1-\delta)\mu } <
        \bigl[  \frac{e^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}} \bigr]^\mu .
%        \qquad\text{ or alternatively }\qquad%
%        \Prob{\bigl. x < (1-\delta)\mu } < \Exp\pth{-\mu\delta^2/2}.
    \end{equation*}
    for any positive $\tau > 1$, we have that
    \begin{math}
      \Prob{ x < \mu/\tau \bigr.} %
      \leq%
      \Exp\pth{-\pth{1 - \frac{1 + \ln \tau}{\tau}} \mu}.
  \end{math}
\end{theorem}


\begin{proof}
    we follow the same proof template seen already. for
    $t = - \ln( 1-\delta ) > 0$, we have
    \begin{math}
        \Ex{\Exp( -t x_i)}%
        =%
        (1-p_i) e^0 + p_i e^{-t}%
        =%
        1-p_i + p_i ( 1-\delta)%
        =%
        1 - p_i\delta%
        \leq%
        \Exp \pth{ -p_i \delta}.
    \end{math}
    as such, we have
    \begin{align*}
      \Prob{ x < (1-\delta)\mu \bigr.} %
      &=%
        \Prob{ -x > -(1-\delta)\mu \bigr.} %
        = %
        \Prob{ \Exp( -t x)  > \Exp\pth{-t(1-\delta)\mu} \bigr.}
        \leq %
        \frac{\prod_{i=1}^n \Ex{\Exp( -t x_i)}}{\Exp\pth{-t(1-\delta)\mu}}\\
      &
        \leq%
        \frac{\Exp\pth{-\sum_{i=1}^n  p_i\delta}}
        {\Exp\pth{-t(1-\delta)\mu}}  %
        =%
        % \\
        % &
            \bigl[  \frac{e^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}} \bigr]^\mu.
    \end{align*}
    for the last inequality, set $\delta = 1-1/\tau$, and observe that 
    \begin{align*}
      \Prob{ x < (1-\delta)\mu \bigr.} %
      &
        \leq%
        \bigl[  \frac{e^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}} \bigr]^\mu
        =%
        \bigl[  \frac{\Exp\pth{-1 + 1/\tau}}
        {(1/\tau)^{1/\tau}} \bigr]^\mu
        =%
        \Exp\pth{-\pth{1 - \frac{1 + \ln \tau}{\tau}} \mu}.
    \end{align*}
\end{proof}

    \begin{lemma}
        \lemlab{chernoff:small:delta:1}%
        % 
        Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be $n$ independent Bernoulli trials, where
        \begin{math}
            \Prob{  X_i = 1 } =p_i,
        \end{math}
        and 
        \begin{math}
            \Prob{  X_i = 0 } =1- p_i,
        \end{math}
        for $i=1,\ldots, n$.  Let $X = \sum_{i=1}^{b} X_i$, and
        $\mu = \Ex{\bigl. X} = \sum_i p_i$.  For $\delta, \in (0,1)$,
        we have
        \begin{equation*}
            \Prob{ \bigl. X > (1+\delta)\mu } < \Exp \pth{-\mu \delta^2 /
               3}.            
        \end{equation*}
    \end{lemma}
\end{fragment}
\begin{proof}
    By \thmref{Chernoff:simplified}, it is sufficient to prove, for
    $\delta\in [0,1]$, that
    \begin{align*}
      & \pth{ \frac{e^\delta}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}}^\mu \leq
        \Exp\pth{- \frac{\mu \delta^2}{c} }
        \iff
        \mu \bigl( \delta -(1+\delta) \ln(1+\delta) \bigr)
        \leq
        - \mu \delta^2/c
      \\[0.2cm]
      \iff
      &
        f(\delta) = \delta^2/c + \delta -(1+\delta) \ln(1+\delta) 
        \leq 0.
    \end{align*}
    We have
    \begin{equation*}
        f'(\delta)
        = 2\delta/c  - \ln(1+\delta).
        \qquad\text{ and }\qquad%
        f''(\delta)
        = 2/c   - \frac{1}{1+\delta}.
    \end{equation*}
    For $c=3$, we have $f''(\delta) \leq 0$ for $\delta \in [0,1/2]$,
    and $f''(\delta) \geq 0$ for $\delta \in [1/2,1]$. Namely,
    $f'(\delta)$ achieves its maximum either at $0$ or $1$. As
    $f'(0) = 0$ and $f'(1) = 2/3 - \ln 2 \approx -0.02<0$, we conclude
    that $f'(\delta) \leq 0$. Namely, $f$ is a monotonically
    decreasing function in $[0,1]$, which implies that
    $f(\delta) \leq 0$, for all $\delta$ in this range, thus implying
    the claim.
\end{proof}

        \Exp \pth{ \Bigl. - \frac{\mu \delta \ln \delta}{2}}.%
    \end{math}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Observe that
    \begin{align}
        \Prob{\Bigl. X > (1+\delta)\mu }%
        <%
        \pth{ \frac{e^\delta}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}}^\mu%
        =%
        \Exp \pth{ \Bigl.\ts \mu \delta - \mu\pth{1+\delta}\ln \pth{
              1+\delta}}.%
        \eqlab{c:imply}%
    \end{align}
    As such, we have
    \begin{align*}
        \Prob{\Bigl. X > (1+\delta)\mu }%
        &<%
        \Exp \pth{ \Bigl. - \mu\pth{1+\delta} \pth{ \bigl. \ln \pth{
                 1+\delta} - 1 }}%
        \leq %
        \Exp \pth{ \Bigl. - \mu \delta { \bigl. \ln {
                 \frac{1+\delta}{e}} }}%
        % \\&%
        \leq %
        \Exp \pth{ \Bigl. - \frac{\mu \delta \ln \delta}{2}},
    \end{align*}
    since for $x \geq e^2$ we have that
    \begin{math}
        \ds%
        \frac{1+x}{e} \geq \sqrt{x} \iff%
        \ln \frac{1+x}{e} \geq \frac{\ln{x}}{2}.
    \end{math}
    % \bigskip
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    \lemlab{chandra:wanted:it}%
    % 
        Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be $n$ independent Bernoulli trials, where
        \begin{math}
            \Prob{  X_i = 1 } =p_i,
        \end{math}
        and
        \begin{math}
            \Prob{  X_i = 0 } =1- p_i,
        \end{math}
        for $i=1,\ldots, n$.  Let $Y = \sum_{i=1}^{b} X_i$, and
        $\mu = \Ex{ Y} = \sum_i p_i$.  For $\delta \in (0,1]$, and
        $\BadProb \in (0,1]$, we have
        \begin{equation*}
            \Prob{ \bigl. Y > (1+\delta)\mu + \frac{3\ln
                  \BadProb^{-1}}{\delta^2} }
            < \BadProb.
        \end{equation*}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\xi = \delta + \frac{3 \ln \BadProb^{-1}}{\mu \delta^2}$.  If
    $\xi \geq 2e -1 \approx 4.43$, by \lemref{chernoff:delta:big}, we
    have
    \begin{equation*}
        \alpha %
        =%
        \Prob{ \bigl. Y > (1+\delta)\mu + \frac{3\ln
              \BadProb^{-1}}{\delta^2} }%
        =%
        \Prob{ \bigl. Y > (1+\xi)\mu  }
        \leq%
        2^{-\mu ( 1+\xi)}
        <%
        % 2^{-\mu \xi}
        % <%
        \BadProb,       
    \end{equation*}
    since
    $-\mu(1+\xi) > -\mu \xi > \mu \frac{3 \ln \BadProb^{-1}}{\mu
       \delta^2} > \log_2 \BadProb^{-1}$, since $\delta \in (0,1]$.

    If $\xi \leq 6$, then by \lemref{chernoff:delta:gap}, we have
    \begin{equation*}
        \alpha%
        =%
        \Prob{ \bigl. Y > (1+\xi)\mu  }%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ - \mu \xi^2  / 5 }
        \leq %
        \BadProb,
    \end{equation*}
    since
    \begin{equation*}
        - \frac{\mu}{5} \xi^2%
        =%
        - \frac{\mu}{5} \pth{  \delta + \frac{3 \ln \BadProb^{-1}}{\mu
              \delta^2} }^2%
        >%        
        - \frac{\mu}{5} \pth{ 2 \cdot  \delta \cdot \frac{3 \ln \BadProb^{-1}}{\mu
              \delta^2} }%
        =%
        -\frac{6}{5} \cdot \frac{\ln \BadProb}{\delta} >
        -\ln \BadProb.        
    \end{equation*}
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
    \thmlab{h:mysterious}%
    %
    Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n \in [0,1]$ be $n$ independent random
    variables, let $X = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$, and let $\mu = \Ex{X}$. We
    have that
    \begin{math}
        \ds%
        \Prob{\Bigl.X - \mu \geq \eta }%
        \leq%
        \pth{ \frac{\mu}{\mu + \eta} }^{\mu + \eta} %
        \pth{ \frac{n - \mu}{ n - \mu - \eta} }^{n - \mu - \eta}.
    \end{math}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Let $s\geq 1$ be some arbitrary parameter. By the standard
    arguments, we have
    \begin{align*}
        \gamma%
        =%
        \Prob{\Bigl. X \geq \mu + \eta } %
        =%
        \Prob{\Bigl. s^X \geq s^{\mu + \eta} } %
        \leq%
        \frac{\Ex{s^X}}{s^{\mu + \eta}}%
%        \leq%
        % \Prob{ s^X \geq s^{\mu + \eta} } %
        =%
        s^{-\mu - \eta} \prod_{i=1}^n \Ex{s^{X_i}}.
    \end{align*}
    By calculations, see \lemref{technical} below, one can show that
    $\Ex{s^{X_1}} \leq 1 + (s-1)\Ex{X_i}$. As such, by the AM-GM
    inequality\footnote{The inequality between arithmetic and
       geometric means:
       $(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i) /n \geq \sqrt[n]{x_1\cdots x_n}$.}, we have
    that
    \begin{align*}
        \prod_{i=1}^n \Ex{s^{X_i}}%
        \leq%
        \prod_{i=1}^n \pth{\Bigl. 1 + (s-1)\Ex{X_i}}%
        \leq%
        \pth{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \pth{\Bigl. 1 +
              (s-1)\Ex{X_i}}}^n%
        =%
        \pth{1 + (s-1) \frac{\mu}{n}}^n.%
    \end{align*}
    Setting
    \begin{math}
        \ds%
        s%
        % =%
        % \frac{ (\mu/n + \eta/ n )(1-\mu/n)}{(\mu/n)(1-\mu/n -
        %    \eta/n)}%
        =%
        \frac{ (\mu + \eta )(n-\mu)}{\mu(n-\mu - \eta)}%
        =%
        \frac{ \mu n - \mu^2 +\eta n - \eta \mu}{\mu n - \mu^2 -
           \eta\mu }%
    \end{math}
    we have that
    \begin{align*}
        \ds%
        1 + (s-1)\frac{\mu}{n}%
        &=%
        1 +%
        \frac{\eta n}{\mu n - \mu^2 - \eta\mu } \cdot \frac{\mu}{n}%
        =%
        1 +%
        \frac{\eta }{n - \mu - \eta } %
        =%
        \frac{ n- \mu}{n-\mu - \eta}.%
    \end{align*}
    As such, we have that
    \begin{align*}
        \gamma%
        &\leq%
        s^{-\mu - \eta} \prod_{i=1}^n \Ex{s^{X_i}}%
        =%
        \pth{\frac{\mu(n-\mu - \eta)}{ (\mu + \eta )(n-\mu)}}^{\mu +
           \eta} \pth{\frac{ n- \mu}{n-\mu - \eta}}^n%
        %\\&%
        =%
        \pth{\frac{\mu}{ (\mu + \eta )}}^{\mu + \eta} \pth{\frac{ n-
              \mu}{n-\mu - \eta}}^{n-\mu -\eta}.
    \end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    Setting $s = (\mu + \eta)/\mu$ in the proof of 
    \thmref{h:mysterious}, we have
    
    \medskip%
    \centerline{%
    \begin{math}
        \Prob{\Bigl.X - \mu \geq \eta }%
        \leq %
        \pth{\frac{\mu}{\mu + \eta}}^{\mu + \eta}%
        \pth{1 + \pth{\frac{\mu + \eta}{\mu}-1} \frac{\mu}{n}}^n%
        = %
        \pth{\frac{\mu}{\mu + \eta}}^{\mu + \eta} \pth{1 +
           \frac{\eta}{n}}^n.%
    \end{math}%
 }
\end{remark}


\begin{corollary}
    \corlab{easy:form}%
    %
    Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n \in [0,1]$ be $n$ independent random
    variables, let $\Bigl.\overline{X} = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i/n$,
    $p = \Ex{\overline{X}} = \mu/n$ and $q = 1-p$. Then, we have that
    \begin{math}
        \ds%
        \Prob{\bigl.\, \overline{X} - p \geq t \, }%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ \bigl. nf(t)},
    \end{math}
    for
    \begin{align}
        \eqlab{f:t:cher}%
        \ds%
        f(t) = (p+t)\ln \frac{p}{p+t} + (q-t)\ln \frac{q}{q-t}.
    \end{align}%
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}
    \thmlab{useful:chernoff}%
    %
    Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n \in [0,1]$ be $n$ independent random
    variables, let $\overline{X} = (\sum_{i=1}^n X_i)/n\bigl.$, and
    let $p = \Ex{X}$. We have that
    \begin{math}%
        \ds%
        \Prob{\bigl.\,\overline{X} - p \geq t\, }%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ -2 n t^2}
    \end{math}
    and 
    \begin{math}%
        \ds%
        \Prob{\bigl.\, \overline{X} - p \leq -t \, }%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ -2 n t^2}.
    \end{math}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Let $p = \mu/n$, $q =1-p$, and let $f(t)$ be the function from
    \Eqref{f:t:cher}, for $t \in (-p,q)$. Now, we have that
    \begin{align*}
        \ds%
        f'(t)%
        &=%
        \ln \frac{p}{p+t}%
        + (p+t) \frac{p+t}{p} \pth{-\frac{p}{(p+t)^2}}%
        - \ln \frac{q}{q-t}%
        - (q-t)\frac{q-t}{q}\frac{q}{(q-t)^2}%
        =%
        \ln \frac{p}{p+t} - \ln \frac{q}{q-t}%
        \\&%
        =%
        \ln \frac{p(q-t)}{q(p+t)}. %
    \end{align*}
    As for the second derivative, we have
    \begin{align*}
        \ds%
        f''(t)%
        &=%
        \frac{q\bcancel{(p+t)}}{p(q-t)} %
        \cdot \frac{p}{q} \cdot%\\
        \frac{(p+t)(-1) - (q-t) } {(p+t)^{\bcancel{2}}}. %
        =%
        \frac{-p-t - q+t } {(q-t)(p+t)} %
        =%
        - \frac{1 } {(q-t)(p+t)} %
        \leq%
        -4.
    \end{align*}
    Indeed, $t\in (-p,q)$ and the denominator is minimized for
    $t = (q-p)/2$, and as such
    \begin{math} 
        (q-t)(p+t)%
        \leq%
        \pth{\Bigl.2q -(q- p)} \pth{\Bigl. 2p +(q -p)}/4 =%
        (p+q)^2/4 = 1/4.
    \end{math}

    Now, $f(0) =0$ and $f'(0) = 0$, and by Taylor's expansion, we have
    that 
    \begin{math}
        \ds%
        f(t) = f(0) + f'(0)t + \frac{f''(x)}{2} t^2 \leq -2 t^2,
    \end{math}
    where $x$ is between $0$ and $t$.

    The first bound now readily follows from plugging this bound into
    \corref{easy:form}. The second bound follows by considering the
    random variants $Y_i =1-X_i$, for all $i$, and plugging this into the
    first bound. Indeed, for $\overline{Y} = 1-\overline{X}$, we have
    that $q = \Ex{\overline{Y}}$, and then
    \begin{math}
        \overline{X} - p \leq -t%
        \iff%
        t \leq p - \overline{X}%
        \iff%
        t \leq 1-q - (1- \overline{Y}) = \overline{Y} - q.
    \end{math}
    Thus, 
    \begin{math}%
        \ds%
        \Prob{\Bigl.\overline{X} - p \leq -t }%
        = \Prob{\Bigl.\overline{Y} - q \geq t }%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ -2 n t^2}.
    \end{math}
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    \corlab{useful:chernoff:2}%
    %
    Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n \in [0,1]$ be $n$ independent random
    variables, let $Y = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$, and let $\mu = \Ex{X}$. For
    any $\Delta > 0$, we have
    \begin{math}%
        \ds%
        \Prob{\bigl.\,Y - \mu \geq \Delta\, }%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ -2 \Delta^2/n}
    \end{math}
    and 
    \begin{math}%
        \ds%
        \Prob{\bigl.\, Y - \mu \leq -\Delta \, }%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ -2 \Delta^2/n}.
    \end{math}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    For $\overline{X} = Y/n$, $p =\mu /n $, and $t = \Delta/n$, by
    \thmref{useful:chernoff}, we have
    \begin{equation*}
        \Prob{\bigl.Y - \mu \geq \Delta }%
        =%
        \Prob{\bigl. \smash{\overline{X}} - p \geq t }%
        \ds%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ -2 nt^2 }
        =%
        \Exp\pth{ -2 \Delta^2 /n  }.
    \end{equation*}
\end{proof}


\begin{theorem}
    \thmlab{special:h:i:e}%
    %    
    Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n \in [0,1]$ be $n$ independent random
    variables, let $X= (\sum_{i=1}^n X_i)$, and let $\mu = \Ex{X}$. We
    have that
    \begin{math}%
        \ds%
        \Prob{\bigl.{X} - \mu \geq \eps \mu }%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ -\eps^2 \mu / 4}
    \end{math}
    and 
    \begin{math}%
        \ds%
        \Prob{\bigl. X - \mu \leq -\eps \mu }%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ - \eps^2 \mu/2}.
    \end{math}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Let $p = \mu /n$, and let $g(x) = f(px)$, for $x \in [0,1]$ and
    $xp < q$. As before, computing the derivative of $g$, we have
    \begin{align*}
        g'(x)%
        =%
        p f'(xp)%
        =%
        p\ln \frac{p(q-xp)}{q(p+xp)} %
        =%
        p\ln \frac{q-xp}{q(1+x)} %
        \leq%
        p\ln \frac{1}{1+x}%
        \leq%
        - \frac{px}{2}, %
    \end{align*}
    since $(q-xp)/q$ is maximized for $x=0$, and
    \begin{math}
        \ln \frac{1}{1+x} \leq -x/2,
    \end{math}
    for $x \in [0,1]$, as can be easily verified%
    \footnote{Indeed, this is equivalent to
       \begin{math}
           \frac{1}{1+x} \leq e^{-x/2}%
           \iff%
           e^{x/2} \leq 1+x,
       \end{math}
       which readily holds for $x \in [0,1]$.%
    }. %
    Now, $g(0) =f (0) = 0$, and by integration, we have that
    \begin{math}
        g(x) =%
        \int_{y=0}^x g'(y) \mathrm{d} y %
        \leq%
        \int_{y=0}^x (-py/2) \mathrm{d} y %
        = -px^2/4.
    \end{math}
    Now, plugging into \corref{easy:form}, we get
    that the desired probability 
    \begin{math}
        \Prob{\bigl.{X} - \mu \geq \eps \mu }%
    \end{math}
    is %equal to
    \begin{align*}%
        \ds%
        % \Prob{\Bigl.{X} - \mu \geq \eps \mu }%
        % =%
        \Prob{\Bigl.\overline{X} - p \geq \eps p }%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ \bigl. nf(\eps p)} = \Exp\pth{ \Bigl. n g(\eps)}
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ \bigl. - p n\eps^2 /4}%
        =%
        \Exp\pth{ \bigl. - \mu\eps^2 /4}.
    \end{align*}
    
    As for the other inequality, set $h(x) = g(-x) = f(-xp)$. Then
    \begin{align*}
        h'(x)%
        &=%
        -p f'(-xp)%
        =%
        -p\ln \frac{p(q+xp)}{q(p-xp)} %
        =%
        p\ln \frac{q(1-x)}{q+xp} %
        =%
        p\ln \frac{q-xq}{q+xp} %
        =%
        p\ln\pth{ 1 - x\frac{p+q}{q+xp}} %
        \\&%
        =%
        p\ln\pth{ 1 - x\frac{1}{q+xp}} %
        \leq%
        p\ln\pth{ 1 - x} %
        \leq%
        -p x,
    \end{align*}
    since $1-x \leq e^{-x}$.  By integration, as before, we conclude
    that $h(x) \leq -p x^2/2$.  Now, plugging into \corref{easy:form},
    we get
    \begin{math}%
        \ds%
        \Prob{\bigl.{X} - \mu \leq -\eps \mu }%
        =%
        \Prob{\bigl.\overline{X} - p \leq -\eps p }%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ \bigl. n f(-\eps p)}%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ \bigl. n h(\eps )}%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ \bigl. -n p \eps^2/2}%
        %\\&%
        \leq%
        \Exp\pth{ \bigl. - \mu \eps^2/2}.
    \end{math}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Some technical lemmas}

\begin{lemma}
    \lemlab{technical}%
    %
    Let $X \in [0,1]$ be a random variable, and let $s \geq 1$. Then
    $\Ex{s^X} \leq 1 + (s-1)\Ex{X}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    For the sake of simplicity of exposition, assume that $X$ is a
    discrete random variable, and that there is a value
    $\alpha \in (0,1/2)$, such that $\beta = \Prob{X = \alpha } > 0
    $.
    Consider the modified random variable $X'$, such that
    $\Prob{ X' = 0 } = \Prob{X = 0} + \beta/2$, and
    $\Prob{X' = 2\alpha } = \Prob{X = \alpha } + \beta/2$. Clearly,
    $\Ex{X} = \Ex{X'}$. Next, observe that
    \begin{math}
        \Ex{s^{X'}} - \Ex{s^X}%
        =%
        (\beta/2)( s^{2\alpha} + s^{0}) - \beta s^{\alpha} \geq 0,
    \end{math}
    by the convexity of $s^x$. We conclude that $ \Ex{s^X}$ achieves
    its maximum if takes only the values $0$ and $1$. But then, we
    have that 
    \begin{math}
        \Ex{s^X}%
        =%
        \Prob{X=0}s^0 + \Prob{X=1}s^1%
        =%
        (1-\Ex{X}) + \Ex{X} s %
        =%
        1 + ( s-1)\Ex{X},
    \end{math}
    as claimed.
\end{proof}




\section{Hoeffding's inequality}
\seclab{h:iequality}

In this section, we prove a generalization of Chernoff's
inequality. The proof is considerably more tedious, and it is included
here for the sake of completeness.

\begin{lemma}
    Let $X$ be a random variable.  If $\;\Ex{X}=0$ and $a\le X\le b$,
    then for any $s > 0$, we have $\displaystyle \Ex{ e^{sX} } \le
    \Exp\pth{ s^{2}(b-a)^{2} / 8 }$.

    \lemlab{exponent:blowup}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Let $a\le x \le b$ and observe that $x$ can be written as a convex
    combination of $a$ and $b$. In particular, we have
    \begin{align*}
        x = \lambda a + (1-\lambda) b \text{\;\; ~ for ~\; } \lambda =
        \frac{b-x}{b-a} \in \pbrcS{ 0,1 }.
    \end{align*}
    Since $s > 0$, the function $\Exp(sx)$ is convex, and as such
    \begin{align*}
        e^{sx} \le \frac{b-x}{b-a} e^{sa} + \frac{x-a}{b-a} e^{sb},
    \end{align*}
    since we have that $f(\lambda x + (1-\lambda) y) \leq \lambda f(x)
    + (1-\lambda) f(y)$ if $f(\cdot)$ is a convex function.  Thus, for
    a random variable $X$, by linearity of expectation, we have
    \begin{align*}
        \Ex{e^{sX}}
        &\le \Ex{ \frac{b-X}{b-a} e^{sa} +
           \frac{X-a}{b-a} e^{sb}} 
        = \frac{b - \Ex{X}}{b-a}e^{sa} + \frac{\Ex{X} -
           a}{b-a}{e^{sb}}%
        \\%
        &=%
        \frac{b }{b-a}e^{sa} - \frac{  a}{b-a}{e^{sb}},
    \end{align*}
    since $\Ex{X} = 0$. 

    
    Next, set $\displaystyle p = - \frac{a}{b-a}$ and observe that
    $\displaystyle 1-p = 1 + \frac{a}{b-a} = \frac{b}{b-a}$ and
    \begin{align*}
    -ps(b-a) = - \pth{- \frac{ a}{b-a}} s (b-a) = sa .
    \end{align*}
    As such, we have
    \begin{align*}        
        \Ex{e^{sX}}%
        &\leq  (1-p)e^{sa} + p e^{sb} 
        =%
        (1-p+p e^{s(b-a)}) e^{sa} \\
        &=%
        (1-p+p e^{s(b-a)}) e^{-ps(b-a)} \\%
        &=%
        \Exp \pth{ -ps(b-a) + \ln\pth{ 1-p+p e^{s(b-a)} }} 
        = \Exp \pth{ -p u + \ln\pth{ 1-p+p e^{u} }},
    \end{align*}
    for $u = s(b-a)$. Setting
    \begin{align*} 
        \phi(u) = -pu + \ln\pth{1-p+pe^{u}},
    \end{align*}
    we thus have $\Ex{e^{sX}} \leq \Exp \pth{ \phi(u) }$.  To prove
    the claim, we will show that $\displaystyle \phi(u) \le {u^{2}} /
    {8} = {s^{2}(b-a)^{2}} / {8}$.
    
    To see that, expand $\phi(u)$ about zero using Taylor's
    expansion. We have 
    \begin{equation}
        \phi(u) = \phi(0)+u\phi'(0)+\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\phi''(\theta)
        \eqlab{phi:u}
    \end{equation}
    where $\theta\in [0,u]$, and notice
    that $\phi(0)=0$.
    Furthermore, we have
    \[
    \phi'(u) = -p + \frac{pe^u}{1-p + pe^u},
    \]
    and as such $\phi'(0) = -p + \frac{p}{1-p + p } = 0$.
    Now,
    \[
    \phi''(u) = \frac{\pth{ 1-p + pe^u}pe^u - \pth{pe^u}^2 }{\pth{1-p
          + pe^u}^2} = \frac{\pth{ 1-p}pe^u }{\pth{1-p + pe^u}^2}.
    \]
    For any $x, y \geq 0$, we have $(x+y)^2 \geq 4xy$ as this is
    equivalent to $(x-y)^2 \geq 0$. Setting $x = 1-p$ and $y = pe^u$,
    we have that
    \[
    \phi''(u) = 
    \frac{\pth{ 1-p}pe^u }{\pth{1-p + pe^u}^2}
    \leq 
    \frac{\pth{ 1-p}pe^u }{4\pth{ 1-p}pe^u} = \frac{1}{4}.
    \]
    Plugging this into \Eqref{phi:u}, we get that
    \[
    \phi(u) \leq \frac{1}{8}u^{2} = \frac{1}{8}\pth{s(b-a)}^{2} \;\;\;
    \text{ and } \;\;\; \Ex{e^{sX}} \leq \Exp \pth{ \phi(u) } \leq
    \Exp\pth{ \frac{1}{8}\pth{s(b-a)}^{2}},
    \]
    as claimed.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    Let $X$ be a random variable.
    If $\;\Ex{X}=0$ and $a\le X\le b$, then for any $s > 0$, we have
    \[ 
    \Prob{ X > t \MakesBig\ts }%
    \leq%
    \frac{\Exp \pth{\frac{s^{2}(b-a)^{2}}{8}}} {e^{st}}.
    \]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Using the same technique we used in proving Chernoff's inequality,
    we have that
    \begin{align*}
        \Prob{ X > t \MakesBig\ts } = \Prob{ e^{sX} > e^{st} }%
        \leq%
        \frac{\Ex{ e^{sX} }}{e^{st}} \leq \frac{\Exp
           \pth{\frac{s^{2}(b-a)^{2}}{8}}} {e^{st}}.
    \end{align*}
\end{proof}


\begin{fragment}{\deflabel{Hoeffding_inequality}}
\begin{theorem}[Hoeffding's inequality]
    Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent random variables, where $X_i
    \in \pbrcS{a_i, b_i}$, for $i=1,\ldots, n$.  Then, for the random
    variable $ S = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$ and any $\eta > 0$, we have
    \[
        \Prob{\ts \cardin{\Bigl. S - \Ex{\ts S\MakesBig } \ts } \geq
           \eta} \leq 2\Exp \pth{ - \frac{2\,\eta^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (b_i
              - a_i)^2} }.
    \]

    \index{inequality!Hoeffding}
    \index{Hoeffding's inequality}

    \thmlab{Hoeffding:inequality}
\end{theorem}
\end{fragment}

\begin{proof}
    Let $Z_{i}=X_{i}-\Ex{X_{i}}$, for $i=1,\ldots, n$.  Set 
    $Z = \sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}$, and observe that 
    \begin{align*}
        \Prob{ Z \geq \eta \MakesBig }  %
        =%
        \Prob{e^{sZ} \geq e^{s \eta}}
        \leq  \frac{\Ex{\Exp\pth{sZ}}}{\Exp(s\eta)},
    \end{align*}
    by Markov's inequality. Arguing as in the proof of Chernoff's
    inequality, we have
    \[
    \Ex{\MakeSBig\! \ts \Exp\pth{sZ}\ts } = \Ex[]{ \prod_{i=1}^{n}
       \Exp\pth{sZ_i}}%
    =%
    \prod_{i=1}^{n} \Ex{ \MakesBig\! \Exp\pth{sZ_i}} \leq
    \prod_{i=1}^{n} \Exp \pth{ \frac{s^{2}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}{8}},
    \]
    since the $Z_i$s are independent and by
    \lemref{exponent:blowup}. This implies that
    \begin{align*}
        \Prob{ Z \geq \eta \MakesBig } %
        \leq%
        \Exp(-s\eta) \prod_{i=1}^{n} e^{s^{2}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}/8} %
        =%
        \Exp\pth{ \frac{s^{2}}{8}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2} -
           s\eta }.
    \end{align*}
    The upper bound is minimized for $s = 4\eta/\pth{\sum_i
       (b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}$, implying
    \[ 
    \Prob{ Z \geq \eta \MakesBig }%
    \leq%
    \Exp \pth{-\frac{2\eta^{2}}{\sum (b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}}.
    \]
    The claim now follows by the symmetry of the upper bound (i.e.,
    apply the same proof to $-Z$).
\end{proof}

\Exercise{Chernoff inequality is tight.}%
{10}%
{Chernoff:tight}%
{%
   Let $S = \sum_{i=1}^{n} S_i$ be a sum of $n$ independent random
   variables each attaining values $+1$ and $-1$ with equal
   probability. Let $P(n,\Delta) = \Prob{S > \Delta}$.  Prove that for
   $\Delta \leq n/C$,
   \begin{align*}
       P(n, \Delta) \geq \frac{1}{C} \Exp \pth[]{-\frac{\Delta^2}{C
             n}},
   \end{align*}
   where $C$ is a suitable constant. That is, the well-known Chernoff
   bound $P(n, \Delta) \leq \Exp(-\Delta^2/2n))$ is close to the
   truth.%
}{%
   \Solution{%
      We need to bound the quantity
      \begin{align}
          \Prob{S > \Delta} =\frac{1}{2^n} \sum_{i=\Delta/2 + n/2}^n
          \binom{n}{i} = \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_{i=\Delta/2}^n
          \binom{n}{n/2 + i}.  \eqlab{sum}
      \end{align}
      Since $\binom{n}{m+1} =\frac{n!}{(n-m-1)! (m+1)!}  =
      \frac{n-m}{m+1} \binom{n}{m}$, we have that
      \begin{align*}
          \binom{n}{n/2 + i}%
          &=%
          \frac{n/2-i + 1}{n/2+i} \binom{n}{n/2+i-1} = \pth{1 +
             \frac{-2i+1}{n/2+i}}
          \binom{n}{n/2+i-1}\\
          &\geq%
          \pth{1 + \frac{-2i+1}{n/2}} \binom{n}{n/2+i-1}%
          \geq%
          \pth{1 - \frac{4i}{n}} \binom{n}{n/2+i-1}.
      \end{align*}
      Next, $1-x \geq e^{-Dx}$ for $0 \leq x < 1/E$, where $D$ is
      arbitrarily close to $1$, and $E$ is a function of $D$, we have
      that
      \begin{align*}
          \binom{n}{n/2 + i} \geq \Exp \pth{ - \frac{4iD}{n}}
          \binom{n}{n/2+i-1} \geq \binom{n}{n/2} \prod_{j=1}^i \pth{
             \Exp \pth{ - \frac{4jD}{n}}},
      \end{align*}
      by repeated application of the first part of this inequality.
      Thus,
      \begin{align*}
          \binom{n}{n/2 + i} \geq \binom{n}{n/2} \Exp \pth{ -
             \frac{4D}{n} \cdot \frac{i(i+1)}{2} } \geq
          \frac{2^n}{4\sqrt{n}} \Exp \pth{ - \frac{4D}{n} \cdot
             \frac{i(i+1)}{2} }
      \end{align*}
      In particular, the first term in \Eqref{sol} is
      \begin{align*}
          \binom{n}{n/2 + \Delta/2} \geq \frac{2^n}{4\sqrt{n}} \Exp
          \pth{ - \frac{4D}{n} \cdot \frac{\Delta(\Delta+2)}{8} }
      \end{align*}
      and
      \begin{align*}
          \binom{n}{n/2 + \Delta/2 + \sqrt{n} - 1} &\geq&
          \frac{2^n}{4\sqrt{n}} \Exp \pth{ - \frac{4D}{n} \cdot
             \frac{(\Delta+2\sqrt{n} - 2 )(\Delta+2\sqrt{n} )}{8} }\\
          &\geq%
          \frac{2^n}{4\sqrt{n}} \Exp \pth{ - \frac{4D}{n} \cdot
             \frac{(\Delta+2\sqrt{n} )^2}{8} }.
      \end{align*}
      
      If $\Delta \leq 100\sqrt{n}$ then
      \begin{align*}
          \binom{n}{n/2 + \Delta/2 + 4\sqrt{n} - 1} &\geq
          \frac{2^n}{4\sqrt{n}} \Exp \pth{ - \frac{4D}{n} \cdot
             \frac{(102\sqrt{n} )^2}{8} } \geq \frac{2^n}{4\sqrt{n}
             e^{6000}}.
      \end{align*}
      And then,
      \begin{align*}
          \Prob{S > \Delta}%
          &=%
          \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_{i=\Delta/2}^n \binom{n}{n/2 + i} \geq
          \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_{i=\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2+\sqrt{n} -1}
          \binom{n}{n/2 + i}%
          \\&%
          \geq%
          \frac{1}{2^n} \cdot \sqrt{n} \cdot \frac{2^n}{4\sqrt{n}
             e^{6000}} = \frac{1}{4e^{6000}},
      \end{align*}
      and the claim holds in this case (With the horrendous constant
      $C = 4e^{6000}$).
      
      If $\Delta \geq 100\sqrt{n}$ then,
      \begin{align*}
          \binom{n}{n/2 + \Delta/2 + 4\sqrt{n} - 1} &\geq%
          \frac{2^n}{4\sqrt{n}} \Exp \pth{ - \frac{4D}{n} \cdot
             \frac{(\Delta/2 + 4\sqrt{n}
                -1)(\Delta/2 + 4 \sqrt{n})}{2} }\\
          &\geq%
          \frac{2^n}{4\sqrt{n}} \Exp \pth{ - \frac{D}{2n} \cdot
             (\Delta + 8\sqrt{n}
             -2)(\Delta + 8 \sqrt{n}) }\\
          &\geq%
          \frac{2^n}{4\sqrt{n}} \Exp \pth{ -
             \frac{D}{2n} \cdot (1.1\Delta)^2 }\\
          &\geq%
          \frac{2^n}{4\sqrt{n}} \Exp \pth{ -
             \frac{\Delta^2}{(2/1.21D)n} }\\
      \end{align*}
      
      But then,
      \begin{align*}
          \Prob{S > \Delta}%
          &=%
          \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_{i=\Delta/2}^n \binom{n}{n/2 + i} \geq
          \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_{i=\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2+4\sqrt{n} -1}
          \binom{n}{n/2
             + i} \\
          &\geq%
          \frac{1}{2^n} \cdot 4 \sqrt{n} \cdot \frac{2^n}{4\sqrt{n}}
          \Exp \pth{ -
             \frac{\Delta^2}{(2/1.21D)n} }\\
          &\geq \frac{1}{2/1.21D} \Exp \pth{ -
             \frac{\Delta^2}{(2/1.21D)n} },
      \end{align*}
      as claimed, as long as we pick $D$ small enough, such that
      $2/(1.21D) > 1$.%
   }%
}

\Exercise{Chernoff inequality is tight by direct calculations.}%
{10}%
{Chernoff:tight:2}%
{%
   For this question use only basic argumentation -- do not use
   Stirling's formula, Chernoff inequality or any similar ``heavy''
   machinery.
   \begin{compactenumA}
        \item Prove that $\ds \sum_{i=0}^{n - k} \binom{2n}{i} \leq
        \frac{n}{4k^2} 2^{2n}$.
        
        Hint: Consider flipping a coin $2n$ times. Write down
        explicitly the probability of this coin to have at most $n-k$
        heads, and use Chebyshev inequality.

        \item Using (A), prove that $\Bigg. \binom{2n}{n} \geq
        2^{2n}/4 \sqrt{n}$ (which is a pretty good estimate).

        \item Prove that $\ds \binom{2n}{n + i + 1} = \pth{ 1-
           \frac{2i+1}{n+i+1}} \binom{2n}{n+i}$.

        \item Prove that $\ds \binom{2n}{n + i}\leq \Exp\pth{ \frac{-
              i(i-1)}{2n}} \binom{2n}{n}$.
        
        \item Prove that $\ds \binom{2n}{n + i} \geq \Exp\pth{ -\frac{
              8 i^2}{n}} \binom{2n}{n}$.

        \item Using the above, prove that $\ds \binom{2n}{n} \leq c
        \frac{2^{2n}}{\sqrt{n}}$ for some constant $c$ (I got $c
        =0.824...$ but any reasonable constant will do). 

        \item Using the above, prove that 
        \[
        \sum_{i=t\sqrt{n} + 1}^{(t+1)\sqrt{n}} \binom{2n}{n - i} \leq
        c 2^{2n} \Exp \pth{-t^2/ 2}.
        \]
        In particular, conclude that when flipping fair coin $2n$
        times, the probability to get less than $n - t\sqrt{n}$ heads
        (for $t$ an integer) is smaller than $c' \Exp \pth{-t^2/2}$,
        for some constant $c'$.

        \medskip

        \item Let $X$ be the number of heads in $2n$ coin flips.
        Prove that for any integer $t > 0$ and any $\delta >0$
        sufficiently small, it holds that
        $\Prob{ X < (1- \delta) n} \geq \Exp \pth{-c''\delta^2 n }$,
        where $c''$ is some constant. Namely, the Chernoff inequality
        is tight in the worst case.
    \end{compactenumA}
 }{}


\Exercise{Tail inequality for geometric variables}%
{10}%
{geometric:tail:inequality}{%
   Let $X_1, \ldots, X_m$ be $m$ independent random variables with
   geometric distribution with probability $p$ (i.e., $\Prob{X_i = j}
   = (1-p)^{j-1} p$). Let $Y = \sum_{i} X_i$, and let $\mu = \Ex{Y} =
   m/p$. Prove that
   \begin{math}
       \ds \Prob{\bigl. Y \geq (1+\delta) \mu \MakesBig }%
       \leq%
       \Exp \pth[]{ - \nfrac{m \delta^2}{8}}.
   \end{math}%
}{%
   % Solution
   Let $Z_1, \ldots, Z_\tau$ be a sequence of independent random
   variables, such that $Z_i=1$ with probability $p$ and $0$
   otherwise, where $\tau = (1+\delta) \mu$. Since a geometric
   variable with probability $p$ can be interpreted as the number of
   random variables $Y_i$ one has to read till encountering one. Let
   $\rho = \Ex{\sum_{1}^{\tau} Z_i} = (1+\delta)m$.  As such,
   \begin{align*}
     \Prob{Y \geq (1+\delta) \mu }%
     &\leq %
       \Prob{\sum_{1}^{\tau} Z_i \leq m}%
       =%
       \Prob{\sum_{1}^{\tau} Z_i \leq (1-\delta/2)\rho }%
       \leq%
       \Exp\pth{ - \rho (\delta/2)^2/ 2 }\\
     &\leq%
       \Exp\pth{ - (1+\delta)m \delta^2/ 8 }%
       \leq%
       \Exp\pth{ -m \delta^2/ 8 }.
   \end{align*}
}
