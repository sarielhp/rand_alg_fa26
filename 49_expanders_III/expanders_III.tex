\section{Building a large expander with constant degree}

\subsection{Notations}

For a vertex $v \in V(\Graph)$, we will denote by
$\NXExt{v}{\Graph}{i} = \NX{v}{i}$ the $i$\th neighbor of $v$ in the
graph $\Graph$ (we order the neighbors of a vertex in an arbitrary
order).

The regular graphs we next discuss have \emphi{consistent
   labeling}. That is, for a regular graph $\Graph$ (we assume here
that $\Graph$ is regular). This means that if $u$ is the $i$\th
neighbor $v$ then $v$ is the $i$\th neighbor of $u$. Formally, this
means that $\NX{\NX{v}{i}}{i} = v$, for all $v$ and $i$.  This is a
non-trivial property, but its easy to verify that the low quality
expander of \thmref{explicit} has this property. It is also easy to
verify that the complete graph can be easily be made into having
consistent labeling (exercise). These two graphs would be sufficient
for our construction.





\subsection{The Zig-Zag product}

At this point, we know how to construct a good ``small'' expander. The
question is how to build a large expander (i.e., large number of
vertices) and with constant degree.

The intuition of the construction is the following: It is easy to
improve the expansion qualities of a graph by squaring it. The problem
is that the resulting graph $\Graph$ has a degree which is too
large. To overcome this, we will replace every vertex in $\Graph$ by a
copy of a small graph that is connected and has low degree. For
example, we could replace every vertex of degree $d$ in $\Graph$ by a
path having $d$ vertices. Every such vertex is now in charge of
original edge of the graph. Naturally, such a replacement operation
reduces the quality of the expansion of the resulting graph. In this
case, replacing a vertex with a path is a potential ``disaster'',
since every such subpath increases the lengths of the paths of the
original graph by a factor of $d$ (and intuitively, a good expander
have ``short'' paths between any pair of vertices).

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \IncludeGraphics{\File{figs/replacement}}
    \caption{An example of a replacement product.}
    \figlab{replacement}
\end{figure}


Consider a ``large'' $(n,D)$-graph $\Graph$ and a ``small''
$(D,d)$-graph $\GraphB$. As a first stage, we replace every vertex of
$\Graph$ by a copy of $\GraphB$.  The new graph $\GraphC$ has
$\RangeI{n}\times\RangeI{D}$ as a vertex set.  Here, the edge $v u \in
V(\Graph)$, where $u=v[i]$ and $v=u[j]$, is replaced by the edge
connecting $(v,i) \in V(\GraphC)$ with $(u,j) \in V(\GraphC)$. We will
refer to this resulting edge $(v,i)(u,j)$ as a \emphi{long}
edge. Also, we copy all the edges of the small graph to each one of
its copies. That is, for each $i\in \RangeI{n}$, and $uv \in
E(\GraphB)$, we add the edge $(i,u) (i,v)$ to $\GraphC$, which is a
\emphi{short} edge. We will refer to $\GraphC$, which is a $(nD,
d+1)$-graph, as a \emphi{replacement product} of $\Graph$ and
$\GraphB$, denoted by $\RProd{\Graph}{\GraphB}$. See \figref{replacement}
for an example.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \IncludeGraphics{\File{figs/zigzag_path}}
    \caption{A zig-zag-zig path in the replacement product graph.}
    \figlab{zigzag:path}
\end{figure}

Again, intuitively, we are losing because the expansion of the
resulting graph had deteriorated too much. To overcome this problem,
we will perform local shortcuts to shorten the  paths in the
resulting graph (and thus improve its expansion properties). A
\emphi{zig-zag-zig path} in the replacement product graph $\GraphC$,
is a three edge path $e_1 e_2 e_3$, where $e_1$ and $e_3$ are short
edges, and the middle edge $e_2$ is a long edge.  That is, if $e_1 =
(i,u) (i,v)$, $e_2 = (i,v)(j,v')$, and $e_3=(j,v')(j,u')$, then $e_1,
e_2, e_3 \in E(\GraphC)$, $ij \in E(\Graph)$, $uv \in E(\GraphB)$ and
$v'u' \in E(\GraphB)$. Intuitively, you can think about $e_1$ as a
small ``zig'' step in $\GraphB$, $e_2$ is a long ``zag'' step in
$\Graph$, and finally $e_3$ is a ``zig'' step in $\GraphB$ (see \figref{zigzag:path}).


Another way of representing a zig-zag-zig path $v_1 v_2 v_3 v_4$
starting at the vertex $v_1 = (i,v) \in V(\GraphD)$, is to
parameterize it by two integers $\ell, \ell'\in\RangeI{d}$, where
\[
v_1 = (i,v), \;\;\;\; v_2 = \pth[]{i,\NXExt{v}{\GraphB}{\ell}}
\;\;\;\; 
v_3 =
\pth{ \NXExt{i}{\Graph}{\MakeSBig\NXExt{v}{\GraphB}{\ell}},
\NXExt{v}{\GraphB}{\ell}}
%\NX{v}{\ell}}
\;\;\;\; 
v_4 =
\pth{ \NXExt{i}{\Graph}{\MakeSBig\NXExt{v}{\GraphB}{\ell}} \;, \;\;
\NXExt{\pth{\NXExt{v}{\GraphB}{\ell}\MakeSBig}}{\GraphB}{\ell'}}.
%
%v_3 = \pth{\NX{i}{\NX{v}{\ell}},\NX{\NX{v}{\ell}}{\ell'}}.
\]

Let $Z$ be the set of all (unordered) pairs of vertices of $\GraphC$
connected by such a zig-zag-zig path. Note, that every vertex $(i,v)$
of $\GraphC$ has $d^2$ paths having $(i,v)$ as an end point.  Consider
the graph $\GraphD = (V(\GraphC), Z)$. The graph $\GraphD$ has $nD$
vertices, and it is $d^2$ regular. Furthermore, since we shortcut all
these zig-zag-zig paths in $\GraphC$, the graph $\GraphD$ is a much
better expander (intuitively) than $\GraphC$. We will refer to the
graph $\GraphD$ as the \emphi{zig-zag} product of $\Graph$ and
$\GraphB$.


\begin{defn}
    The \emphi{zig-zag product} of $(n,D)$-graph $\Graph$ and a
    $(D,d)$-graph $\GraphB$, is the $(nD, d^2)$ graph $\GraphD =
    \ZProd{\Graph}{\GraphB}$, where the set of vertices is
    $\RangeI{n}\times\RangeI{D}$ and for any $v \in \RangeI{n}$, $i
    \in \RangeI{D}$, and $\ell, \ell' \in \RangeI{d}$ we have in
    $\GraphD$ the edge connecting the vertex $(i,v)$ with the vertex
    $\pth[]{ \NXExt{i}{\Graph}{\MakeSBig\NXExt{v}{\GraphB}{\ell}} \,,
       \,\,
       \NXExt{\pth{\NXExt{v}{\GraphB}{\ell}\MakeSBig}}{\GraphB}{\ell'}}$.
\end{defn}

\begin{remark}
    We need the resulting zig-zag graph to have consistent
    labeling. For the sake of simplicity of exposition, we are just
    going to assume this property.
\end{remark}


We next bound the tension of the zig-zag product graph.

\begin{theorem}
    We have $\DialX{\ZProd{\Graph}{\GraphB}} \leq \BiDialX{\Graph}
    \pth[]{\BiDialX{\GraphB}}^2$.  and
    $\BiDialX{\ZProd{\Graph}{\GraphB}} \leq \BiDialX{\Graph}
    \pth[]{\BiDialX{\GraphB}}^2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\Graph = \pth{\RangeI{n}, E}$ be a $(n,D)$-graph and $\GraphB
    = \pth{\RangeI{D}, E'}$ be a $(D,d)$-graph. Fix any function $f:
    \RangeI{n}\times \RangeI{D} \rightarrow \Re$, and observe that
    \begin{align*}
        \psi &= \ExExt{\substack{u,v \in \RangeI{n} \\
              k,\ell \in \RangeI{D}}\,}{\cardin{f(u,k) - f(v,\ell)}^2}
        = \ExExt{ k,\ell \in \RangeI{D} \,}{\ExExt{u,v \in \RangeI{n}}
           {\cardin{f(u,k) - f(v,\ell)}^2}}\\
        &\leq \ExExt{ k,\ell \in \RangeI{D} \,}{ \BiDialX{\Graph}
           \ExExt{uv \in E(\Graph)} {\cardin{f(u,k) - f(v,\ell)}^2}} %
        % 
        = \BiDialX{\Graph} \underbrace{\ExExt{ k,\ell \in \RangeI{D} \,}{
              \ExExtS{u \in \RangeI{n}\\
                    p \in \RangeI{D}}
                 {\cardin{f(u,k) - f\pth{ \NX{u}{p},\ell}}^2}}}_{=\Delta_1}.
       \end{align*}
       Now,
    \begin{align*}
        %
        \Delta_1 &= \ExExtS{u \in \RangeI{n}\\
              \ell \in \RangeI{D} \,}{
           \ExExt{k, p \in \RangeI{D}}
           {\cardin{f(u,k) - f\pth{ \NX{u}{p},\ell}}^2}}
        \;\; \leq \;\;  \ExExtS{u \in \RangeI{n}\\
              \ell \in \RangeI{D} \,}{
           \BiDialX{\GraphB}
           \ExExt{k p \in E(\GraphB)}
           {\cardin{f(u,k) - f\pth{ \NX{u}{p},\ell}}^2}}\\
        %
        &=  \BiDialX{\GraphB}
        \underbrace{\ExExtS{u \in \RangeI{n}\\
              \ell \in \RangeI{D} \,}{
              \ExExtS{p \in \RangeI{D}\\
                 j \in \RangeI{d}}
              {\cardin{f\pth{u,\NX{p}{j}} - f\pth{
                       \NX{u}{p},\ell}}^2}}}_{=\Delta_2}.
    \end{align*}
    Now,
    \begin{align*}
        \Delta_2
        &= 
        \ExExtS{   j \in \RangeI{d}\\
           \ell \in \RangeI{D} \,}{
           \ExExtS{u \in \RangeI{n}\\
              p \in \RangeI{D}}
           {\cardin{f\pth{u,\NX{p}{j}} - f\pth{ \NX{u}{p},\ell}}^2}}
        =
        {\ExExtS{j \in \RangeI{d}\\
              \ell \in \RangeI{D} \,}{
              \ExExtS{v \in \RangeI{n}\\
                 p \in \RangeI{D} }
              {\cardin{f\pth{\NX{v}{p},\NX{p}{j}} - f\pth{
                       v,\ell}}^2}}}
        \\
        &=  \ExExtS{
           j \in \RangeI{d}\\
           v \in \RangeI{n} \,}{%
           \ExExtS{
              p \in \RangeI{D}\\
              \ell \in \RangeI{D} }
           {\cardin{f\pth{\NX{v}{p},\NX{p}{j}} - f\pth{ v,\ell}}^2}}\\
        &= \BiDialX{\GraphB}\underbrace{ \ExExtS{
           j \in \RangeI{d}\\
           v \in \RangeI{n} \,}{ \ExExtS{ p \ell \in E(\GraphB) }
        {\cardin{f\pth{\NX{v}{p},\NX{p}{j}} - f\pth{ v,\ell}}^2}}}_{=\Delta_3}.
    \end{align*}
    Now, we have
    \begin{align*}
        \Delta_3 &= \ExExtS{
           j \in \RangeI{d}\\
           v \in \RangeI{n} \,}{ \ExExtS{
              p \in \RangeI{D}\\
              i \in \RangeI{d} }
           {\cardin{f\pth{\NX{v}{p},\NX{p}{j}} - f\pth{ v,\NX{p}{i}}}^2}}
        = \ExExtS{
           \pth[]{u,k}\pth[]{\ell,v} \in E\pth[]{ \ZProd{\Graph}{\GraphB}}}
           {\cardin{f\pth{u, k} - f\pth{ \ell, v} \MakeBig}},
    \end{align*}
    as $\pth[]{\NX{v}{p},\NX{p}{j}}$ is adjacent to $\pth[]{\NX{v}{p},
       p}$ (a short edge), which is in turn adjacent to $\pth[]{v, p}$
    (a long edge), which is adjacent to $\pth[]{ v,\NX{p}{i}}$ (a
    short edge). Namely, $\pth[]{\NX{v}{p},\NX{p}{j}}$ and $\pth[]{
       v,\NX{p}{i}}$ form the endpoints of a zig-zag path in the
    replacement product of $\Graph$ and $\GraphB$. That is, these two
    endpoints are connected by an edge in the zig-zag product graph.
    Furthermore, it is easy to verify that each zig-zag edge get
    accounted for in this representation exactly once, implying the
    above inequality. Thus, we have $\psi \leq \BiDialX{\Graph}
    \pth{\BiDialX{\GraphB}}^2 \Delta_3$, which implies the claim.

    The second claim follows by similar argumentation.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Squaring}

The last component in our construction, is
\emphi{squaring}{squaring!graph} a graph. Given a $(n,d)$-graph
$\Graph$, consider the multigraph $\Graph^2$ formed by connecting any vertices
connected in $\Graph$ by a path of length $2$. Clearly, if $\Mtrx$ is
the adjacency matrix of $\Graph$, then the adjacency matrix of
$\Graph^2$ is the matrix $\Mtrx^2$. Note, that $\pth{\Mtrx^2}_{ij}$ is
the number of distinct paths of length $2$ in $\Graph$ from $i$ to
$j$. Note, that the new graph might have self loops, which does not
effect our analysis, so we keep them in. 

\begin{lemma}
    Let $\Graph$ be a $(n,d)$-graph.  The graph $\Graph^2$ is a
    $(n,d^2)$-graph. Furthermore $\BiDialX{\Graph^2} =
    \frac{\pth{\BiDialX{\Graph}}^2}{2\BiDialX{\Graph} - 1}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    The graph $\Graph^2$ has eigenvalues $\pth{\TEV{1}(\Graph)}^2,
    \ldots, \pth{\TEV{1}(\Graph)}^2$ for its matrix $\TMtrx^2$. As
    such, we have that
    \[
    \EVal\pth{\Graph^2} = \max\pth{\TEV{2}\pth{\Graph^2},
       -\TEV{n}\pth{\Graph^2}}.
    \]
    Now, $\TEV{1}\pth{\Graph^2} = 1$. Now, if $\TEV{2}(\Graph) \geq
    \cardin{\TEV{n}(\Graph)} < 1$ then $\EVal\pth{\Graph^2} =
    \TEV{2}\pth{\Graph^2} = \pth{\TEV{2}\pth{\Graph}}^2 =
    \pth{\EVal\pth{\Graph}}^2$.

    If $\TEV{2}(\Graph) < \cardin{\TEV{n}(\Graph)}$ then
    $\EVal\pth{\Graph^2} = \TEV{2}\pth{\Graph^2} = 
    \pth{\TEV{n}\pth{\Graph}}^2 = \pth{\EVal\pth{\Graph}}^2$..
    
    Thus, in either case $\EVal\pth{\Graph^2} =
    \pth{\EVal\pth{\Graph}}^2$. Now, By \lemref{b:i:expansion}
    $\BiDialX{\Graph} = \frac{1}{1-\EVal\pth{\Graph}}$, which implies
    that $\EVal(\Graph) = 1- 1/\BiDialX{\Graph}$, and thus
    \begin{align*}
        \BiDialX{\Graph^2}%
        = %
        \frac{1}{1-\EVal\pth{\Graph^2}}%
        =%
        \frac{1}{1-\pth{\EVal\pth{\Graph}}^2}%
        =%
        \frac{1}{1-\pth{1- \frac{1}{\BiDialX{\Graph}}}^2}%
        =%
        \frac{\BiDialX{\Graph}}{2- \frac{1}{\BiDialX{\Graph}}}%
        =%
        \frac{\pth{\BiDialX{\Graph}}^2}{2\BiDialX{\Graph} - 1}.
    \end{align*}
\end{proof}


\subsection{The construction}

So, let build an expander using \thmref{explicit}, with parameters
$r=7$ $q=2^4=32$. Let $d= q^2 = 256$.  The resulting graph $\GraphB$
has $N=q^{r+1} = d^4$ vertices, and it is $d=q^2$
regular. Furthermore, $\TEV{i}\leq r/q= 7/32$, for all $i\geq 2$. As
such, we have
\[
\DialX{\GraphB}=
\BiDialX{\GraphB} = \frac{1}{1-7/32} = \frac{32}{25}.
\]
Let $\Graph_0$ be any graph that its square is the complete graph over
$n_0 = N+1$ vertices. Observe that $\Graph_0^2$ is $d^4$-regular. Set
$\Graph_i = \pth{\ZProd{\Graph_{i-1}^2}{\GraphB}}$, Clearly, the graph
$\Graph_i$ has
\[
n_i = n_{i-1} N
\]
vertices. The graph $\ZProd{\Graph_{i-1}^2}{\GraphB}$ is $d^2$
regular. As far as the bi-tension, let $\alpha_i =
\BiDialX{\Graph_i}$. We have that
\[
\alpha_i = 
\frac{\alpha_{i-1}^2}{2\alpha_{i-1} -1 }
\pth{\BiDialX{\GraphB}}^2
=
\frac{\alpha_{i-1}^2}{2\alpha_{i-1} -1 }
\pth{\frac{32}{25}}^2 \leq 
1.64 \frac{\alpha_{i-1}^2}{2\alpha_{i-1} -1 }.
\]

It is now easy to verify, that $\alpha_i$ can not be bigger than
$5$.

\begin{theorem}
    For any $i \geq 0$, one can compute deterministically a graph
    $G_i$ with $n_i = (d^4+1)d^{4i}$ vertices, which is $d^2$ regular,
    where $d=256$. The graph $G_i$ is a $(1/10)$-expander.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    The construction is described above. As for the expansion, since
    the bi-tension bounds the tension of a graph, we have that
    $\DialX{\Graph_i} \leq \BiDialX{\Graph_i} \leq 5$. Now, by
    \lemref{tension:to:expansion}, we have that $\Graph_i$ is a
    $\delta$-expander, where $\delta \geq 1/(2\DialX{\Graph_i}) \geq
    1/10$.
\end{proof}





\section{Bibliographical notes}



A good survey on expanders is the monograph by Hoory \etal
\cite{hlw-egta-06}. The small expander construction is from the paper
by Alon \etal \cite{ass-eccde-08} (but its originally from the work by
Along and Roichman \cite{ar-rcge-94}). The work by Alon \etal
\cite{ass-eccde-08} contains a construction of an expander that is
constant degree, which is of similar complexity to the one we
presented here. Instead, we used the zig-zag expander construction
from the influential work of Reingold \etal \cite{rvw-ewzzg-02}. Our
analysis however, is from an upcoming paper by Mendel and Naor
\cite{mn-tcnls-08}. This analysis is arguably reasonably simple (as
simplicity is in the eye of the beholder, we will avoid claim that its
the simplest), and (even better) provide a good intuition and a
systematic approach to analyzing the expansion.

We took a creative freedom in naming notations, and the name tension
and bi-tension are the author's own invention.


\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[\textsc{Expanders made easy.}]
    By considering a random bipartite three-regular graph on $2n$
    vertices obtained by picking three random permutations between the
    two sides of the bipartite graph, prove that there is a $c>0$ such
    that for every $n$ there exits a $(2n,3,c)$-expander. (What is the
    value of $c$ in your construction?)
\end{exercise}


\begin{exercise}[\textsc{Is your consistency in vain?}]
    In the construction, we assumed that the graphs we are dealing
    with when building expanders have consistent labeling.  This can
    be enforced by working with bipartite graphs, which implies
    modifying the construction slightly.
    \begin{compactenumA}
        \item Prove that a $d$-regular bipartite graph always has a
        consistent labeling (hint: consider matchings in this graph).

        \item Prove that if $\Graph$ is bipartite so is the graph
        $\Graph^3$ (the cubed graph).

        \item Let $\Graph$ be a $(n,D)$-graph and let $\GraphB$ be a
        $(D,d)$-graph. Prove that if $\Graph$ is bipartite then
        $\Graph \ZProd{\Graph}{\GraphB}$ is bipartite.

        \item Describe in detail a construction of an expander that
        is: (i) bipartite, and (ii) has consistent labeling at every
        stage of the construction (prove this property if necessary).
        For the $i$\th graph in your series, what is its vertex
        degree, how many vertices it has, and what is the quality of
        expansion it provides?
    \end{compactenumA}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[\textsc{Tension and bi-tension}.]
    \points{30}

    Disprove (i.e., give a counter example) that there exists a
    universal constant $c$, such that for any connected graph
    $\Graph$, we have that $\DialX{\Graph} \leq \BiDialX{\Graph} \leq
    c \DialX{\Graph}$.
\end{exercise}
