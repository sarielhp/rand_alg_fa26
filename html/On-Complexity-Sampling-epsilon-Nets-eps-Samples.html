In this chapter we will try to quantify the notion of geometric complexity. It is intuitively clear that a ● (i.e., disk) is a simpler shape than an ● (i.e., ellipse), which is in turn simpler than a blacksmiley (i.e., smiley). This becomes even more important when we consider several such shapes and how they interact with each other. As these examples might demonstrate, this notion of complexity is somewhat elusive.

To this end, we show that one can capture the structure of a distribution/point set by a small subset. The size here would depend on the complexity of the shapes/ranges we care about, but surprisingly it would be independent of the size of the point set.

40.1  dimension

  • Definition 40.1.1. A range space \(\RangeSpace \) is a pair \((\GroundSet ,\RangeSet )\), where \(\GroundSet \) is a ground set (finite or infinite) and \(\RangeSet \) is a (finite or infinite) family of subsets of \(\GroundSet \). The elements of \(\GroundSet \) are points and the elements of \(\RangeSet \) are ranges.

Our interest is in the size/weight of the ranges in the range space. For technical reasons, it will be easier to consider a finite subset \(\FGroundSet \) as the underlining ground set.

  • Definition 40.1.2. Let \(\RangeSpace = (\GroundSet ,\RangeSet )\) be a range space, and let \(\FGroundSet \) be a finite (fixed) subset of \(\GroundSet \). For a range \(\range \in \RangeSet \), its measure is the quantity

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} \Measure {\range }=\frac {\cardin {\range \cap \FGroundSet }}{\cardin {\FGroundSet }}. \end{equation*}

While \(\FGroundSet \) is finite, it might be very large. As such, we are interested in getting a good estimate to \(\Measure {\range }\) by using a more compact set to represent the range space.

  • Definition 40.1.3. Let \(\RangeSpace = (\GroundSet ,\RangeSet )\) be a range space. For a subset \(\SetC \) (which might be a multi-set) of \(\FGroundSet \), its estimate of the measure of \(\Measure {\range }\), for \(\range \in \RangeSet \), is the quantity

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{0}\)

    \begin{align*} \sMeasure {\range }=\frac {\cardin {\range \cap \SetC }}{\cardin {\SetC }}. \end{align*}

The main purpose of this chapter is to come up with methods to generate a sample \(\SetC \), such that \(\Measure {\range } \approx \sMeasure {\range }\), for all the ranges \(\range \in \RangeSet \).

It is easy to see that in the worst case, no sample can capture the measure of all ranges. Indeed, given a sample \(\SetC \), consider the range \(\FGroundSet \setminus \SetC \) that is being completely missed by \(\SetC \). As such, we need to concentrate on range spaces that are “low dimensional”, where not all subsets are allowable ranges. The notion of dimension (named after Vapnik and Chervonenkis [vc-ucrfe-71]) is one way to limit the complexity of a range space.

  • Definition 40.1.4. Let \(\RangeSpace = (\GroundSet ,\RangeSet )\) be a range space. For \(Y \subseteq \GroundSet \), let

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation} \VCProj {\RangeSet }{Y}=\Set {\range \cap Y}{ \range \in \RangeSet \bigr .\,} \eqlab {v:c:project} \end{equation}

    denote the projection of \(\RangeSet \) on \(Y\). The range space \(\RangeSpace \) projected to \(Y\) is \(\VCProj {\RangeSpace }{Y} = \pth {Y, \VCProj {\RangeSet }{Y}}\).

    If \(\VCProj {\RangeSet }{Y}\) contains all subsets of \(Y\) (i.e., if \(Y\) is finite, we have \(\cardin {\VCProj {\RangeSet }{Y}} = 2^{\cardin {Y}}\)), then \(Y\) is shattered by \(\RangeSet \) (or equivalently \(Y\) is shattered by \(\RangeSpace \)).

    The Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension (or  dimension) of \(\RangeSpace \), denoted by \(\VCDim (\RangeSpace )\), is the maximum cardinality of a shattered subset of \(\GroundSet \). If there are arbitrarily large shattered subsets, then \(\VCDim (S) = \infty \).

40.1.1 Examples

Intervals. Consider the set \(\GroundSet \) to be the real line, and consider \(\RangeSet \) to be the set of all intervals on the real line. Consider the set \(Y = \brc {1,2}\). Clearly, one can find four intervals that contain all possible subsets of \(Y\). Formally, the projection \(\VCProj {\RangeSet }{Y} = \brc { \MakeSBig \! \brc {\,}, \brc { 1 }, \brc {2}, \brc {1,2} }\). The intervals realizing each of these subsets are depicted in Figure 40.1 (a).

However, this is false for a set of three points \(B = \brc {\pnt ,\pntA ,\pntB }\) (see Figure 40.1 (b)), since there is no interval that can contain the two extreme points \(\pnt \) and \(\pntB \) without also containing \(\pntA \). Namely, the subset \(\brc {\pnt ,\pntB }\) is not realizable for intervals, implying that the largest shattered set by the range space \(\pth []{\text {real line}, \text {intervals} }\) is of size two. We conclude that the dimension of this space is two.

Disks. Let \(\GroundSet = \Re ^2\), and let \(\RangeSet \) be the set of disks in the plane. Clearly, for any three points in the plane (in general position), denoted by \(\pnt , \pntA \), and \(\pntB \), one can find eight disks that realize all possible \(2^3\) different subsets. See the figure on the right.

But can disks shatter a set with four points? Consider such a set \(\PntSet \) of four points. If the convex hull of \(\PntSet \) has only three points on its boundary, then the subset \(X\) having only those three vertices (i.e., it does not include the middle point) is impossible, by convexity. Namely, there is no disk that contains only the points of \(X\) without the middle point.

   
   

Alternatively, if all four points are vertices of the convex hull and they are \(a,b,c,d\) along the boundary of the convex hull, either the set \(\brc {a, c}\) or the set \(\brc {b, d}\) is not realizable. Indeed, if both options are realizable, then consider the two disks \(\disk _1\) and \(\disk _2\) that realize those assignments. Clearly, \(\bd D_1\) and \(\bd D_2\) must intersect in four points, but this is not possible, since two circles have at most two intersection points. See the figure on the left. Hence the dimension of this range space is \(3\).

   

Convex sets. Consider the range space \(\RangeSpace = (\Re ^2, \RangeSet )\), where \(\RangeSet \) is the set of all (closed) convex sets in the plane. We claim that \(\VCDim (\RangeSpace )=\infty \). Indeed, consider a set \(U\) of \(n\) points \(p_1,\ldots , p_n\) all lying on the boundary of the unit circle in the plane. Let \(V\) be any subset of \(U\), and consider the convex hull \(\CHX {V}\). Clearly, \(\CHX {V} \in \RangeSet \), and furthermore, \(\CHX {V} \cap U = V\). Namely, any subset of \(U\) is realizable by \(\RangeSpace \). Thus, \(\RangeSpace \) can shatter sets of arbitrary size, and its dimension is unbounded.

Complement. Consider the range space \(\RangeSpace = (\GroundSet , \RangeSet )\) with \(\Dim =\VCDim (\RangeSpace )\). Next, consider the complement space, \(\overline {\RangeSpace } = \bigl (\ts \GroundSet , \overline {\RangeSet }\, \bigr )\), where

\(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{1}\)

\begin{equation*} \overline {\RangeSet }=\Set {\GroundSet \setminus \range }{ \range \in \RangeSet \bigr . }. \end{equation*}

Namely, the ranges of \(\overline {\RangeSpace }\) are the complement of the ranges in \(\RangeSpace \). What is the dimension of \(\overline {\RangeSpace }\)? Well, a set \(B \subseteq \GroundSet \) is shattered by \(\overline {\RangeSpace }\) if and only if it is shattered by \(\RangeSpace \). Indeed, if \(\RangeSpace \) shatters \(B\), then for any \(Z \subseteq B\), we have that \((B \setminus Z) \in \VCProj {\RangeSet }{B}\), which implies that \(Z = {B \setminus \MakeSBig (B \setminus Z)} \in \VCProj {\overline {\RangeSet }}{B}\). Namely, \(\VCProj {\overline {\RangeSet }}{B}\) contains all the subsets of \(B\), and \(\overline {\RangeSpace }\) shatters \(B\). Thus, \(\VCDim \bigl ( \ts \overline {\RangeSpace } \ts \bigr ) = \VCDim ( \RangeSpace )\).

  • Lemma 40.1.5. For a range space \(\RangeSpace = (\GroundSet , \RangeSet )\) we have that \(\VCDim (\RangeSpace ) = \VCDim \pth {\overline {\RangeSpace }}\,\), where \(\overline {\RangeSpace }\) is the complement range space.

40.1.1.1 Halfspaces

Let \(\RangeSpace = (\GroundSet ,\RangeSet )\), where \(\GroundSet =\Re ^\hdim \) and \(\RangeSet \) is the set of all (closed) halfspaces in \(\Re ^\hdim \). We need the following technical claim.

  • Claim 40.1.6. Let \(\PntSet = \brc {\pnt _1, \ldots , \pnt _{\hdim +2}}\) be a set of \(\hdim +2\) points in \(\Re ^\hdim \). There are real numbers \(\;\beta _1, \ldots , \beta _{\hdim +2}\), not all of them zero, such that \(\sum _i \beta _i \pnt _i = 0\) and \(\sum _i \beta _i = 0\).

  • Proof: Indeed, set \(\pntA _i = \pth []{\pnt _i, 1}\), for \(i=1,\ldots , d+2\). Now, the points \(\pntA _1, \ldots , \pntA _{\hdim +2} \in \Re ^{\hdim +1}\) are linearly dependent, and there are coefficients \(\beta _1, \ldots , \beta _{\hdim +2}\), not all of them zero, such that \(\sum _{i=1}^{\hdim +2} \beta _i \pntA _i = 0\). Considering only the first \(\hdim \) coordinates of these points implies that \(\sum _{i=1}^{\hdim +2} \beta _i \pnt _i = 0\). Similarly, by considering only the \((d+1)\)st coordinate of these points, we have that \(\sum _{i=1}^{\hdim +2} \beta _i = 0\).

To see what the dimension of halfspaces in \(\Re ^\hdim \) is, we need the following result of Radon. (For a reminder of the formal definition of convex hulls, see Definition 40.5.1.)

  • Theorem 40.1.7 (Radon’s theorem). Let \(\PntSet = \brc {\pnt _1, \ldots , \pnt _{\hdim +2}}\) be a set of \(\hdim +2\) points in \(\Re ^\hdim \). Then, there exist two disjoint subsets \(C\) and \(D\) of \(\PntSet \), such that \(\CHX {C} \cap \CHX {D} \ne \emptyset \) and \(C \cup D = \PntSet \).

  • Proof: By Claim 40.1.6 there are real numbers \(\beta _1, \ldots , \beta _{\hdim +2}\), not all of them zero, such that \(\sum _i \beta _i \pnt _i = 0\) and \(\sum _i \beta _i = 0\).

    Assume, for the sake of simplicity of exposition, that \(\beta _1, \ldots , \beta _k \geq 0\) and \(\beta _{k+1}, \ldots \), \(\beta _{\hdim +2} < 0\). Furthermore, let \(\mu = \sum _{i=1}^{k} \beta _i = -\sum _{i=k+1}^{\hdim +2} \beta _i\). We have that

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{1}\)

    \begin{equation*} \sum _{i=1}^{k} \beta _i \pnt _i = - \sum _{i=k+1}^{\hdim +2} \beta _i \pnt _i. \end{equation*}

    In particular, \(v = \sum _{i=1}^{k} (\beta _i/\mu ) \pnt _i\) is a point in \(\CHX {\brc {\pnt _1,\ldots , \pnt _k}}\). Furthermore, for the same point \(v\) we have \(v = \sum _{i=k+1}^{\hdim +2} - \ts (\beta _i/\mu ) \pnt _i\) \(\in \CHX {\brc {\pnt _{k+1}, \ldots , \pnt _{\hdim +2}}}\). We conclude that \(v\) is in the intersection of the two convex hulls, as required.

The following is a trivial observation, and yet we provide a proof to demonstrate it is true.

  • Lemma 40.1.8. Let \(\PntSet \subseteq \Re ^\hdim \) be a finite set, let \(\pntB \) be any point in \(\CHX {\PntSet }\), and let \(h^+\) be a halfspace of \(\Re ^\hdim \) containing \(\pntB \). Then there exists a point of \(\PntSet \) contained inside \(h^+\).

  • Proof: The halfspace \(h^+\) can be written as \(h^+ = \Set { \pntC \in \Re ^\hdim }{\! \DotProd {\pntC }{v} \leq c}\!.\)Now \(\pntB \in \CHX {\PntSet } \cap h^+\), and as such there are numbers \(\alpha _1, \ldots , \alpha _m \geq 0\) and points \(\pnt _1,\ldots , \pnt _m \in \PntSet \), such that \(\sum _i \alpha _i = 1\) and \(\sum _i \alpha _i \pnt _i =\pntB \). By the linearity of the dot product, we have that

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{1}\)

    \begin{equation*} \pntB \in h^+ \implies \DotProd {\pntB }{v} \leq c\implies \DotProd {\Bigl .\smash {\sum _{i=1}^m \alpha _i \pnt _i }}{v} \leq c\implies \beta = \sum _{i=1}^m \alpha _i\DotProd { \pnt _i }{v} \leq c. \end{equation*}

    Setting \(\,\beta _i = \DotProd { \pnt _i }{v}\), for \(i=1,\ldots , m\), the above implies that \(\,\beta \) is a weighted average of \(\,\beta _1, \ldots , \beta _m\). In particular, there must be a \(\,\beta _i\) that is no larger than the average. That is \(\,\beta _i \leq c\). This implies that \(\DotProd { \pnt _i }{v} \leq c\). Namely, \(\pnt _i \in h^+\) as claimed.

Let \(\RangeSpace \) be the range space having \(\Re ^\hdim \) as the ground set and all the close halfspaces as ranges. Radon’s theorem implies that if a set \(\PntSetB \) of \(\hdim + 2\) points is being shattered by \(\RangeSpace \), then we can partition this set \(\PntSetB \) into two disjoint sets \(Y\) and \(Z\) such that \(\CHX {Y} \cap \CHX {Z} \ne \emptyset \). In particular, let \(\pntB \) be a point in \(\CHX {Y} \cap \CHX {Z}\). If a halfspace \(h^+\) contains all the points of \(Y\), then \(\CHX {Y} \subseteq h^+\), since a halfspace is a convex set. Thus, any halfspace \(h^+\) containing all the points of \(Y\) will contain the point \(\pntB \in \CHX {Y}\). But \(\pntB \in \CHX {Z} \cap h^+\), and this implies that a point of \(Z\) must lie in \(h^+\), by Lemma 40.1.8. Namely, the subset \(Y \subseteq \PntSetB \) cannot be realized by a halfspace, which implies that \(\PntSetB \) cannot be shattered. Thus \(\VCDim (S) < d+2\). It is also easy to verify that the regular simplex with \(\hdim +1\) vertices is shattered by \(\RangeSpace \). Thus, \(\VCDim (S) = \hdim +1\).

40.2 Shattering dimension and the dual shattering dimension

The main property of a range space with bounded dimension is that the number of ranges for a set of \(n\) elements grows polynomially in \(n\) (with the power being the dimension) instead of exponentially. Formally, let the growth function be

\(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{1}\)

\begin{equation} \Growth {\Dim }{n} = \sum _{i=0}^{\Dim } \binom {n}{i} \leq \sum _{i=0}^{\Dim } \frac {n^i}{i!} \leq n^\Dim , \eqlab {g:d:n:estimate} \end{equation}

for \(\Dim >1\) (the cases where \(\Dim =0\) or \(\Dim =1\) are not interesting and we will just ignore them). Note that for all \(n,\Dim \geq 1\), we have \(\Growth {\Dim }{n} = \Growth {\Dim }{n-1} + \Growth {\Dim -1}{n-1}\)40.1.

40.1 Here is a cute (and standard) counting argument: \(\Growth {\Dim }{n}\) is just the number of different subsets of size at most \(\Dim \) out of \(n\) elements. Now, we either decide to not include the first element in these subsets (i.e., \(\Growth {\Dim }{n-1}\)) or, alternatively, we include the first element in these subsets, but then there are only \(\Dim -1\) elements left to pick (i.e., \(\Growth {\Dim -1}{n-1}\)).

  • Lemma 40.2.1 (Sauer’s lemma). If \((\GroundSet ,\RangeSet )\) is a range space of dimension \(\Dim \) with \(|\GroundSet | = n\), then \(|\RangeSet | \leq \Growth {\Dim }{n}\).

  • Proof: The claim trivially holds for \(\Dim =0\) or \(n=0\).

    Let \(x\) be any element of \(\GroundSet \), and consider the sets

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{2}\)

    \begin{equation*} \RangeSet _x=\Set { \range \setminus \brc {x} }{\bigl . \range \cup \brc {x} \in \RangeSet \text { and }\range \setminus \brc {x} \in \RangeSet }\quad \text {and}\quad \RangeSet \setminus x=\Set { \range \setminus \brc {x} }{ \bigl . \range \in \RangeSet }. \end{equation*}

    Observe that \(\cardin {\RangeSet } = \cardin {\RangeSet _x} + \cardin {\RangeSet \setminus x}\). Indeed, we charge the elements of \(\RangeSet \) to their corresponding element in \(\RangeSet \setminus x\). The only bad case is when there is a range \(\range \) such that both \(\range \cup \brc {x} \in \RangeSet \) and \(\range \setminus \brc {x} \in \RangeSet \), because then these two distinct ranges get mapped to the same range in \(\RangeSet \setminus x\). But such ranges contribute exactly one element to \(\RangeSet _x\). Similarly, every element of \(\RangeSet _x\) corresponds to two such “twin” ranges in \(\RangeSet \).

    Observe that \(( X \setminus \brc {x}, \RangeSet _x)\) has dimension \(\Dim -1\), as the largest set that can be shattered is of size \(\Dim -1\). Indeed, any set \(B \subset \GroundSet \setminus \brc {x}\) shattered by \(\RangeSet _x\) implies that \(B \cup \brc {x}\) is shattered in \(\RangeSet \).

    Thus, we have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{2}\)

    \begin{equation*} \cardin {\RangeSet }=\cardin {\RangeSet _x} + \cardin {\RangeSet \setminus x} \leq \Growth {\Dim -1}{n-1} + \Growth {\Dim }{n-1}=\Growth {\Dim }{n}, \end{equation*}

    by induction.

Interestingly, Lemma 40.2.1 is tight.

Next, we show pretty tight bounds on \(\Growth {\Dim }{n}\). The proof is technical and not very interesting, and it is delegated to Section 40.4.

  • Lemma 40.2.2. For \(n \geq 2\Dim \) and \(\Dim \geq 1\), we have \(\;\displaystyle \pth {\frac {n }{\Dim }}^\Dim \leq \Growth {\Dim }{n}\leq 2\, \pth {\frac {n e}{\Dim }}^\Dim ,\)where \(\ds \Growth {\Dim }{n} = \sum _{i=0}^{\Dim } \binom {n}{i}\).

  • Definition 40.2.3 (Shatter function). Given a range space \(\RangeSpace = (\GroundSet , \RangeSet )\), its shatter function \(\ShatterFunc {\RangeSpace }(m)\) is the maximum number of sets that might be created by \(\RangeSpace \) when restricted to subsets of size \(m\). Formally,

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{2}\)

    \begin{equation*} \ShatterFunc {\RangeSpace }(m)=\smash {\max _{\substack {B \subset \GroundSet \\ \cardin {B} = m}} \cardin { \VCProj {\RangeSet }{B} }}; \end{equation*}

    see Eq. (40.1).

Our arch-nemesis in the following is the function \(x / \ln x\). The following lemma states some properties of this function, and its proof is left as an exercise.

  • Lemma 40.2.4. For the function \(\,f(x) = x / \ln x\,\) the following hold.

    • (A) \(f(x)\) is monotonically increasing for \(x \geq e\).

    • (B) \(f(x) \geq e\), for \(x >1\).

    • (C) For \(u \geq \sqrt {e}\), if \(f(x) \leq u\), then \(x \leq 2 u \ln u\).

    • (D) For \(u \geq \sqrt {e}\), if \(x > 2 u \ln u\), then \(f(x) > u\).

    • (E) For \(u \geq e\), if \(f(x) \geq u\), then \(x \geq u \ln u\).

40.2.1 Mixing range spaces

  • Lemma 40.2.5. Let \(\RangeSpace = (\GroundSet ,\RangeSet )\) and \(\RangeSpaceA = (\GroundSet ,\RangeSetA )\) be two range spaces of   dimension \(\Dim \) and \(\DimA \), respectively, where \(\Dim , \DimA > 1\). Let \(\Bigl . \widehat {\RangeSet }=\Set {\range \cup \rangeA }{ \range \in \RangeSet , \rangeA \in \RangeSetA }.\)Then, for the range space \(\widehat {\RangeSpace } = \bigl ( \ts \GroundSet , \widehat {\RangeSet } \bigr ),\)we have that \(\VCDim \bigl ( \ts \widehat {\RangeSpace } \ts \bigr )=O(\Dim + \DimA )\).

  • Proof: As a warm-up exercise, we prove a somewhat weaker bound here of \(O( (\Dim + \DimA ) \log \pth {\Dim + \DimA } )\). The stronger bound follows from Theorem 40.2.6 below. Let \(B\) be a set of \(n\) points in \(\GroundSet \) that are shattered by \(\widehat {\RangeSpace }\). There are at most \(\Growth {\Dim }{n }\) and \(\Growth {\DimA }{n }\) different ranges of \(B\) in the range sets \(\VCProj {\RangeSet }{B}\) and \(\VCProj {\RangeSetA }{B}\), respectively, by Lemma 40.2.1. Every subset \(C\) of \(B\) realized by \(\widehat {r} \in \widehat {\RangeSet }\) is a union of two subsets \(B \cap \range \) and \(B \cap \rangeA \), where \(\range \in R\) and \(\rangeA \in \RangeSetA \), respectively. Thus, the number of different subsets of \(B\) realized by \(\widehat {\RangeSpace }\) is bounded by \(\Growth {\Dim }{n} \Growth {\DimA }{n }\). Thus, \(2^n \leq n^{\Dim } n^{\DimA }\), for \(\Dim , \DimA > 1\). We conclude that \(n \leq (\Dim + \DimA ) \lg n\), which implies that \(n = O \pth { \pth []{\Dim + \DimA } \log \pth { \Dim + \DimA }}\), by Lemma 40.2.4(C).

Interestingly, one can prove a considerably more general result with tighter bounds. The required computations are somewhat more painful.

  • Theorem 40.2.6. Let \(\RangeSpace _1 = \pth {\GroundSet ,\RangeSet ^1}, \ldots , \RangeSpace _k = \pth {\GroundSet ,\RangeSet ^k}\) be range spaces with dimension \(\Dim _1, \ldots , \Dim _k\), respectively. Next, let \(f\pth { \range _1, \ldots , \range _k}\) be a function that maps any \(k\)-tuple of sets \(\range _1 \in \RangeSet ^1, \ldots , \range _k \in \RangeSet ^k\) into a subset of \(\GroundSet \). Here, the function \(f\) is restricted to be defined by a sequence of set operations like complement, intersection and union. Consider the range set

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{2}\)

    \begin{align*} \RangeSetA =\Set { f\pth { \range _1, \ldots , \range _k}}{ \range _1 \in \RangeSet _1, \ldots , \range _k \in \RangeSet _k \bigr .} \end{align*} and the associated range space \(\RangeSpaceA = \pth {\GroundSet , \RangeSetA }\). Then, the dimension of \(\;\RangeSpaceA \) is bounded by \(O\pth { k \Dim \lg k}\), where \(\Dim = \max _i \Dim _i\).

  • Proof: Assume a set \(Y \subseteq \GroundSet \) of size \(t\) is being shattered by \(\RangeSetA \), and observe that

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{2}\)

    \begin{align*} \bigl | \VCProj {\RangeSetA }{Y} \bigr |&\leq \cardin {\,\Set {\,\pth []{ \range _1, \ldots , \range _k} }{ \range _1 \in \smash {\VCProj {\RangeSet ^1}{Y}}, \ldots , \range _k \in \smash {\VCProj {\RangeSet ^k}{Y}} \bigr .} \Bigr . }\leq \cardin {\smash {\VCProj {\RangeSet ^1}{Y}} \bigr .} \cdots \cardin {\smash {\VCProj {\RangeSet ^k}{Y}}\bigr .}\leq {\Growth {\Dim _1}{t}} \cdot {\Growth {\Dim _2}{t}} \cdots {\Growth {\Dim _k}{t}}\\ &\leq \pth {\bigl . \Growth {\Dim }{t}}^k\leq \Bigl ( 2\, \bigl ( {t e}/{\Dim } \bigr )^\Dim \Bigr )^k , \end{align*} by Lemma 40.2.1 and Lemma 40.2.2. On the other hand, since \(Y\) is being shattered by \(\RangeSetA \), this implies that \(\bigl | \VCProj {\RangeSetA }{Y}\bigr | = 2^t\). Thus, we have the inequality \(2^t \leq \bigl ( 2 \pth { \nfrac {t e}{\Dim }}^\Dim \bigr )^k\), which implies \(t \leq k \pth { 1 + \Dim \lg (\nfrac {t e}{\Dim }) }\). Assume that \(t \geq e\) and \(\Dim \lg \pth {\nfrac {t e}{\Dim }} \geq 1\) since otherwise the claim is trivial, and observe that \(t \leq k \pth { 1 + \Dim \lg \pth {\nfrac {t e}{\Dim }} } \leq 3 k \Dim \lg (t/ \Dim )\). Setting \(x = t/\Dim \), we have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{2}\)

    \begin{equation*} \frac {t}{\Dim }\leq 3k \frac {\ln \pth { \nfrac {t}{\Dim }}}{\ln 2} \leq 6k \ln \frac {t}{\Dim }\implies \frac {x}{\ln x} \leq 6k\implies x \leq 2 \cdot 6k \ln \pth { 6k }\implies x \leq 12 k \ln (6 k ), \end{equation*}

    by Lemma 40.2.4(C). We conclude that \(t \leq 12 \Dim k \ln (6 k )\), as claimed.

  • Corollary 40.2.7. Let \(\RangeSpace =(\GroundSet , \RangeSet )\) and \(\RangeSpaceA = (\GroundSet , \RangeSetA )\) be two range spaces of dimension \(\Dim \) and \(\DimA \), respectively, where \(\Dim ,\DimA > 1\). Let \(\widehat {\RangeSet }=\Set { \range \cap \rangeA }{ \range \in \RangeSet , \rangeA \in \RangeSetA \bigr .}.\)Then, for the range space \(\widehat {\RangeSpace } = (\GroundSet , \widehat {\RangeSet })\), we have that \(\VCDim ( \widehat {\RangeSpace } ) = O( \Dim + \DimA )\).

  • Corollary 40.2.8. Any finite sequence of combining range spaces with finite dimension (by intersecting, complementing, or taking their union) results in a range space with a finite dimension.

40.3 On \(\eps \)-nets and \(\eps \)-sampling

40.3.1 \(\eps \)-nets and \(\eps \)-samples

  • Definition 40.3.1 (\(\eps \)-sample). Let \(\RangeSpace = (\GroundSet ,\RangeSet )\) be a range space, and let \(\FGroundSet \) be a finite subset of \(\GroundSet \). For \(0 \leq \eps \leq 1\), a subset \(C \subseteq \FGroundSet \) is an \(\eps \)-sample for \(\FGroundSet \) if for any range \(\range \in \RangeSet \), we have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{2}\)

    \begin{align*} \cardin {\, \Measure {\range } - \sMeasure {\range } \bigr .\, }\leq \eps , \end{align*} where \(\Measure {\range } = {\cardin {\FGroundSet \cap \range }} \, /\, {\cardin {\FGroundSet }}\) is the measure of \(\range \) (see Definition 40.1.2) and \(\sMeasure {\range } = {\cardin {C \cap \range }} \, /\,{\cardin {C}}\) is the estimate of \(\range \) (see Definition 40.1.3). (Here \(C\) might be a multi-set, and as such \(\cardin {C \cap \range }\) is counted with multiplicity.)

As such, an \(\eps \)-sample is a subset of the ground set \(\FGroundSet \) that “captures” the range space up to an error of \(\eps \). Specifically, to estimate the fraction of the ground set covered by a range \(\range \), it is sufficient to count the points of \(C\) that fall inside \(\range \).

If \(\GroundSet \) is a finite set, we will abuse notation slightly and refer to \(C\) as an \(\eps \)-sample for \(\RangeSpace \).

To see the usage of such a sample, consider \(\FGroundSet = \GroundSet \) to be, say, the population of a country (i.e., an element of \(\GroundSet \) is a citizen). A range in \(\RangeSet \) is the set of all people in the country that answer yes to a question (i.e., would you vote for party Y?, would you buy a bridge from me?, questions like that). An \(\eps \)-sample of this range space enables us to estimate reliably (up to an error of \(\eps \)) the answers for all these questions, by just asking the people in the sample.

The natural question of course is how to find such a subset of small (or minimal) size.

  • Theorem 40.3.2 (\(\eps \)-sample theorem, [vc-ucrfe-71]). There is a positive constant \(c\) such that if \((\GroundSet ,\RangeSet )\) is any range space with dimension at most \(\Dim \), \(\FGroundSet \subseteq \GroundSet \) is a finite subset and \(\eps , \BadProb > 0\), then a random subset \(C\subseteq \FGroundSet \) of cardinality

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{2}\)

    \begin{align*} s = \frac {c}{\eps ^2} \pth { \Dim \log \frac {\Dim }{\eps } + \log \frac {1}{\BadProb }}\ \end{align*} is an \(\eps \)-sample for \(\FGroundSet \) with probability at least \(1-\BadProb \).

(In the above theorem, if \(s > \cardin {\FGroundSet }\), then we can just take all of \(\FGroundSet \) to be the \(\eps \)-sample.)

Sometimes it is sufficient to have (hopefully smaller) samples with a weaker property – if a range is “heavy”, then there is an element in our sample that is in this range.

  • Definition 40.3.3 (\(\eps \)-net). A set \(N \subseteq \FGroundSet \) is an \(\eps \)-net for \(\FGroundSet \) if for any range \(\range \in \RangeSet \), if \(\Measure {\range } \geq \eps \) (i.e., \(\cardin {\range \cap \FGroundSet } \geq \eps \cardin {\FGroundSet }\)), then \(\range \) contains at least one point of \(N\) (i.e., \(\range \cap N \ne \emptyset \)).

  • Theorem 40.3.4 (\(\eps \)-net theorem, [hw-ensrq-87]). Let \((\GroundSet ,\RangeSet )\) be a range space of dimension \(\Dim \), let \(\FGroundSet \) be a finite subset of \(\GroundSet \), and suppose that \(0 < \eps \leq 1\) and \(\BadProb < 1\). Let \(N\) be a set obtained by \(m\) random independent draws from \(\FGroundSet \), where

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{2}\)

    \begin{equation} m \geq \max \pth {\; \frac {4}{\eps } \lg \frac {4}{\BadProb } \,,\, \frac {8\Dim }{\eps }\lg \frac {16}{\eps } \;}. \eqlab {e:net} \end{equation}

    Then \(N\) is an \(\eps \)-net for \(\FGroundSet \) with probability at least \(1-\BadProb \).

(We remind the reader that \(\lg =\log _2\).)

The proofs of the above theorems are somewhat involved and we first turn our attention to some applications before presenting the proofs.

  • Remark 40.3.5. The above two theorems also hold for spaces with shattering dimension at most \(\Dim \), in which case the sample size is slightly larger. Specifically, for Theorem 40.3.4, the sample size needed is \(\ds O\pth { \frac {1}{\eps } \lg \frac {1}{\BadProb } + \frac {\Dim }{\eps }\lg \frac {\Dim }{\eps } }\).

  • Remark 40.3.6. The \(\eps \)-net theorem is a relatively easy consequence (up to constants) of the \(\eps \)-sample theorem – see bibliographical notes for details.

40.3.2 Some applications

We mention two (easy) applications of these theorems, which (hopefully) demonstrate their power.

40.3.2.1 Range searching

So, consider a (very large) set of points \(\PntSet \) in the plane. We would like to be able to quickly decide how many points are included inside a query rectangle. Let us assume that we allow ourselves \(1\%\) error. What Theorem 40.3.2 tells us is that there is a subset of constant size (that depends only on \(\eps \)) that can be used to perform this estimation, and it works for all query rectangles (we used here the fact that rectangles in the plane have finite dimension). In fact, a random sample of this size works with constant probability.

40.3.2.2 Learning a concept

Assume that we have a function \(f\) defined in the plane that returns ‘\(1\)’ inside an (unknown) disk \(\disk _{\text {unknown}}\) and ‘\(0\)’ outside it. There is some distribution \(\Distribution \) defined over the plane, and we pick points from this distribution. Furthermore, we can compute the function for these labels (i.e., we can compute \(f\) for certain values, but it is expensive). For a mystery value \(\eps >0\), to be explained shortly, Theorem 40.3.4 tells us to pick (roughly) \(O((1/\eps ) \log (1/\eps ))\) random points in a sample \(\Sample \) from this distribution and to compute the labels for the samples. This is demonstrated in Figure 40.2 (a), where black dots are the sample points for which \(f(\cdot )\) returned \(1\).

So, now we have positive examples and negative examples. We would like to find a hypothesis that agrees with all the samples we have and that hopefully is close to the true unknown disk underlying the function \(f\). To this end, compute the smallest disk \(\disk \) that contains the sample labeled by ‘\(1\)’ and does not contain any of the ‘\(0\)’ points (see Figure 40.2 (b)), and let \(g:\Re ^2 \rightarrow \brc {0,1}\) be the function \(g\) that returns ‘\(1\)’ inside the disk and ‘\(0\)’ otherwise. We claim that \(g\) classifies correctly all but an \(\eps \)-fraction of the points (i.e., the probability of misclassifying a point picked according to the given distribution is smaller than \(\eps \)); that is, \(\ProbExt {\pnt \in \Distribution }{f(\pnt ) \ne g(\pnt )} \leq \eps \).

Geometrically, the region where \(g\) and \(f\) disagree is all the points in the symmetric difference between the two disks. That is, \(\Error = \disk \oplus \disk _{\text {unknown}}\); see Figure 40.2 (c).

Thus, consider the range space \(\RangeSpace \) having the plane as the ground set and the symmetric difference between any two disks as its ranges. By Corollary 40.2.8, this range space has finite dimension. Now, consider the (unknown) disk \(\diskA \) that induces \(f\) and the region \(\range = \disk _\text {unknown} \oplus \disk \). Clearly, the learned classifier \(g\) returns incorrect answers only for points picked inside \(\range \).

Thus, the probability of a mistake in the classification is the measure of \(\range \) under the distribution \(\Distribution \). So, if \(\ProbLTR _\Distribution [ \range ] > \eps \) (i.e., the probability that a sample point falls inside \(\range \)), then by the \(\eps \)-net theorem (i.e., Theorem 40.3.4) the set \(\Sample \) is an \(\eps \)-net for \(\RangeSpace \) (ignore for the time being the possibility that the random sample fails to be an \(\eps \)-net) and as such, \(\Sample \) contains a point \(\pntA \) inside \(\range \). But, it is not possible for \(g\) (which classifies correctly all the sampled points of \(\Sample \)) to make a mistake on \(\pntA \), a contradiction, because by construction, the range \(\range \) is where \(g\) misclassifies points. We conclude that \(\ProbLTR _\Distribution \, \pbrc { \range } \leq \eps \), as desired.

Little lies. The careful reader might be tearing his or her hair out because of the above description. First, Theorem 40.3.4 might fail, and the above conclusion might not hold. This is of course true, and in real applications one might use a much larger sample to guarantee that the probability of failure is so small that it can be practically ignored. A more serious issue is that Theorem 40.3.4 is defined only for finite sets. Nowhere does it speak about a continuous distribution. Intuitively, one can approximate a continuous distribution to an arbitrary precision using a huge sample and apply the theorem to this sample as our ground set. A formal proof is more tedious and requires extending the proof of Theorem 40.3.4 to continuous distributions. This is straightforward and we will ignore this topic altogether.

40.4 A better bound on the growth function

In this section, we prove Lemma 40.2.2. Since the proof is straightforward but tedious, the reader can safely skip reading this section.

  • Lemma 40.4.1. For any positive integer \(n\), the following hold.

    .
    (i) \((1+1/n)^n \leq e\).                    (ii) \((1-1/n)^{n-1} \geq e^{-1}\).
    (iii) \(n! \geq (n/e)^n\). (iv) For any \(k \leq n\), we have \(\;\;\displaystyle \pth {\frac {n}{k}}^k \leq \binom {n}{k} \leq \pth {\frac {n e}{k}}^k\).

  • Proof: (i) Indeed, \(1+1/n\leq \exp \pth {1/n}\), since \(1+x \leq e^x\), for \(x \geq 0\). As such \((1+1/n)^n \leq \exp \pth {n(1/n)} = e\).

    (ii) Rewriting the inequality, we have that we need to prove \(\pth {\frac {n-1}{n}}^{n-1} \geq \frac {1}{e}\). This is equivalent to proving \(e \geq \pth {\frac {n}{n-1}}^{n-1} = \pth { 1+\frac {1}{n-1}}^{n-1}\), which is our friend from (i).

    (iii) Indeed,

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{3}\)

    \begin{equation*} \frac {n^n}{n!} \leq \sum _{i=0}^{\infty } \frac {n^i}{i!} = e^n, \end{equation*}

    by the Taylor expansion of \(e^x = \sum _{i=0}^\infty \frac {x^i}{i!}\). This implies that \((n/e)^n \leq n!\), as required.

    (iv) Indeed, for any \(k \leq n\), we have \(\frac {n}{k} \leq \frac {n-1}{k-1}\), as can be easily verified. As such, \(\frac {n}{k} \leq \frac {n-i}{k-i}\), for \(1 \leq i \leq k-1\). As such,

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{3}\)

    \begin{equation*} \pth {\frac {n}{k}}^k \leq \frac {n}{k} \cdot \frac {n-1}{k-1} \cdots \frac {n-k+1} {1} = \binom {n}{k}. \end{equation*}

    As for the other direction, by (iii), we have \(\displaystyle \; \binom {n}{k} \leq \frac {n^k}{k!} \leq \frac {n^k}{\pth {\frac {k}{e}}^{k}} = \pth { \frac {n e}{k} }^k\).

Lemma 40.2.2 restated. For \(n \geq 2\Dim \) and \(\Dim \geq 1\), we have \(\;\displaystyle \pth {\frac {n }{\Dim }}^\Dim \leq \Growth {\Dim }{n}\leq 2\, \pth {\frac {n e}{\Dim }}^\Dim ,\)where \(\ds \Growth {\Dim }{n} = \sum _{i=0}^{\Dim } \binom {n}{i}\).

  • Proof: Note that by Lemma 40.4.1 (iv), we have \(\displaystyle \Growth {\Dim }{n} = \sum _{i=0}^{\Dim } \binom {n}{i} \leq 1 + \sum _{i=1}^{\Dim } \pth {\frac {ne}{i}}^i\). This series behaves like a geometric series with constant larger than \(2\), since

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{40.}{3}\)

    \begin{equation*} {\pth {\frac {ne}{i}}^i} \;/ \; {\pth {\frac {ne}{i-1}}^{i-1}} = \frac {ne }{i} \pth {\frac {i-1}{i}}^{i-1} = \frac {ne }{i} \pth {1 - \frac {1}{i}}^{i-1} \geq \frac {ne }{i} \frac {1}{e} = \frac {n}{i} \geq \frac {n}{\Dim } \geq 2, \end{equation*}

    by Lemma 40.4.1. As such, this series is bounded by twice the largest element in the series, implying the claim.
