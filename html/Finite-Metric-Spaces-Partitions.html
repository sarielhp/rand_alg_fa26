42.1 Finite Metric Spaces

  • Definition 42.1.1. A metric space is a pair \((\FMS , \DistChar )\) where \(\FMS \) is a set and \(\DistChar : \FMS \times \FMS \rightarrow [0, \infty )\) is a metric, satisfying the following axioms:

    • (i) \(\DistChar (x,y) = 0\) \(\iff \) \(x =y\),

    • (ii) \(\DistMtr {x}{y}= \DistMtr {y}{x}\), and

    • (iii) \(\DistMtr {x}{y} + \DistMtr {y}{z} \geq \DistMtr {x}{z}\) (triangle inequality).

The plane, \(\Re ^2\), with the regular Euclidean distance is a metric space.

Of special interest is the finite case, where \(\FMS \) is an \(n\)-point set. Then, the function \(\DistChar \) can be specified by \(\binom {n}{2}\) real numbers. Alternatively, one can think about \((\FMS ,\DistChar )\) as a weighted complete graph, where positive weights are specified on the edges, and these weights comply with the triangle inequality.

Finite metric spaces rise naturally from (sparse) graphs. Indeed, let \(\Graph =(\FMS ,\Edges )\) be an undirected weighted graph defined over \(\FMS \), and let \(\dGY {x}{y}\) be the length of the shortest path between \(x\) and \(y\) in \(\Graph \). It is easy to verify that \((\FMS , \DistChar _G)\) is a finite metric space. As such if the graph \(\Graph \) is sparse, it provides a compact representation to the finite space \((\FMS , \DistChar _G)\).

  • Definition 42.1.2. Let \((\FMS ,d)\) be an \(n\)-point metric space. We denote the open ball of radius \(r\) about \(x \in \FMS \), by \(\Ball (x,r) = \Set { y \in \FMS }{ \dY {x}{y} < r }\).

Underling our discussion of metric spaces are algorithmic applications. The hardness of various computational problems depends heavily on the structure of the finite metric space. Thus, given a finite metric space, and a computational task, it is natural to try to map the given metric space into a new metric where the task at hand becomes easy.

  • Example 42.1.3. Computing the diameter of a point set is not trivial in two dimensions (if one wants near linear running time), but is easy in one dimension. Thus, if we could map points in two dimensions into points in one dimension, such that the diameter is preserved, then computing the diameter becomes easy. This approach yields an efficient approximation algorithm, see Exercise 42.7.3 below.

Of course, this mapping from one metric space to another, is going to introduce error. Naturally, one would like to minimize the error introduced by such a mapping.

  • Definition 42.1.4. Let \((\FMS ,\DistChar _\FMS )\) and \((\FMSB ,\DistChar _\FMSB )\) be two metric spaces. A mapping \(f:\FMS \to \FMSB \) is an embedding, and it is \(C\)-Lipschitz if \(\DistChar _Y\bigl (f(x),f(y) \bigr )\leq C \cdot \DistChar _\FMS (x,y)\)for all \(x,y\in \FMS \). The mapping \(f\) is \(K\)-bi-Lipschitz if there exists a \(C > 0\) such that

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} CK^{-1} \cdot \DistChar _\FMS (x,y)\leq \DistChar _Y\bigl (f(x),f(y)\bigr )\leq C\cdot \DistChar _\FMS (x,y), \end{equation*}

    for all \(x,y\in \FMS \).

    The least \(K\) for which \(f\) is \(K\)-bi-Lipschitz is the distortion of \(f\), and is denoted \(\Distor (f)\). The least distortion with which \(\FMS \) may be embedded in \(\FMSB \) is denoted \(c^{}_\FMSB (\FMS )\).

Informally, if \(f:\FMS \to \FMSB \) has distortion \(K\), then the distances in \(\FMS \) and \(f(\FMS ) \subseteq \FMSB \) are the same up to a factor of \(K\) (one might need to scale up the distances by some constant \(C\)).

There are several powerful results about low distortion embeddings that would be presented:

  • (I) Probabilistic trees. Every finite metric can be randomly embedded into a tree such that the “expected” distortion for a specific pair of points is \(O( \log n)\).

  • (II) Bourgain embedding. Any \(n\)-point metric space can be embedded into (finite dimensional) euclidean metric space with \(O( \log n )\) distortion.

  • (III) Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma. Any \(n\)-point set in Euclidean space with the regular Euclidean distance can be embedded into \(\Re ^k\) with distortion \((1+\eps )\), where \(k = O(\eps ^{-2} \log n)\).

42.2 Examples

What is distortion? When considering a mapping \(f:\FMS \rightarrow \Re ^d\) of a metric space \((\FMS , \DistChar )\) to \(\Re ^d\), it would useful to observe that since \(\Re ^d\) can be scaled, we can consider \(f\) to be an expansion (i.e., no distances shrink). Furthermore, we can assume that there is at least one pair of points \(x, y \in \FMS \), such that \(\DistChar (x,y) = \dist {x-y}\). As such, we have \(\Distor (f) = \max _{x,y} \frac {\dist {x - y}}{\DistChar (x,y)}\).

Why is distortion necessary? Consider the a graph \(\Graph =(\Vertices ,\Edges )\) with one vertex \(s\) connected to three other vertices \(a, b, c\), where the weights on the edges are all one (i.e., \(\Graph \) is the star graph with three leafs). We claim that \(\Graph \) can not be embedded into Euclidean space with distortion \(\leq \sqrt {2}\). Indeed, consider the associated metric space \((\Vertices ,\DistChar _G)\) and an (expansive) embedding \(f: \Vertices \rightarrow \Re ^d\).

   

Consider the triangle formed by \(\triangle = a'b' c'\), where \(a' = f(a), b'= f(b)\) and \(c' =f(c)\). Next, consider the following quantity \(\max \pth { \dist {a'-s'},\dist {b'-s'},\dist {c'-s'}}\) which lower bounds the distortion of \(f\). This quantity is minimized when \(r= \dist {a'-s'}=\dist {b'-s'}=\dist {c'-s'}\). Namely, \(s'\) is the center of the smallest enclosing circle of \(\triangle \). However, \(r\) is minimized when all the edges of \(\triangle \) are of equal length, and are of length \(\DistChar _G(a,b) = 2\). It follows that \(\Distor (f) \geq r \geq 2/\sqrt {3}\).

This quantity is minimized when \(r= \dist {a'-s'}=\dist {b'-s'}=\dist {c'-s'}\). Namely, \(s'\) is the center of the smallest enclosing circle of \(\triangle \). However, \(r\) is minimized when all the edges of \(\triangle \) are of equal length and are of length \(\dGY {a}{b} = 2\). Observe that the height of the equilateral triangle with sidelength \(2\) is \(h = \sqrt {3}\), and the radius of its inscribing circle is \(r = (2/3)h = 2 /\mysqrt {3}\); see Figure 42.1. As such, it follows that \(\Distor (f) \geq r = 2/\sqrt {3}\).

Note that the above argument is independent of the target dimension \(\hdim \). A packing argument shows that embedding the star graph with \(n\) leaves into \(\Re ^\hdim \) requires distortion \(\Omega \pth {n^{1/\hdim }}\); see Exercise ??. It is known that \(\Omega (\log n)\) distortion is necessary in the worst case when embedding a graph into Euclidean space (this is shown using expanders). A proof of distortion \(\Omega \pth { \log n / \log \log n}\) is sketched in the bibliographical notes.

42.2.1 Hierarchical Tree Metrics

The following metric is quite useful in practice, and nicely demonstrate why algorithmically finite metric spaces are useful.

  • Definition 42.2.1. Hierarchically well-separated tree (HST) is a metric space defined on the leaves of a rooted tree \(T\). To each vertex \(u\in T\) there is associated a label \(\Delta _u \ge 0\) such that \(\Delta _u=0\) if and only if \(u\) is a leaf of \(T\). The labels are such that if a vertex \(u\) is a child of a vertex \(v\) then \(\Delta _u\leq \Delta _v\). The distance between two leaves \(x,y\in T\) is defined as \(\Delta _{\lca (x,y)}\), where \(\lca (x,y)\) is the least common ancestor of \(x\) and \(y\) in \(T\).

    A HST \(T\) is a \(k\)-HST if for a vertex \(v \in T\), we have that \(\Delta _v \leq \Delta _{\parent (v)}/k\), where \(\parent (v)\) is the parent of \(v\) in \(T\).

Note that a HST is a very limited metric. For example, consider the cycle \(G = C_n\) of \(n\) vertices, with weight one on the edges, and consider an expansive embedding \(f\) of \(\Graph \) into a HST \(\HST \). It is easy to verify, that there must be two consecutive nodes of the cycle, which are mapped to two different subtrees of the root \(r\) of \(\HST \). Since \(\HST \) is expansive, it follows that \(\Delta _r \geq n/2\). As such, \(\Distor (f) \geq n/2\). Namely, HSTs fail to faithfully represent even very simple metrics.

42.2.2 Clustering

One natural problem we might want to solve on a graph (i.e., finite metric space) \((\FMS ,\DistChar )\) is to partition it into clusters. One such natural clustering is the \(k\)-median clustering, where we would like to choose a set \(C \subseteq \FMS \) of \(k\) centers, such that \(\MdPrc _C(\FMS ,\DistChar ) = \sum _{\pntA \in \FMS } \DistChar (\pntA , C)\) is minimized, where \(\DistChar (\pntA ,C) = \min _{c \in C} \DistChar (\pntA , c)\) is the distance of \(\pntA \) to its closest center in \(C\).

It is known that finding the optimal \(k\)-median clustering in a (general weighted) graph is NP-complete. As such, the best we can hope for is an approximation algorithm. However, if the structure of the finite metric space \((\FMS ,\DistChar )\) is simple, then the problem can be solved efficiently. For example, if the points of \(\FMS \) are on the real line (and the distance between \(a\) and \(b\) is just \(\cardin {a-b}\)), then \(k\)-median can be solved using dynamic programming.

Another interesting case is when the metric space \((\FMS ,\DistChar )\) is a HST. Is not too hard to prove the following lemma. See Exercise 42.7.1.

  • Lemma 42.2.2. Let \((\FMS ,\DistChar )\) be a HST defined over \(n\) points, and let \(k > 0\) be an integer. One can compute the optimal \(k\)-median clustering of \(\FMS \) in \(O(k^2 n )\) time.

Thus, if we can embed a general graph \(\Graph \) into a HST \(\HST \), with low distortion, then we could approximate the \(k\)-median clustering on \(\Graph \) by clustering the resulting HST, and “importing” the resulting partition to the original space. The quality of approximation, would be bounded by the distortion of the embedding of \(\Graph \) into \(\HST \).

42.3 Random Partitions

Let \((\FMS ,d)\) be a finite metric space. Given a partition \(P=\brc {C_1,\ldots ,C_m}\) of \(\FMS \), we refer to the sets \(C_i\) as clusters. We write \(\mathcal {P}_\FMS \) for the set of all partitions of \(\FMS \). For \(x\in \FMS \) and a partition \(P\in \mathcal { P}_\FMS \) we denote by \(P(x)\) the unique cluster of \(P\) containing \(x\). Finally, the set of all probability distributions on \(\mathcal {P}_\FMS \) is denoted \(\mathcal {D}_\FMS \).

The following partition scheme is due to [ckr-aazep-04].

42.3.1 Constructing the partition

Consider a given metric space \((\FMS , \DistChar )\), where \(\FMS \) is a set of \(n\) points.

Let \(\Delta = 2^u\) be a prescribed parameter, which is the required diameter of the resulting clusters. Choose, uniformly at random, a permutation \(\pi \) of \(\FMS \) and a random value \(\alpha \in \pbrcS {\nfrac {1}{4},\nfrac {1}{2}}\). Let \(R = \alpha \Delta \), and observe that it is uniformly distributed in the interval \([\Delta /4, \Delta /2]\).

The partition is now defined as follows: A point \(x\in \FMS \) is assigned to the cluster \(C_y\) of \(y\), where \(y\) is the first point in the permutation in distance \(\leq R\) from \(x\). Formally,

\(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

\begin{equation*} C_y = \brc { x \in \FMS \sep { x \in \Ball (y, R) \textrm { and } \pi (y) \leq \pi (z) \textrm { for all $z \in \FMS $ with } x \in \Ball (z, R)}}. \end{equation*}

Let \(P = \{C_y\}_{y \in \FMS }\) denote the resulting partition.

Here is a somewhat more intuitive explanation: Once we fix the radius of the clusters \(R\), we start scooping out balls of radius \(R\) centered at the points of the random permutation \(\pi \). At the \(i\)th stage, we scoop out only the remaining mass at the ball centered at \(x_i\) of radius \(r\), where \(x_i\) is the \(i\)th point in the random permutation.

42.3.2 Properties

The following lemma quantifies the probability of a (crystal) ball of radius \(t\) centered at a point \(x\) is fully contained in one of the clusters of the partition? (Otherwise, the crystal ball is of course broken.)

  • Lemma 42.3.1. Let \((\FMS ,d)\) be a finite metric space, \(\Delta = 2^u\) a prescribed parameter, and let \(P\) be the partition of \(\FMS \) generated by the above random partition. Then the following holds:

    • (i) For any \(C \in P\), we have \(\diam (C) \leq \Delta \).

    • (ii) Let \(x\) be any point of \(\FMS \), and \(t\) a parameter \(\leq \Delta /8\). Then,

      \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

      \begin{equation*} \Prob {\bigl . \Ball (x,t) \nsubseteq P(x)}\leq \frac {8t}{\Delta } \ln \frac {b}{a}, \end{equation*}

      where \(a = |\Ball (x, \Delta /8)|\), and \(b = |\Ball (x, \Delta )|\).

  • Proof: Since \(C_y \subseteq \Ball (y, R)\), we have that \(\diam (C_y) \leq \Delta \), and thus the first claim holds.

    Let \(U\) be the set of points of \(\Ball (x, \Delta )\), such that \(w \in U\) iff \(\Ball (w,R) \cap \Ball (x,t) \ne \emptyset \). Arrange the points of \(U\) in increasing distance from \(x\), and let \(w_1, \ldots , w_{b'}\) denote the resulting order, where \(b'= \cardin {U}\). Let \(I_k = [d(x, w_k)-t, d(x,w_k)+t]\) and write \(\Event _k\) for the event that \(w_k\) is the first point in \(\pi \) such that \(\Ball (x,t) \cap C_{w_k} \neq \emptyset \), and yet \(\Ball (x,t) \nsubseteq C_{w_k}\). Note that if \(w_k \in \Ball (x, \Delta /8)\), then \(\Prob {\Event _k} = 0\) since \(\Ball (x, t) \subseteq \Ball (x, \Delta /8) \subseteq \Ball (w_k, \Delta /4) \subseteq \Ball (w_k, R)\).

    In particular, \(w_1, \ldots , w_a \in \Ball (x,\Delta /8)\) and as such \(\Prob { \Event _1} = \cdots = \Prob { \Event _a} = 0\). Also, note that if \(\DistChar (x, w_k)< R- t\) then \(\Ball (w_k, R)\) contains \(\Ball (x, t)\) and as such \(\Event _k\) can not happen. Similarly, if \(\DistChar (x, w_k) > R + t\) then \(\Ball (w_k, R) \cap \Ball (x, t) = \emptyset \) and \(\Event _k\) can not happen. As such, if \(\Event _k\) happen then \(R-t \leq \DistChar (x,w_k) \leq R+t\). Namely, if \(\Event _k\) happen then \(R \in I_k\). Namely, \(\Prob { \Event _k } = \Prob { \Event _k \cap (R \in I_k) } = \Prob {R \in I_k} \cdot \Prob { \Event _k\,|\,R \in I_k}\). Now, \(R\) is uniformly distributed in the interval \([\Delta /4, \Delta /2]\), and \(I_k\) is an interval of length \(2t\). Thus, \(\Prob {R \in I_k} \leq 2t/(\Delta /4) = 8t/\Delta \).

    Next, to bound \(\Prob { \Event _k\,|\,R \in I_k}\), we observe that \(w_1, \ldots , w_{k-1}\) are closer to \(x\) than \(w_k\) and their distance to \(\Ball (x,t)\) is smaller than \(R\). Thus, if any of them appear before \(w_k\) in \(\pi \) then \(\Event _k\) does not happen. Thus, \(\Prob { \Event _k\,|\,R \in I_k}\) is bounded by the probability that \(w_k\) is the first to appear in \(\pi \) out of \(w_1, \ldots , w_k\). But this probability is \(1/k\), and thus \(\Prob { \Event _k\,|\,R \in I_k} \leq 1/k\).

    We are now ready for the kill. Indeed,

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

    \begin{align*} \Prob {\Ball (x,t) \nsubseteq P(x)}&=\sum _{k=1}^{b'} \Prob {\Event _k} = \sum _{k=a+1}^{b'} \Prob {\Event _k} =\sum _{k=a+1}^{b'} \Prob {R \in I_k} \cdot \Prob {\mathcal E_k\,|\,R \in I_k} \\& \leq \sum _{k=a+1}^{b'} \frac {8t}{\Delta } \cdot \frac {1}{k}\leq \frac {8t}{\Delta } \ln \frac {b'}{a}\leq \frac {8t}{\Delta } \ln \frac {b}{a}, \end{align*} since \(\sum _{k=a+1}^b \frac {1}{k} \leq \int _{a}^b \frac {dx}{x} = \ln \frac {b}{a}\) and \(b' \leq b\).

42.4 Probabilistic embedding into trees

In this section, given \(n\)-point finite metric \((\FMS ,\DistChar )\). we would like to embed it into a HST. As mentioned above, one can verify that for any embedding into HST, the distortion in the worst case is \(\Omega (n)\). Thus, we define a randomized algorithm that embed \((\FMS ,d)\) into a tree. Let \(T\) be the resulting tree, and consider two points \(x,y \in \FMS \). Consider the random variable \(\DistChar _T(x,y)\). We constructed the tree \(T\) such that distances never shrink; i.e. \(\DistChar (x,y) \leq \DistChar _T(x,y)\). The probabilistic distortion of this embedding is \(\max _{x,y} \Ex {\frac {\DistChar _T(x,y)}{\DistChar (x,y)}}\). Somewhat surprisingly, one can find such an embedding with logarithmic probabilistic distortion.

  • Theorem 42.4.1. Given \(n\)-point metric \((\FMS ,d)\) one can randomly embed it into a \(2\)-HST with probabilistic distortion \(\leq 24 \ln n\).

  • Proof: The construction is recursive. Let \(\diam (P)\), and compute a random partition of \(\FMS \) with cluster diameter \(\diam (P)/2\), using the construction of Section 42.3.1. We recursively construct a \(2\)-HST for each cluster, and hang the resulting clusters on the root node \(v\), which is marked by \(\Delta _v = \diam (P)\). Clearly, the resulting tree is a \(2\)-HST.

    For a node \(v \in T\), let \(\FMS (v)\) be the set of points of \(\FMS \) contained in the subtree of \(v\).

    For the analysis, assume \(\diam (P)=1\), and consider two points \(x,y \in \FMS \). We consider a node \(v \in T\) to be in level \(i\) if \(\level (v) = \ceil {\lg \Delta _v } =i\). The two points \(x\) and \(y\) correspond to two leaves in \(T\), and let \(\widehat {u}\) be the least common ancestor of \(x\) and \(y\) in \(t\). We have \(\DistChar _T(x,y) \leq 2^{\level (v)}\). Furthermore, note that along a path the levels are strictly monotonically increasing.

    Being more conservative, let \(w\) be the first ancestor of \(x\), such that \(\Ball = \Ball \bigl ( x,\DistChar (x,y) \bigr )\) is not completely contained in \(\FMS ({u_1}), \ldots , \FMS ({u_m})\), where \(u_1, \ldots , u_m\) are the children of \(w\). Clearly, \(\level (w) > \level \pth {\widehat { u}}\). Thus, \(\DistChar _T(x,y) \leq 2^{\level (w)}\).

    Consider the path \(\sigma \) from the root of \(T\) to \(x\), and let \(\Event _i\) be the event that \(\Ball \) is not fully contained in \(\FMS (v_i)\), where \(v_i\) is the node of \(\sigma \) of level \(i\) (if such a node exists). Furthermore, let \(Y_i\) be the indicator variable which is \(1\) if \(\Event _i\) is the first to happened out of the sequence of events \(\Event _0, \Event _{-1}, \ldots \). Clearly, \(\DistChar _T(x,y) \leq \sum Y_i 2^i\).

    Let \(t = \DistChar (x,y)\) and \(j = \floor {\lg \DistChar (x,y)}\), and \(n_i = \cardin {\Ball (x,2^i)}\) for \(i=0, \ldots , -\infty \). We have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} \Ex { \DistChar _T(x,y)} \leq \sum _{i=j}^{0} \Ex {Y_i} 2^i \leq \sum _{i=j}^{0} 2^i \Prob { \Event _i \cap \overline {\Event _{i-1}} \cap \overline {\Event _{i-1}} \cdots \overline {\Event _{0}}} \leq \sum _{i=j}^{0} 2^i \cdot \frac {8t}{2^i} \ln \frac {n_i}{n_{i-3}}, \end{equation*}

    by Lemma 42.3.1. Thus,

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} \Ex { \DistChar _T(x,y)} \leq 8t \ln \pth {\prod _{i=j}^{0} \frac {n_i}{n_{i-3}}} \leq 8t \ln \pth { n_0 \cdot n_1 \cdot n_2 } \leq 24 t \ln n. \end{equation*}

    It thus follows, that the expected distortion for \(x\) and \(y\) is \(\leq 24 \ln n\).

42.4.1 Application: approximation algorithm for \(k\)-median clustering

Let \((\FMS ,\DistChar )\) be a \(n\)-point metric space, and let \(k\) be an integer number. We would like to compute the optimal \(k\)-median clustering. Number, find a subset \(\Copt \subseteq \FMS \), such that \(\MdPrc _{\Copt }(\FMS ,\DistChar )\) is minimized, see Section 42.2.2. To this end, we randomly embed \((\FMS ,\DistChar )\) into a HST \(\HST \) using Theorem 42.4.1. Next, using Lemma 42.2.2, we compute the optimal \(k\)-median clustering of \(\HST \). Let \(C\) be the set of centers computed. We return \(C\) together with the partition of \(\FMS \) it induces as the required clustering.

  • Theorem 42.4.2. Let \((\FMS ,\DistChar )\) be a \(n\)-point metric space. One can compute in polynomial time a \(k\)-median clustering of \(\FMS \) which has expected price \(O\pth { \alpha \log n }\), where \(\alpha \) is the price of the optimal \(k\)-median clustering of \((\FMS ,\DistChar )\).

  • Proof: The algorithm is described above, and the fact that its running time is polynomial can be easily be verified. To prove the bound on the quality of the clustering, for any point \(p \in \FMS \), let \(\CenterPnt (p)\) denote the closest point in \(\Copt \) to \(p\) according to \(\DistChar \), where \(\Copt \) is the set of \(k\)-medians in the optimal clustering. Let \(C\) be the set of \(k\)-medians returned by the algorithm, and let \(\HST \) be the HST used by the algorithm. We have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} \beta =\MdPrc _C(\FMS ,\DistChar ) \leq \MdPrc _C(\FMS ,\DistChar _\HST ) \leq \MdPrc _{\Copt }(\FMS ,\DistChar _\HST ) \leq \sum _{p \in \FMS } \DistChar _\HST (p, \Copt ) \leq \sum _{p \in \FMS } \DistChar _\HST (p, \CenterPnt (p)). \end{equation*}

    Thus, in expectation we have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

    \begin{align*} \Ex {\beta }&= \Ex { \Bigl . \smash {\sum _{p \in \FMS } \DistChar _\HST (p, \CenterPnt (p))}}=\sum _{p \in \FMS } \Ex { \DistChar _\HST (p, \CenterPnt (p))} = \sum _{p \in \FMS } O \pth { \DistChar (p, \CenterPnt (p)) \log n } \\&=O\pth { \Bigl . \smash { (\log n) \sum _{p \in \FMS } \DistChar (p, \CenterPnt (p)) }}=O \pth { \MdPrc _{\Copt }(\FMS ,\DistChar ) \log n}, \end{align*} by linearity of expectation and Theorem 42.4.1.

42.5 Embedding any metric space into Euclidean space

  • Lemma 42.5.1. Let \((\FMS ,\DistChar )\) be a metric, and let \(Y \subset \FMS \). Consider the mapping \(f:\FMS \rightarrow \Re \), where \(f(x) = \DistChar (x,Y)= \min _{y \in Y} \DistChar ( x, y)\). Then for any \(x,y \in \FMS \), we have \(\cardin {f(x) - f(y)} \leq \DistChar (x,y)\). Namely \(f\) is nonexpansive.

  • Proof: Indeed, let \(x'\) and \(y'\) be the closet points of \(Y\), to \(x\) and \(y\), respectively. Observe that

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} f(x)=\DistChar (x, x')\leq \DistChar (x,y')\leq \DistChar ( x,y) + \DistChar (y, y')=\DistChar (x,y) + f(y) \end{equation*}

    by the triangle inequality. Thus, \(f(x) - f(y) \leq \DistChar (x,y)\). By symmetry, we have \(f(y) - f(x) \leq \DistChar (x,y)\). Thus, \(\cardin {f(x) - f(y)} \leq \DistChar (x,y)\).

42.5.1 The bounded spread case

Let \((\FMS ,\DistChar )\) be a \(n\)-point metric. The spread of \(\FMS \), denoted by

\(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

\begin{equation*} \Spread (\FMS )=\frac {\diam (\FMS )}{\min _{x,y \in \FMS , x \ne y} \DistChar (x,y)}, \end{equation*}

is the ratio between the diameter of \(\FMS \) and the distance between the closest pair of points.

  • Theorem 42.5.2. Given a \(n\)-point metric \(\FMSC = (\FMS ,d)\), with spread \(\Spread \), one can embed it into Euclidean space \(\Re ^k\) with distortion \(O\bigl ( \sqrt {\ln \Phi } \ln n\bigr )\), where \(k= O \pth { \ln \Phi \ln n}\).

  • Proof: Assume that \(\diam (\FMSC ) = \Spread \) (i.e., the smallest distance in \(\FMSC \) is \(1\)), and let \(r_i = 2^{i-2}\), for \(i=1,\ldots , \alpha \), where \(\alpha =\ceil {\lg \Spread }\). Let \(P_{i,j}\) be a random partition of \(P\) with diameter \(r_i\), using Theorem 42.4.1, for \(i=1,\ldots , \alpha \) and \(j=1,\ldots , \beta \), where \(\beta = \ceil { c \log n}\) and \(c\) is a large enough constant to be determined shortly.

    For each cluster of \(P_{i,j}\) randomly toss a coin, and let \(V_{i,j}\) be the all the points of \(\FMS \) that belong to clusters in \(P_{i,j}\) that got ’\(T\)’ in their coin toss. For a point \(u \in x\), let

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} f_{i,j}(x)=\DistChar (x, \FMS \setminus V_{i,j})=\min _{v \in \FMS \setminus V_{i,j}} \DistChar (x,v), \end{equation*}

    for \(i=0,\ldots , m\) and \(j=1,\ldots , \beta \). Let \(F: \FMS \rightarrow \Re ^{(m+1) \cdot \beta }\) be the embedding, such that

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} F(x)=\bigl ( \underbrace {f_{0,1}(x), f_{0,2}(x), \ldots , f_{0,\beta }(x)}_{\text {first n resolution block}}, f_{1,1}(x), f_{1,2}(x), \ldots , f_{1,\beta }(x), \ldots , f_{m,1}(x), f_{m,2}(x), \ldots , f_{\alpha ,\beta }(x)\bigr ). \end{equation*}

    Next, consider two points \(x,y \in \FMS \), with distance \(\phi = \DistChar (x,y)\). Let \(k\) be an integer such that \(r_u \leq \phi /2 \leq r_{u+1}\). Clearly, in any partition of \(P_{u,1}, \ldots , P_{u,\beta }\) the points \(x\) and \(y\) belong to different clusters. Furthermore, with probability half \(x \in V_{u,j}\) and \(y \notin V_{u,j}\) or \(x \notin V_{u,j}\) and \(y \in V_{u,j}\), for \(1 \leq j \leq \beta \).

    Let \(\Event _j\) denote the event that \(\Ball (x,\rho ) \subseteq V_{u,j}\) and \(y \notin V_{u,j}\), for \(j=1,\ldots , \beta \), where \(\rho =\phi /(64 \ln n)\). By Lemma 42.3.1, we have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} \Prob {\Ball (x,\rho ) \nsubseteq P_{u,j}(x) } \leq \frac {8\rho }{r_u} \ln n \leq \frac {\phi }{8r_u} \leq 1/2. \end{equation*}

    Thus,

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

    \begin{align*} \Prob {\Event _j}&=\Prob {\pth {\Ball (x,\rho ) \subseteq P_{u,j}(x) \MakeBig } \cap \pth {x \in V_{u,j}} \cap \pth { y \notin V_{u,j}} }\\&=\Prob {\Ball (x,\rho ) \subseteq P_{u,j}(x) } \cdot \Prob { x \in V_{u,j}} \cdot \Prob { y \notin V_{u,j} } \geq 1/8, \end{align*} since those three events are independent. Notice, that if \(\Event _j\) happens, than \(f_{u,j}(x) \geq \rho \) and \(f_{u,j}(y)=0\).

    Let \(X_j\) be an indicator variable which is \(1\) if \(\Event _j\) happens, for \(j=1,\ldots ,\beta \). Let \(Z = \sum _j X_j\), and we have \(\mu = \Ex {Z } = \Ex { \sum _j X_j} \geq \beta /8\). Thus, the probability that only \(\beta /16\) of \(\Event _1, \ldots , \Event _\beta \) happens, is \(\Prob {Z < (1-1/2)\Ex {Z}}\). By the Chernoff inequality, we have \(\Prob {Z < (1-1/2)\Ex {Z}} \leq \exp \pth { - \mu 1/(2\cdot 2^2)} = \exp \pth { -\beta /64} \leq 1/n^{10}\), if we set \(c=640\).

    Thus, with high probability

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} \dist {F(x) - F(y)} \geq \sqrt {\sum _{j=1}^{\beta } \pth {f_{u,j}(x) - f_{u,j}(y)}^2 }\geq \sqrt { \rho ^2 \frac {\beta }{16}} = \sqrt {\beta } \frac {\rho }{4} = \phi \cdot \frac {\sqrt {\beta }}{256 \ln n}. \end{equation*}

    On the other hand, \(\cardin {f_{i,j}(x) - f_{i,j}(y)} \leq \DistChar (x,y) = \phi \leq 64 \rho \ln n\). Thus,

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} \dist {F(x) - F(y)} \leq \sqrt {\alpha \beta \pth {64 \rho \ln n}^2 } \leq 64 \sqrt {\alpha \beta } \rho \ln n = \sqrt {\alpha \beta } \cdot \phi . \end{equation*}

    Thus, setting \(G(x) = F(x) \frac {256 \ln n}{\sqrt {\beta }}\), we get a mapping that maps two points of distance \(\phi \) from each other to two points with distance in the range \(\pbrc {\phi , \phi \cdot \sqrt {\alpha \beta } \cdot \frac {256 \ln n}{\sqrt {\beta }} }\). Namely, \(G(\cdot )\) is an embedding with distortion \(O( \sqrt {\alpha } \ln n) = O( \sqrt {\ln \Phi } \ln n)\).

    The probability that \(\Graph \) fails on one of the pairs, is smaller than \((1/n^{10}) \cdot \binom {n}{2} < 1/n^{8}\). In particular, we can check the distortion of \(\Graph \) for all \(\binom {n}{2}\) pairs, and if any of them fail (i.e., the distortion is too big), we restart the process.

42.5.2 The unbounded spread case

Our next task, is to extend Theorem 42.5.2 to the case of unbounded spread. Indeed, let \((\FMS ,d)\) be a \(n\)-point metric, such that \(\diam (\FMS ) \leq 1/2\). Again, we look on the different resolutions \(r_1, r_2, \ldots \), where \(r_i = 1/2^{i-1}\). For each one of those resolutions \(r_i\), we can embed this resolution into \(\beta \) coordinates, as done for the bounded case. Then we concatenate the coordinates together.

There are two problems with this approach: (i) the number of resulting coordinates is infinite, and (ii) a pair \(x,y\), might be distorted a “lot” because it contributes to all resolutions, not only to its “relevant” resolutions.

Both problems can be overcome with careful tinkering. Indeed, for a resolution \(r_i\), we are going to modify the metric, so that it ignores short distances (i.e., distances \(\leq r_i/n^2\)). Formally, for each resolution \(r_i\), let \(G_i =(\FMS ,\widehat {E_i})\) be the graph where two points \(x\) and \(y\) are connected if \(\DistChar (x,y) \leq r_i/n^2\). Consider a connected component \(C \in G_i\). For any two points \(x, y \in C\), we have \(\DistChar (x,y) \leq n( r_i/n^2 ) \leq r_i/n\). Let \(\FMS _i\) be the set of connected components of \(G_i\), and define the distances between two connected components \(C, C' \in \FMS _i\), to be \(\DistChar _i(C, C') = \DistChar (C, C') = \min _{c \in C, c' \in C'} \DistChar (c, c')\).

It is easy to verify that \((\FMS _i, \DistChar _i)\) is a metric space (see Exercise 42.7.2). Furthermore, we can naturally embed \((\FMS ,\DistChar )\) into \((\FMS _i, \DistChar _i)\) by mapping a point \(x \in \FMS \) to its connected components in \(\FMS _i\). Essentially \((\FMS _i,\DistChar _i)\) is a snapped version of the metric \((\FMS ,d)\), with the advantage that \(\Spread \pth { (\FMS , \DistChar _i) } = O(n^2)\). We now embed \(\FMS _i\) into \(\beta = O(\log n)\) coordinates. Next, for any point of \(\FMS \) we embed it into those \(\beta \) coordinates, by using the embedding of its connected component in \(\FMS _i\). Let \(E_i\) be the embedding for resolution \(r_i\). Namely, \(E_i(x) = (f_{i,1}(x), f_{i,2}(x), \ldots , f_{i,\beta }(x))\), where \(f_{i,j}(x) = \min ( \DistChar _i(x, \FMS \setminus V_{i,j}), 2r_i )\). The resulting embedding is \(F(x) = \oplus E_i(x) = (E_1(x), E_2(x), \ldots , )\).

Since we slightly modified the definition of \(f_{i,j}(\cdot )\), we have to show that \(f_{i,j}(\cdot )\) is nonexpansive. Indeed, consider two points \(x,y \in \FMS _i\), and observe that

\(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

\begin{equation*} \cardin {f_{i,j}(x) - f_{i,j}(y) } \leq \cardin {\DistChar _i(x, V_{i,j}) - \DistChar _i(y, V_{i,j})} \leq \DistChar _i(x,y) \leq \DistChar (x,y), \end{equation*}

as a simple case analysis42.1 shows.

For a pair \(x,y \in \FMS \), and let \(\phi =\DistChar (x,y)\). To see that \(F(\cdot )\) is the required embedding (up to scaling), observe that, by the same argumentation of Theorem 42.5.2, we have that with high probability

\(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

\begin{equation*} \dist {F(x)- F(y)} \geq \phi \cdot \frac {\sqrt {\beta }}{256 \ln n}. \end{equation*}

To get an upper bound on this distance, observe that for \(i\) such that \(r_i > \phi n^2\), we have \(E_i(x) = E_i(y)\). Thus,

\(\seteqnumber{0}{42.}{0}\)

\begin{align*} \dist {F(x) - F(y)}^2&=\sum _i \dist {E_i(x) - E_i(y)}^2 = \sum _{i, r_i < \phi n^2} \dist {E_i(x) - E_i(y)}^2\\ &=\sum _{i, \phi /n^2 < r_i < \phi n^2 } \dist {E_i(x) - E_i(y)}^2 + \sum _{i, r_i < \phi / n^2 } \dist {E_i(x) - E_i(y)}^2 \\ &=\beta \phi ^2 \lg \pth {n^4} + \sum _{i, r_i < \phi /n^2} (2r_i)^2 \beta \leq 4\beta \phi ^2 \lg n + \frac {4\phi ^2\beta }{n^4 } \leq 5\beta \phi ^2 \lg n. \end{align*} Thus, \(\dist {F(x) - F(y)} \leq \phi \sqrt {5 \beta \lg n}\). We conclude, that with high probability, \(F(\cdot )\) is an embedding of \(\FMS \) into Euclidean space with distortion \(\nfrac {\pth {\phi \sqrt {5 \beta \lg n}}}{\pth {\phi \cdot \frac {\sqrt {\beta }}{256 \ln n}}} = O( \log ^{3/2} n)\).

We still have to handle the infinite number of coordinates problem. However, the above proof shows that we care about a resolution \(r_i\) (i.e., it contributes to the estimates in the above proof) only if there is a pair \(x\) and \(y\) such that \(r_i/n^2 \leq \DistChar (x,y) \leq r_i n^2\). Thus, for every pair of distances there are \(O( \log n)\) relevant resolutions. Thus, there are at most \(\eta = O(n^2 \beta \log n) = O(n^2 \log ^2 n)\) relevant coordinates, and we can ignore all the other coordinates. Next, consider the affine subspace \(h\) that spans \(F(P)\). Clearly, it is \(n-1\) dimensional, and consider the projection \(G:\Re ^\eta \rightarrow \Re ^{n-1}\) that projects a point to its closest point in \(h\). Clearly, \(G(F(\cdot ))\) is an embedding with the same distortion for \(P\), and the target space is of dimension \(n-1\).

Note, that all this process succeeds with high probability. If it fails, we try again. We conclude:

42.1 Indeed, if \(f_{i,j}(x) < \DistChar _i(x, V_{i,j})\) and \(f_{i,j}(y) < \DistChar _i(x, V_{i,j})\) then \(f_{i,j}(x) = 2 r_i\) and \(f_{i,j}(y) = 2 r_i\), which implies the above inequality. If \(f_{i,j}(x) = \DistChar _i(x, V_{i,j})\) and \(f_{i,j}(y) = \DistChar _i(x, V_{i,j})\) then the inequality trivially holds. The other option is handled in a similar fashion.

  • Theorem 42.5.3 (Low quality Bourgain theorem). Given a \(n\)-point metric \(M\), one can embed it into Euclidean space of dimension \(n-1\), such that the distortion of the embedding is at most \(O(\log ^{3/2} n)\).

Using the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma, the dimension can be further reduced to \(O(\log n)\). Being more careful in the proof, it is possible to reduce the dimension to \(O(\log n)\) directly.

42.6 Bibliographical notes

The partitions we use are due to Calinescu et al. [ckr-aazep-04]. The idea of embedding into spanning trees is due to Alon et al. [akpw-gtgak-95], which showed that one can get a probabilistic distortion of \(2^{O \pth {\sqrt {\log n \log \log n}}}\). Yair Bartal realized that by allowing trees with additional vertices, one can get a considerably better result. In particular, he showed [b-pamsa-96] that probabilistic embedding into trees can be done with polylogarithmic average distortion. He later improved the distortion to \(O( \log n \log \log n)\) in [b-aamtm-98]. Improving this result was an open question, culminating in the work of Fakcharoenphol et al. [frt-tbaam-04] which achieve the optimal \(O(\log n)\) distortion.

Our proof of Lemma 42.3.1 (which is originally from [frt-tbaam-04]) is taken from [klmn-mdnem-05]. The proof of Theorem 42.5.3 is by Gupta [g-efm-00].

A good exposition of metric spaces is available in Matoušek [m-ldg-02].

Embedding into spanning trees.

The above embeds the graph into a Steiner tree. A more useful representation, would be a random embedding into a spanning tree. Surprisingly, this can be done, as shown by Emek et al. [eest-lsst-08]. This was improved to \(O( \log n \cdot \log \log n \cdot ( \log \log \log n)^3 )\)42.1 by Abraham et al. [abn-ntlss-08, abn-ntlsst-08-arxiv].

Alternative proof of the tree embedding result.

Interestingly, if one does not care about the optimal distortion, one can get similar result (for embedding into probabilistic trees), by first embedding the metric into Euclidean space, then reduce the dimension by the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma, and finally, construct an HST by constructing a quadtree over the points. The “trick” is to randomly translate the quadtree. It is easy to verify that this yields \(O( \log ^4 n)\) distortion. See the survey by Indyk [i-aaldg-01] for more details. This random shifting of quadtrees is a powerful technique that was used in getting several result, and it is a crucial ingredient in Arora [a-ptase-98] approximation algorithm for Euclidean TSP.
