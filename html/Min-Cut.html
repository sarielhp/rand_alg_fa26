17.1 Branching processes – Galton-Watson Process

17.1.1 The problem

In the 19th century, Victorians were worried that aristocratic surnames were disappearing, as family names passed on only through the male children. As such, a family with no male children had its family name disappear. So, imagine the number of male children of a person is an independent random variable \(X \in \brc {0,1,2,\ldots }\). Starting with a single person, its family (as far as male children are concerned) is a random tree with the degree of a node being distributed according to \(X\). We continue recursively in constructing this tree, again, sampling the number of children for each current leaf according to the distribution of \(X\). It is not hard to see that a family disappears if \(\Ex {X} \leq 1\), and it has a constant probability of surviving if \(\Ex {X} >1\).

Francis Galton asked the question of what is the probability of such a blue-blood family name to survive, and this question was answered by Henry William Watson [wg-pef-1875]. The Victorians were worried about strange things, see [g-wwr-1869] for a provocatively titled article from the period, and [s-wnrsf-12] for a more recent take on this issue.

Of course, since infant mortality is dramatically down (as is the number of aristocrat males dying to maintain the British empire), the probability of family names to disappear is now much lower than it was in the 19th century. Interestingly, countries with family names that were introduced long time ago have very few surnames (i.e., Korean have 250 surnames, and three surnames form 45% of the population). On the other hand, countries that introduced surnames more recently have dramatically more surnames (for example, the Dutch have surnames only for the last 200 years, and there are \(68,000\) different family names).

Here we are going to look on a very specific variant of this problem. Imagine that starting with a single male. A male has exactly two children, and one of them is a male with probability half (i.e., the \(Y\)-chromosome is being passed only to its male children). As such, the natural question is what is the probability that \(h\) generations down, there is a male decedent that all his ancestors are male (i.e., it caries the original family name, and the original \(Y\)-chromosome).

17.1.2 On coloring trees

Let \(T_h\) be a complete binary tree of height \(h\). We randomly color its edges by black and white. Namely, for each edge we independently choose its color to be either black or white, with equal probability (say, black indicates the child is male). We are interested in the event that there exists a path from the root of \(T_h\) to one of its leafs, that is all black. Let \(\Event _h\) denote this event, and let \(\rho _h = \Prob {\Event _h}\). Observe that \(\rho _0 =1\) and \(\rho _1 = 3/4\) (see below).

To bound this probability, consider the root \(u\) of \(T_h\) and its two children \(u_l\) and \(u_r\). The probability that there is a black path from \(u_l\) to one of its children is \(\rho _{h-1}\), and as such, the probability that there is a black path from \(u\) through \(u_l\) to a leaf of the subtree of \(u_l\) is \(\Prob {\text {the edge } \MakeSBig u {} u_l \text { is colored black}} \cdot \rho _{h-1} = \rho _{h-1}/2\). As such, the probability that there is no black path through \(u_l\) is \(1-\rho _{h-1}/2\). As such, the probability of not having a black path from \(u\) to a leaf (through either children) is \((1-\rho _{h-1}/2)^2\). In particular, there desired probability, is the complement; that is

\(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{0}\)

\begin{equation*} \rho _h=1 - \pth {1-\frac {\rho _{h-1}}{2}}^2 =\frac {\rho _{h-1}}{2} \pth {2-\frac {\rho _{h-1}}{2}} = \rho _{h-1} - \frac {\rho _{h-1}^2}{4} = f \bigl ( \rho _{h-1} \bigr ) \qquad \text { for } \qquad f(x) = x -x^2/4. \end{equation*}

The starting values are \(\rho _0 = 1\), and \(\rho _1 = 3/4\).

  • Lemma 17.1.1. We have that \(\rho _h \geq 1/(h+1)\).

  • Proof: (Feel free to skip reading.) The proof is by induction. For \(h=1\), we have \(\rho _1 = 3/4 \geq 1/(1+1)\).

    Observe that \(\rho _h = f\pth {\rho _{h-1} }\) for \(f(x) = x -x^2/4\), and \(f'(x) = 1-x/2\). As such, \(f'(x) > 0\) for \(x\in [0,1]\) and \(f(x)\) is increasing in the range \([0,1]\). As such, by induction, we have that

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} \ds \rho _h = f\pth []{ \rho _{h-1}} \geq f\pth []{ \frac {1}{(h-1)+1}} = \frac {1}{h} - \frac {1}{4 h^2}. \end{equation*}

    We need to prove that \(\rho _h \geq 1/(h+1)\), which is implied by the above if

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} \frac {1}{h} - \frac {1}{4 h^2} \geq \frac {1}{h+1} \;\;\;\Leftrightarrow \;\;\; 4h(h+1) - (h+1) \geq 4h^2 \;\;\;\Leftrightarrow \;\;\; 4h^2 +4h - h- 1 \geq 4h^2 \;\;\;\Leftrightarrow \;\;\; 3h \geq 1, \end{equation*}

    which trivially holds.

  • Lemma 17.1.2. We have that \(\rho _h =O( 1/h)\).

  • Proof: (Feel free to skip reading.) We prove the claim for infinite number of values of \(h\) – the claim then follows for all \(h\) by fiddling with the constants. The claim trivially holds for small values of \(h\). For any \(j > 0\), let \(h_j\) be the minimal index such that \(\rho _{h_j} \leq 1/2^j\). It is easy to verify that \(\rho _{h_j} \geq 1/2^{j+1}\). We claim (mysteriously) that

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} \ds h_{j+1} - h_j\leq \frac {\rho _{h_{j}} - \rho _{h_{j+1}}} { (\rho _{h_{j+1}} )^2/4 }. \end{equation*}

    Indeed, \(\rho _{k+1}\) is the number resulting from removing \(\rho _k^2/4\) from \(\rho _k\). Namely, the sequence \(\rho _1, \rho _2, \ldots \) is a monotonically decreasing sequence of numbers in the interval \([0,1]\), where the gaps between consecutive numbers decreases. In particular, to get from \(\rho _{h_{j}}\) to \(\rho _{h_{j+1}}\), the gaps used were of size at least \(\Delta = \bigl (\rho _{h_{j+1}} \bigr )^2\), which means that there are at least \((\rho _{h_{j}} - \rho _{h_{j+1}}) / \Delta - 1\) numbers in the series between these two elements. As such, since \(\rho _{h_{j}} \leq 1/2^{j}\) and \(\rho _{h_{j+1}} \geq 1/2^{j+2}\), we have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} h_{j+1} - h_j\leq \frac {\rho _{h_{j}} - \rho _{h_{j+1}}} { ( \rho _{h_{j+1}} )^2/4 }\leq \frac {1/2^{j} - 1/2^{j+2}} { 1/2^{2(j+2)+2}}\leq 2^{2j + 6} /2^{j} =2^{j+6}. \end{equation*}

    This implies that \(h_j \leq (h_j - h_{j-1}) + (h_{j-1} - h_{j-2}) + \cdots + (h_1 - h_0) \leq 2^{j+6}.\)As such, we have \(\rho _{h_j} \leq 1/2^{j} \leq 2^6/2^{j+6} \leq 2^6/h_j\), which implies the claim.

17.2 Min Cut

17.2.1 Problem Definition

Let \(\Graph =(\Vertices , \Edges )\) be an undirected graph with \(n\) vertices and \(m\) edges. We are interested in cuts in \(\Graph \).

  • Definition 17.2.1. A cut in \(\Graph \) is a partition of the vertices of \(\Vertices \) into two sets \(S\) and \(\Vertices \setminus S\), where the edges of the cut are

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation*} (S, \Vertices \setminus S)=\brc { uv \sep { u \in S, v \in \Vertices \setminus S, \text { and } uv \in E}}, \end{equation*}

    where \(S \neq \emptyset \) and \(\Vertices \setminus S \ne \emptyset \). We will refer to the number of edges in the cut \((S, \Vertices \setminus S)\) as the size of the cut. For an example of a cut, see Figure 17.2.

We are interested in the problem of computing the minimum cut (i.e., mincut), that is, the cut in the graph with minimum cardinality. Specifically, we would like to find the set \(S\subseteq \Vertices \) such that \((S, \Vertices \setminus S)\) is as small as possible, and \(S\) is neither empty nor \(\Vertices \setminus S\) is empty.

17.2.2 Some Definitions

We remind the reader of the following concepts. The conditional probability of \(X\) given \(Y\) is \(\Prob {X=x\sep { Y=y}} = \nfrac {\Prob {(X=x)\cap (Y=y)}}{\Prob {Y=y}}\). An equivalent, useful restatement of this is that

\(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{0}\)

\begin{equation} \Prob {\bigl . (X=x)\cap (Y=y)} = \Prob {X=x\sep { Y=y \MakeSBig }} \cdot \Prob {Y=y}.\eqlab {conditional:probability} \end{equation}

The following is easy to prove by induction using Eq. (17.1).

  • Lemma 17.2.2. Let \(\Event _{1},\ldots ,\Event _{n}\) be \(n\) events which are not necessarily independent. Then,

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{1}\)

    \begin{align*} \Prob {\bigl . \cap _{i=1}^{n}\Event _{i}} =\Prob {\bigl . \Event _{1}} * \Prob {\Event _{2}\sep { \Event _{1}}} * \Prob {\Event _{3}\sep {\Event _{1}\cap \Event _{2}}} * \ldots * \Prob {\Event _{n} \sep { \Event _{1}\cap \ldots \cap \Event _{n-1}}}. \end{align*}

17.3 The Algorithm

The basic operation used by the algorithm is edge contraction, depicted in Figure 17.3. We take an edge \(e=x y\) in \(\Graph \) and merge the two vertices into a single vertex. The new resulting graph is denoted by \(\Graph / x y\). Note, that we remove self loops created by the contraction. However, since the resulting graph is no longer a regular graph, it has parallel edges – namely, it is a multi-graph. We represent a multi-graph, as a regular graph with multiplicities on the edges. See Figure 17.4.

The edge contraction operation can be implemented in \(O(n)\) time for a graph with \(n\) vertices. This is done by merging the adjacency lists of the two vertices being contracted, and then using hashing to do the fix-ups (i.e., we need to fix the adjacency list of the vertices that are connected to the two vertices).

Note, that the cut is now computed counting multiplicities (i.e., if \(e\) is in the cut and it has weight \(w\), then the contribution of \(e\) to the cut weight is \(w\)).

  • Observation 17.3.1. A set of vertices in \(\Graph /x y\) corresponds to a set of vertices in the graph \(\Graph \). Thus a cut in \(\Graph /x y\) always corresponds to a valid cut in \(\Graph \). However, there are cuts in \(\Graph \) that do not exist in \(\Graph / x y\). For example, the cut \(S = \brc {x}\), does not exist in \(\Graph / x y\). As such, the size of the minimum cut in \(\Graph /x y\) is at least as large as the minimum cut in \(\Graph \) (as long as \(\Graph /x y\) has at least one edge). Since any cut in \(\Graph /x y\) has a corresponding cut of the same cardinality in \(\Graph \).

Our algorithm works by repeatedly performing edge contractions. This is beneficial as this shrinks the underlying graph, and we would compute the cut in the resulting (smaller) graph. An “extreme” example of this, is shown in Figure 17.5, where we contract the graph into a single edge, which (in turn) corresponds to a cut in the original graph. (It might help the reader to think about each vertex in the contracted graph, as corresponding to a connected component in the original graph.)

Figure 17.5 also demonstrates the problem with taking this approach. Indeed, the resulting cut is not the minimum cut in the graph.

So, why did the algorithm fail to find the minimum cut in this case?17.1 The failure occurs because of the contraction at Figure 17.5 (e), as we had contracted an edge in the minimum cut. In the new graph, depicted in Figure 17.5 (f), there is no longer a cut of size 3, and all cuts are of size 4 or more. Specifically, the algorithm succeeds only if it does not contract an edge in the minimum cut.

17.1 Naturally, if the algorithm had succeeded in finding the minimum cut, this would have been our success.

  • Observation 17.3.2. Let \(e_{1},\ldots ,e_{n-2}\) be a sequence of edges in \(\Graph \), such that none of them is in the minimum cut, and such that \(\Graph '=G/\brc { e_{1},\ldots ,e_{n-2}} \) is a single multi-edge. Then, this multi-edge corresponds to a minimum cut in \(\Graph \).

Note, that the claim in the above observation is only in one direction. We might be able to still compute a minimum cut, even if we contract an edge in a minimum cut, the reason being that a minimum cut is not unique. In particular, another minimum cut might survived the sequence of contractions that destroyed other minimum cuts.

Using Observation 17.3.2 in an algorithm is problematic, since the argumentation is circular, how can we find a sequence of edges that are not in the cut without knowing what the cut is? The way to slice the Gordian knot here, is to randomly select an edge at each stage, and contract this random edge.

See Figure 17.6 for the resulting algorithm MinCut.

17.3.1 Analysis
17.3.1.1 The probability of success

Naturally, if we are extremely lucky, the algorithm would never pick an edge in the mincut, and the algorithm would succeed. The ultimate question here is what is the probability of success. If it is relatively “large” then this algorithm is useful since we can run it several times, and return the best result computed. If on the other hand, this probability is tiny, then we are working in vain since this approach would not work.

  • Lemma 17.3.3. If a graph \(\Graph \) has a minimum cut of size \(k\) and \(\Graph \) has \(n\) vertices, then \(|E(G)|\geq \frac {kn}{2}\).

  • Proof: Each vertex degree is at least \(k\), otherwise the vertex itself would form a minimum cut of size smaller than \(k\). As such, there are at least \(\sum _{v \in \Vertices } \mathrm {degree(v)} / 2 \geq n k /2\) edges in the graph.

  • Lemma 17.3.4. Fix a specific minimum cut \(C = (S,\overline {S})\) in the graph. If we pick in random an edge \(e\) from a graph \(\Graph \), uniformly at random, then with probability at most \(\nfrac {2}{n}\) it belongs to the minimum cut \(C\).

  • Proof: There are at least \(n k/2\) edges in the graph and exactly \(k\) edges in the minimum cut. Thus, the probability of picking an edge from the minimum cut is smaller then \(k/(n k/2)=2/n.\)

The following lemma shows (surprisingly) that MinCut succeeds with reasonable probability.

  • Lemma 17.3.5. MinCut outputs the mincut with probability \(\displaystyle \geq \frac {2}{n ( n-1 ) }\).

7

  • Proof: Let \(\Event _{i}\) be the event that \(e_{i}\) is not in the minimum cut of \(\Graph _{i}\). By Observation 17.3.2, MinCut outputs the minimum cut if the events \(\Event _{0},\ldots ,\Event _{n-3}\) all happen (namely, all edges picked are outside the minimum cut).

    By Lemma 17.3.4, it holds \(\displaystyle \Prob {\Event _{i}\sep { \Event _{0} \cap \Event _{1}\cap \ldots \cap \Event _{i-1}}} \geq 1-\frac {2}{\cardin {\Vertices (G_{i})}} = 1-\frac {2}{n-i}.\)Implying that

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{1}\)

    \begin{align*} \Delta &=\Prob {\Event _{0}\cap \ldots \cap \Event _{n-3}}=\Prob {\Event _{0}} \cdot \Prob {\Event _{1}\sep {\Event _{0}}} \cdot \Prob {\Event _{2}\sep {\Event _{0}\cap \Event _{1}}} \cdot \ldots \cdot \Prob {\Event _{n-3}\sep { \Event _{0}\cap \ldots \cap \Event _{n-4}}}. \end{align*} As such, we have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{1}\)

    \begin{align*} \Delta &\geq \prod _{i=0}^{n-3}\pth {1-\frac {2}{n-i}}=\prod _{i=0}^{n-3}\frac {n-i-2}{n-i}\\&=\frac {\CCancel [green]{n-2}}{n} * \frac {\Ccancel {n-3}}{n-1} * \frac {\Ccancel [orange]{n-4}}{\Ccancel [green]{n-2}}* \frac {\bcancel {n-5}}{\cancel {n-3}} * \frac {\Ccancel [red]{n-6}}{\Ccancel [orange]{n-4}} * \cdots * \frac {\Ccancel [purple]{3}}{\Ccancel [yellow]{5}} * \frac {2}{\CCancel [brown]{4}} * \frac {1}{\CCancel [purple]{3}}\\& =\frac {2}{n (n-1)}. \end{align*}

17.3.1.2 Running time analysis.

  • Observation 17.3.6. MinCut runs in \(O( n^{2} )\) time.

  • Observation 17.3.7. The algorithm always outputs a cut, and the cut is not smaller than the minimum cut.

  • Definition 17.3.8. (informal) Amplification is the process of running an experiment again and again till the things we want to happen, with good probability, do happen.

Let MinCutRep be the algorithm that runs MinCut \(n(n-1)\) times and return the minimum cut computed in all those independent executions of MinCut.

  • Lemma 17.3.9. The probability that MinCutRep fails to return the minimum cut is \(< 0.14\).

  • Proof: The probability of failure of MinCut to output the mincut in each execution is at most \(1-\frac {2}{n( n-1)}\), by Lemma 17.3.5. Now, MinCutRep fails, only if all the \(n (n-1)\) executions of MinCut fail. But these executions are independent, as such, the probability to this happen is at most

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{1}\)

    \begin{align*} \pth {1-\frac {2}{n (n-1)}}^{n(n-1)}\le \exp \pth {-\frac {2}{n(n-1)}\cdot n(n-1)}=\exp (-2)<0.14, \end{align*} since \(1-x\leq e^{-x}\) for \(0\leq x\leq 1\).

  • Theorem 17.3.10. One can compute the minimum cut in \(O(n^{4})\) time with constant probability to get a correct result. In \(O\pth {n^{4}\log n}\) time the minimum cut is returned with high probability.

17.3.2 An alternative implementation using MST

The algorithm. The above algorithm can be restated as follows. Randomly assign weights to the edges of \(\G \) (say, by picking numbers in \([0,1]\)). Next, compute the MST \(\Tree \) of the graph according to these weights. Remove the heaviest edge in the MST. The resulting partition of \(\Tree \) into two trees, corresponds to a cut in the original graph. Return this cut as a candidate to be the minimum cut.

The analysis. To see that this algorithm is equivalent to MinCut (Figure 17.6), observe that the contraction algorithm simulates Kruskal’s MST algorithm when run on randomly weighted edges. First, imagine implementing MinCut so that it keeps parallel edges. Then, the edges connecting two vertices that are not contracted are exactly the edges between the two connected components. Picking a random edge to contract, is equivalent to picking the edge with the minimum random weight. Thus, the MST algorithm here just simulates MinCut (or vice versa).

A small optimization. It is possible to compute the heaviest edge in the MST, and the partition it induces in (deterministic) linear time – it is a nice example of the search and prune technique.

  • Exercise 17.3.11. Given a graph \(\G \) with weights on the edges, show how to compute the maximum weight edge in the MST of \(\G \) in \(O(n + m)\) time, where \(n\) are \(m\) are the number of vertices and edges of \(\G \), respectively.

Thus, this yields a \(O(n + m)\) implementation of MinCut. We get the following result.

  • Lemma 17.3.12. MinCut can implemented to run in \(O(n + m)\) time, and it outputs the mincut with probability \(\displaystyle \geq \frac {2}{n ( n-1 ) }\).

17.4 A faster algorithm

The algorithm presented in the previous section is extremely simple. Which raises the question of whether we can get a faster algorithm17.1?

So, why MinCutRep needs so many executions? Well, the probability of success in the first \(\nu \) iterations is

\(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{1}\)

\begin{align} \Prob {\Event _{0}\cap \ldots \cap \Event _{\nu -1}} &\geq \prod _{i=0}^{\nu -1}\pth {1-\frac {2}{n-i}} = \prod _{i=0}^{\nu -1}\frac {n-i-2}{n-i} \nonumber \\ &= \frac {n-2}{n}\ *\ \frac {n-3}{n-1}\ *\ \frac {n-4}{n-2}\ldots = \frac {(n-\nu )(n-\nu -1)}{n\cdot (n-1)}. \eqlab {first} \end{align} Namely, this probability deteriorates very quickly toward the end of the execution, when the graph becomes small enough. (To see this, observe that for \(\nu =n/2\), the probability of success is roughly \(1/4\), but for \(\nu =n-\sqrt {n}\) the probability of success is roughly \(1/n\).)

So, the key observation is that as the graph get smaller the probability to make a bad choice increases. So, instead of doing the amplification from the outside of the algorithm, we will run the new algorithm more times when the graph is smaller. Namely, we put the amplification directly into the algorithm.

The basic new operation we use is Contract, depicted in Figure 17.7, which also depict the new algorithm FastCut.

17.1 This would require a more involved algorithm, that is life.

  • Lemma 17.4.1. The running time of FastCut\((G)\) is \(O\pth {n^{2}\log n}\), where \(n=\cardin {V(G)}\).

  • Proof: Well, we perform two calls to Contract\((G,t)\) which takes \(O(n^{2})\) time. And then we perform two recursive calls on the resulting graphs. We have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{2}\)

    \begin{equation*} T(n)=O(n^{2}) + 2 T\bigl ( n/ \sqrt {2} \ts \bigr ). \end{equation*}

    The solution to this recurrence is \(O\pth {n^{2}\log n}\) as one can easily (and should) verify.

  • Lemma 17.4.3. The probability that Contract\(\bigl ( \Graph , n/\sqrt {2} \ts \bigr )\) had not contracted the minimum cut is at least \(1/2\).

    Namely, the probability that the minimum cut in the contracted graph is still a minimum cut in the original graph is at least \(1/2\).

  • Proof: Just plug in \(\nu = n -t = n - \ceil {1 + n/\sqrt {2} }\) into Eq. (17.2). We have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{17.}{2}\)

    \begin{align*} \Prob {\Bigl . \Event _{0}\cap \ldots \cap \Event _{n-t}}\geq & \frac {t(t-1)}{n\cdot (n-1)} = \frac {\ceil {1 + n/\sqrt {2} } \pth { \ceil {1 + n/\sqrt {2} } - 1}}{n(n-1)} \geq \frac {1}{2}. \end{align*}

The following lemma bounds the probability of success.

  • Lemma 17.4.4. FastCut finds the minimum cut with probability larger than \(\Omega \pth []{1/\log n}\).

  • Proof: Let \(T_h\) be the recursion tree of the algorithm of depth \(h = \Theta ( \log n)\). Color an edge of recursion tree by black if the contraction succeeded. Clearly, the algorithm succeeds if there is a path from the root to a leaf that is all black. This is exactly the settings of Lemma 17.1.1, and we conclude that the probability of success is at least \(1/(h+1) = \Theta (1/\log n)\), as desired.

  • Exercise 17.4.5. Prove, that running FastCut repeatedly \(c\cdot \log ^{2}n\) times, guarantee that the algorithm outputs the minimum cut with probability \(\geq 1-1/n^{2}\), say, for \(c\) a constant large enough.

  • Theorem 17.4.6. One can compute the minimum cut in a graph \(\Graph \) with \(n\) vertices in \(O(n^2 \log ^3 n)\) time. The algorithm succeeds with probability \(\geq 1-1/n^2\).

  • Proof: We do amplification on FastCut by running it \(O(\log ^2 n)\) times. The running time bound follows from Lemma 17.4.1. The bound on the probability follows from Lemma 17.4.4, and using the amplification analysis as done in Lemma 17.3.9 for MinCutRep.
