33.1 Random walks on graphs

Let \(\Graph = (\Vertices ,\Edges )\) be a connected, non-bipartite, undirected graph, with \(n\) vertices. We define the natural Markov chain on \(\Graph \), where the transition probability is

\(\seteqnumber{0}{33.}{0}\)

\begin{align*} \MCPrbY {u}{v} = \begin{cases} \frac {1}{d(u)} & \text {if } uv \in \Edges \\ 0 & \text {otherwise}, \end {cases} \end{align*} where \(d(w)\) is the degree of vertex \(w\). Clearly, the resulting Markov chain \(M_G\) is irreducible. Note, that the graph must have an odd cycle, and it has a cycle of length 2. Thus, the \(\gcd \) of the lengths of its cycles is \(1\). Namely, \(M_G\) is aperiodic. Now, by the Fundamental theorem of Markov chains, \(M_G\) has a unique stationary distribution \(\pi \).

  • Lemma 33.1.1. For all \(v \in \Vertices \), we have \(\pi _v = d(v) / 2m\).

  • Proof: Since \(\pi \) is stationary, and the definition of \(\MCPrbY {u}{v}\), we get

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{33.}{0}\)

    \begin{align*} \pi _v = \pbrc []{\pi \MCPrbM }_v = \sum _{uv} \pi _u \MCPrbY {u}{v}, \end{align*} and this holds for all \(v\). We only need to verify the claimed solution, since there is a unique stationary distribution. Indeed,

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{33.}{0}\)

    \begin{align*} \frac {d(v)}{2m} = \pi _v = [\pi \MCPrbM ]_v = \sum _{uv} \frac {d(u)}{2m} \frac {1}{d(u)} = \frac {d(v)}{2m}, \end{align*} as claimed.

  • Definition 33.1.2. The hitting time \(\MCExSteps {u}{v}\) is the expected number of steps in a random walk that starts at \(u\) and ends upon first reaching \(v\).

    The commute time between \(u\) and \(v\) is denoted by \(\CommuteTime {u}{v} = \MCExSteps {u}{v} + \MCExSteps {v}{u}\).

    Let \(\CoverTime _u(G)\) denote the expected length of a walk that starts at \(u\) and ends upon visiting every vertex in \(\Graph \) at least once. The cover time of \(\Graph \) denotes by \(\CoverTime (G)\) is defined by \(\CoverTime (G) = \max _u \CoverTime _u(G)\).

  • Lemma 33.1.3. For all \(v \in \Vertices \), we have \(\MCExSteps {v}{v} = 1/\pi _v = 2m/d(v)\).

  • Example 33.1.4 (Lollipop). Let \(L_{2n}\) be the \(2n\)-vertex lollipop graph, this graph consists of a clique on \(n\) vertices, and a path on the remaining \(n\) vertices. There is a vertex \(u\) in the clique which is where the path is attached to it. Let \(v\) denote the end of the path, see figure on the right.

    Taking a random walk from \(u\) to \(v\) requires in expectation \(O(n^2)\) steps, as we already saw in class. This ignores the probability of escape – that is, with probability \((n-1)/n\) when at \(u\) we enter the clique \(K_{n}\) (instead of the path). As such, it turns out that \(\MCExSteps {u}{v} = \Theta (n^3)\), and \(\MCExSteps {v}{u} = \Theta (n^2)\). (Thus, hitting times are not symmetric!)

    Note, that the cover time is not monotone decreasing with the number of edges. Indeed, the path of length \(n\), has cover time \(O(n^2)\), but the larger graph \(L_n\) has cover time \(\Omega (n^3)\).

 

  • Example 33.1.5 (More on walking on the Lollipop). To see why \(\MCExSteps {u}{v} = \Theta (n^3)\), number the vertices on the stem \(x_1, \ldots , x_n\). Let \(T_i\) be the expected time to arrive to the vertex \(x_i\) when starting a walk from \(u\). Observe, that surprisingly, \(T_1 = \Theta ( n^2 )\). Indeed, the walk has to visit the vertex \(u\) about \(n\) times in expectation, till the walk would decide to go to \(x_1\) instead of falling back into the clique. The time between visits to \(u\) is in expectation \(O(n)\) (assuming the walk is inside the clique).

    Now, observe that \(T_{2i} = T_i + \Theta (i^2) + \frac {1}{2} T_{2i}\). Indeed, starting with \(x_i\), it takes in expectation \(\Theta (i^2)\) steps of the walk to either arrive (with equal probability) at \(x_{2i}\) (good), or to get back to \(u\) (oopsi). In the later case, the game begins from scratch. As such, we have that

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{33.}{0}\)

    \begin{align*} T_{2i}=2T_i + \Theta \pth { i^2}=2\pth []{\Big . 2T_{i/2} + \Theta \pth { (i/2)^2}}+ \Theta \pth { i^2}=\cdots = 2^{1 + \log _2 i}T_1 + \Theta \pth { i^2 }, \end{align*} assuming \(i\) is a power of two (why not?). As such, \(T_n = nT_1 + \Theta (n^2 )\). Since \(T_1 = \Theta (n^2)\), we have that \(T_n = \Theta (n^3)\).

  • Definition 33.1.6. A \(n \times n\) matrix \(\Matrix \) is stochastic if all its entries are non-negative and for each row \(i\), it holds \(\sum _{k} \Matrix _{ik} =1\). It is doubly stochastic if in addition, for any \(i\), it holds \(\sum _{k} \Matrix _{ki} =1\).

  • Lemma 33.1.7. Let \(\MC \) be a Markov chain, such that transition probability matrix \(\MCPrbM \) is doubly stochastic. Then, the distribution \(u = (1/n, 1/n,\ldots , 1/n)\) is stationary for \(\MC \).

  • Proof: \(\displaystyle [u \MCPrbM ]_i = \sum _{k=1}^n \frac {\MCPrbY {k}{i}}{n} = \frac {1}{n}\).

We can interpret every edge in \(\G \) as corresponding to two directed edges. In particular, imagine performing a random walk in \(\G \), but remembering not only the current vertex in the walk, but also the (directed) edge used the walk to arrive to this vertex. One can interpret this as a random walk on the (directed) edges. Observe, that there are \(2m\) directed edges. Furthermore, a vertex \(u\) of degree \(d(u)\), has stationary distribution \(\pi _u = d(u)/2m\). As such, the probability that the random walk would use any of the \(d(u)\) outgoing edges from \(u\) is exactly \(\alpha = \pi _u / d(u) = 1/2m\). Namely, if we interpret the walk on the graph as walk on the directed edges, the stationary distribution is uniform. This readily implies that if \(\dirEdge {u}{v}\) is in the graph, then \(h_{\dirEdge {u}{v}\dirEdge {u}{v}}\) is \(1/\alpha = 2m\). This readily implies that the expected time to go from \(u\) to \(v\) and back to \(u\) is at most \(2m\). Next, we provide a more formal (and somewhat different) proof of this.

  • Lemma 33.1.8. For any edge \(\dirEdge {u}{v} \in \Edges \), we have \(\MCExSteps {u}{v} + \MCExSteps {v}{u} \leq 2m\).

(Note, that \(\dirEdge {u}{v}\) being an edge in the graph is crucial. Indeed, without it a significantly worst case bound holds, see Theorem 33.2.1.)

  • Proof: Consider a new Markov chain defined by the edges of the graph (where every edge is taken twice as two directed edges), where the current state is the last (directed) edge visited. There are \(2m\) edges in the new Markov chain, and the new transition matrix, has \(Q_{\dirEdge {u}{v}, \dirEdge {v}{w} } = \MCPrbY {v}{w} = \frac {1}{d(v)}\). This matrix is doubly stochastic, meaning that not only do the rows sum to one, but the columns sum to one as well. Indeed, for an edge \(\dirEdge {v}{w}\) we have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{33.}{0}\)

    \begin{align*} \sum _{x \in V, y \in \Nbr (x)} Q_{\dirEdge {x}{y}, \dirEdge {v}{w}} = \sum _{u \in \Nbr (v)} Q_{\dirEdge {u}{v}, \dirEdge {v}{w}} = \sum _{u \in \Nbr (v)} \MCPrbY {v}{w} = d(v) \times \frac {1}{d(v)} = 1. \end{align*} Thus, the stationary distribution for this Markov chain is uniform, by Lemma 33.1.7. Namely, the stationary distribution of \(e =\dirEdge {u}{v}\) is \(\MCExSteps {e}{e} = \pi _e = 1/(2m)\). Thus, the expected time between successive traversals of \(e\) is \(1/\pi _e = 2m\), by Theorem 32.4.11 (iii).

    Consider \(\MCExSteps {u}{v} + \MCExSteps {v}{u}\) and interpret this as the time to go from \(u\) to \(v\) and then return to \(u\). Conditioned on the event that the initial entry into \(u\) was via the edge \(\dirEdge {v}{u}\), we conclude that the expected time to go from there to \(v\) and then finally use \(\dirEdge {v}{u}\) is \(2m\). The memorylessness property of a Markov chains now allows us to remove the conditioning: since how we arrived to \(u\) is not relevant. Thus, the expected time to travel from \(u\) to \(v\) and back is at most \(2m\).

33.2 Electrical networks and random walks

A resistive electrical network is an undirected graph. Each edge has branch resistance associated with it. The electrical flow is determined by two laws: Kirchhoff’s law (preservation of flow - all the flow coming into a node, leaves it) and Ohm’s law (the voltage across a resistor equals the product of the resistance times the current through it). Explicitly, Ohm’s law states

\(\seteqnumber{0}{33.}{0}\)

\begin{align*} \mathrm {voltage} = \mathrm {resistance} * \mathrm {current}. \end{align*}

The effective resistance between nodes \(u\) and \(v\) is the voltage difference between \(u\) and \(v\) when one ampere is injected into \(u\) and removed from \(v\) (or injected into \(v\) and removed from \(u\)). The effective resistance is always bounded by the branch resistance, but it can be much lower.

Given an undirected graph \(\Graph \), let \(\NetworkE (G)\) be the electrical network defined over \(\Graph \), associating one ohm resistance on the edges of \(\NetworkE (G)\).

You might now see the connection between a random walk on a graph and electrical network. Intuitively (used in the most unscientific way possible), the electricity, is made out of electrons each one of them is doing a random walk on the electric network. The resistance of an edge, corresponds to the probability of taking the edge. The higher the resistance, the lower the probability that we will travel on this edge. Thus, if the effective resistance \(\EffectResist {u}{v}\) between \(u\) and \(v\) is low, then there is a good probability that travel from \(u\) to \(v\) in a random walk, and \(\MCExSteps {u}{v}\) would be small.

33.2.1 A tangent on parallel and series resistors

Consider having \(n\) resistors in parallel with resistance \(R_1, \ldots , R_n\), connecting two nodes \(u\) and \(v\). As follows:

The effective resistance between \(u\) and \(v\) is

\(\seteqnumber{0}{33.}{0}\)

\begin{equation*} R_{uv} = \frac {1}{ \frac {1}{R_1} + \frac {1}{R_2} \cdots + \frac {1}{R_n}}. \end{equation*}

In particular, if \(R_1 = \cdots =R_n = R\), then we have that \(R_{uv} = 1/(1/R + \cdots 1/R) = 1/(n/R) = R/n\).

Similarly, if we have \(n\) resistors in series, with resistance \(R_1, R_2, \ldots , R_n\), between \(u\) and \(v\):

Then, the effective resistance between \(u\) and \(v\) is

\(\seteqnumber{0}{33.}{0}\)

\begin{equation*} R_{uv} = R_1 + \cdots + R_n. \end{equation*}

In particular, if \(R_1= \cdots = R_n\), then \(R_{uv} = n R\).

33.2.2 Back to random walks

  • Theorem 33.2.1. For any two vertices \(u\) and \(v\) in \(\Graph \), the commute time \(\CommuteTime {u}{v} = 2 m \EffectResist {u}{v}\), where \(\EffectResist {u}{v}\) is the effective resistance between \(u\) and \(v\).

  • Proof: Let \(\phi _{uv}\) denote the voltage at \(u\) in \(\NetworkE (G)\) with respect to \(v\), where \(d(x)\) amperes of current are injected into each node \(x \in \Vertices \), and \(2m\) amperes are removed from \(v\). We claim that

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{33.}{0}\)

    \begin{align*} \MCExSteps {u}{v} = \phi _{uv}. \end{align*} Note, that the voltage on an edge \(x y\) is \(\phi _{xy} = \phi _{xv} - \phi _{yv}\). Thus, using Kirchhoff’s Law and Ohm’s Law, we obtain that

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{33.}{0}\)

    \begin{equation} x \in V \setminus \brc {v}\;\;\;\;\;d(x) = \sum _{w \in \Nbr (x)} \mathrm {current}(x w) = \sum _{w \in \Nbr (x)} \frac {\phi _{x w}}{\mathrm {resistance}(x w)} = \sum _{w \in \Nbr (x)} \pth { \phi _{x v} - \phi _{wv}},\eqlab {voltage} \end{equation}

    since the resistance of every edge is \(1\) ohm. (We also have the “trivial” equality that \(\phi _{vv}=0\).) Furthermore, we have only \(n\) variables in this system; that is, for every \(x \in \Vertices \), we have the variable \(\phi _{x v}\).

    Now, for the random walk interpretation – by the definition of expectation, we have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{33.}{1}\)

    \begin{align*} x \in V \setminus \brc {v} \;\;\;\;\; &\MCExSteps {x}{v} = \frac {1}{d(x)}\sum _{w \in \Nbr (x)} \pth {1 + \MCExSteps {w}{v}}\;\;\;\; \Longleftrightarrow \;\;\;\;d(x)\, \MCExSteps {x}{v} = \sum _{w \in \Nbr (x)} 1 + \sum _{w \in \Nbr (x)}\MCExSteps {w}{v}\\ &\Longleftrightarrow \;\;\;\; \sum _{w \in \Nbr (x)} 1 = d(x)\, \MCExSteps {x}{v} - \sum _{w \in \Nbr (x)}\MCExSteps {w}{v}= \sum _{w \in \Nbr (x)} \pth { \MCExSteps {x}{v} - \MCExSteps {w}{v}}. \end{align*} Since \(d(x) = \sum _{w \in \Nbr (x)} 1\), this is equivalent to

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{33.}{1}\)

    \begin{align} x \in V \setminus \brc {v} \;\;\;\;\; d(x) = \sum _{w \in \Nbr (x)} \pth { \MCExSteps {x}{v} - \MCExSteps {w}{v}}. \eqlab {hitting:time} \end{align} Again, we also have the trivial equality \(\MCExSteps {v}{v}=0\).33.1 Note, that this system also has \(n\) equalities and \(n\) variables.

    Eq. (33.1) and Eq. (33.2) show two systems of linear equalities. Furthermore, if we identify \(\MCExSteps {u}{v}\) with \(\phi _{xv}\) then they are exactly the same system of equalities. Furthermore, since Eq. (33.1) represents a physical system, we know that it has a unique solution. This implies that \(\phi _{x v} = \MCExSteps {x}{v}\), for all \(x \in \Vertices \).

    Imagine the network where \(u\) is injected with \(2m\) amperes, and for all nodes \(w\) remove \(d(w)\) units from \(w\). In this new network, \(\MCExSteps {v}{u} = - \phi _{vu}' = \phi _{uv}'\). Now, since flows behaves linearly, we can superimpose them (i.e., add them up). We have that in this new network \(2m\) unites are being injected at \(u\), and \(2m\) units are being extracted at \(v\), all other nodes the charge cancel itself out. The voltage difference between \(u\) and \(v\) in the new network is \(\widehat {\phi } = \phi _{uv} + \phi _{uv}' = \MCExSteps {u}{v} + \MCExSteps {v}{u} = \CommuteTime {u}{v}\). Now, in the new network there are \(2m\) amperes going from \(u\) to \(v\), and by Ohm’s law, we have

    \(\seteqnumber{0}{33.}{2}\)

    \begin{align*} \widehat {\phi } = \mathrm {voltage} = \mathrm {resistance} * \mathrm {current} = 2m \EffectResist {u}{v}, \end{align*} as claimed.

33.1 In previous lectures, we interpreted \(\MCExSteps {v}{v}\) as the expected length of a walk starting at \(v\) and coming back to \(v\).

  • Example 33.2.2. Recall the lollipop \(L_n\) from Exercise 33.1.4, see Figure 33.1. Let \(u\) be the connecting vertex between the clique and the stem (i.e., the path). The effective resistance between \(u\) and \(v\) is \(n\) since there are \(n\) resistors in series along the stem. That is \(R_{uv} = n\).

    The number of edges in the lollipop is \(\binom {n}{2} + n = n(n-1)/2 + n = n(n+1)/2\). As such, the commute time \(\MCExSteps {v}{u} + \MCExSteps {u}{v}= \CommuteTime {u}{v} = 2 m \EffectResist {u}{v} = 2\bigl (n(n+1)/2\bigr ) n = n^2(n+1)\).

    We already know that \(\MCExSteps {v}{u}=\Theta (n^2)\). This implies that \(\MCExSteps {u}{v} = \CommuteTime {u}{v} - \MCExSteps {v}{u} = \Theta (n^3)\).

  • Lemma 33.2.3. For any \(n\) vertex connected graph \(\Graph \), and for all \(u,v \in V(G)\), we have \(\CommuteTime {u}{v} < n^3\).

  • Proof: The effective resistance between any two nodes in the network is bounded by the length of the shortest path between the two nodes, which is at most \(n-1\). As such, plugging this into Theorem 33.2.1, yields the bound, since \(m<n^2\).
