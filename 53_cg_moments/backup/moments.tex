\section{Vertical decomposition}
\seclab{vertical:decomposition}


\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \IncludeGraphics{\File{figs/arrangement}}
    \caption{The arrangement of a set of segments in the plane.}
    \figlab{arrangement}
\end{figure}

Given a set $\SegSet$ of $n$ segments in the plane, its
\emphOnly{arrangement}\index{arrangement}, denoted by $\ArrX{\bigl.\SegSet}$, is the
decomposition of the plane into faces, edges, and vertices. The
\emphOnly{vertices}\index{vertices} of $\ArrX{\bigl.\SegSet}$ are the endpoints, or the
intersection points of the segments of $\SegSet$, the
\emphic{edges}{edge} are the maximal connected portions of the
segments not containing any vertex, and the \emphic{faces}{face} are
the connected components of the complement of the union of the
segments of $\SegSet$.  These definitions are depicted in
\figref{arrangement}.

\NEW{%
   For numerical reasons (and also conceptually), a symbolic
   representation would be better than a numerical one. Thus, an
   intersection vertex would be represented by two pointers to the
   segments that their intersection is this vertex. Similarly, an edge
   would be represented as a pointer to the segment that contains it,
   and two pointers to the vertices forming its endpoints.
   
   Naturally, we are assuming here that we have geometric primitives
   that can resolve any decision problem of interest that involve a
   few geometric entities. For example, for a given segment $\seg$ and
   a point $\pnt$, we would be interested in deciding if $\pnt$ lies
   vertically below $\seg$.  From a theoretical point of view, all
   these primitives require a constant amount of computation, and are
   ``easy''. In the real world, numerical issues and degeneracies make
   implementing these primitives surprisingly challenging. We are
   going to ignore this major headache here, but the reader should be
   aware of it.
   
}

We will be interested in computing the arrangement
$\ArrX{\bigl.\SegSet}$ and a representation of it that makes it easy
to manipulate. In particular, we would like to be able to quickly
resolve questions of the type
%\smallskip%
\begin{compactenumi}[leftmargin=3em]
    \item are two points in the same face?
    \item can one traverse from one point to the other without
    crossing any segment?

\end{compactenumi}
% \smallskip%
The naive representation of each face as polygons (potentially with
holes) is not conducive to carrying out such tasks, since a polygon
might be arbitrarily complicated. Instead, we will prefer to break the
arrangement into smaller canonical tiles.


To this end, a \emphOnly{vertical trapezoid}\index{vertical trapezoid} is a quadrangle with two
vertical sides. The breaking of the faces into such trapezoids is the
\emphOnly{vertical decomposition}\index{vertical decomposition} of the arrangement
$\ArrX{\bigl.\SegSet}$.






Formally, for a subset $\Sample \subseteq \SegSet$, let
$\ArrVD{\bigl.\Sample}$ denote the \emphOnly{vertical decomposition}\index{vertical decomposition} of
the plane formed by the arrangement $\ArrX{\bigl. \Sample}$ of the
segments of $\Sample$. This is the partition of the plane into
interior disjoint vertical trapezoids formed by erecting vertical
walls through each vertex of $\ArrVD{\bigl.\Sample}$.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \IncludeGraphics[scale=0.8]{\File{figs/vertical}}
    \caption{The vertical decomposition of an arrangement of segments.}
    \figlab{vert:decomp}
\end{figure}
A \emphic{vertex}{vertical decomposition!vertex} of
$\ArrVD{\bigl.\Sample}$ is either an endpoint of a segment of
$\Sample$ or an intersection point of two of its segments. From each
such vertex we shoot up (similarly, down) a vertical ray till it hits
a segment of $\Sample$ or it continues all the way to infinity.  See
\figref{vert:decomp}.

Note that a vertical trapezoid is defined by at most four segments:
two segments defining its ceiling and floor and two segments defining
the two intersection points that induce the two vertical walls on its
boundary. Of course, a vertical trapezoid might be degenerate and thus
be defined by fewer segments (i.e., an unbounded vertical trapezoid or
a triangle with a vertical segment as one of its sides).


Vertical decomposition breaks the faces of the arrangement that might
be arbitrarily complicated into entities (i.e., vertical trapezoids)
of constant complexity. This makes handling arrangements (decomposed
into vertical trapezoid) much easier computationally.


In the following, we assume that the $n$ segments of $\SegSet$ have
$k$ pairwise intersection points overall, and we want to compute the
arrangement $\Arr = \ArrX{\bigl.\SegSet}$; namely, compute the edges,
vertices, and faces of $\ArrX{\bigl.\SegSet}$. One possible way is the
following: Compute a random permutation of the segments of $\SegSet$:
$\SegSet = \permut{\seg_1, \ldots, \seg_n}$. Let $\SegSet_i =
\permut{\seg_1,\ldots, \seg_i}$ be the prefix of length $i$ of
$\SegSet$. Compute $\ArrVD{\bigl.\SegSet_i}$ from
$\ArrVD{\bigl.\SegSet_{i-1}}$, for $i=1, \ldots, n$.  Clearly,
$\ArrVD{\bigl.\SegSet} = \ArrVD{\bigl.\SegSet_n}$, and we can extract
$\ArrX{\bigl.  \SegSet }$ from it. Namely, in the $i$\th iteration, we
insert the segment $\seg_i$ into the arrangement
$\ArrVD{\bigl.\SegSet_{i-1}}$.

This technique of building the arrangement by inserting the segments
one by one is called \emphic{randomized incremental
   construction}{random incremental construction}.

\NEW{%
\paragraph*{Who need these pesky arrangements anyway?}

The reader might wonder who needs arrangements? As a concrete
examples, consider a situation where you are give several maps of a
city containing different layers of information (i.e., streets map,
sewer map, electric lines map, train lines map, etc). We would like to
compute the overlay map formed by putting all these maps on top of
each other. For example, we might be interested in figuring out if
there are any buildings lying on a planned train line, etc.

More generally, think about a set of general constraints in
$\Re^d$. Each constraint is bounded by a surface, or a patch of a
surface. The decomposition of $\Re^d$ formed by the arrangement of
these surfaces gives us a description of the parametric space in a way
that is algorithmically useful. For example, finding if there is a
point inside all the constraints, when all the constraints are induced
by linear inequalities, is linear programming. Namely, arrangements
are a useful way to think about any parametric space partitioned by
various constraints.

}

\subsection{Randomized incremental construction (\RIC{})}

\index{random incremental construction}

Imagine that we had computed the arrangement $\ArrB_{i-1} =
\ArrVD{\bigl.  \SegSet_{i-1} }$. In the $i$\th iteration we compute
$\ArrB_i$ by inserting $\seg_i$ into the arrangement
$\ArrB_{i-1}$. This involves splitting some trapezoids (and merging
some others).


\begin{figure}[t]
    \centerline{\IncludeGraphics[scale=0.99]{\File{figs/merge}}}
    \caption{}
    \figlab{merge:v:d}
\end{figure}

As a concrete example, consider \figref{merge:v:d}. Here we insert
$\seg$ in the arrangement. To this end we split the ``vertical
trapezoids'' $\triangle \pnt \pntA \pntC$ and
$\triangle \pntD \pntA \pntC$, each into three trapezoids. The two
trapezoids $\TrapA$ and $\TrapB$ now need to be merged together to
form the new trapezoid which appears in the vertical decomposition of
the new arrangement. (Note that the figure does not show all the
trapezoids in the vertical decomposition.)

To facilitate this, we need to compute the trapezoids of $\ArrB_{i-1}$
that intersect $\seg_i$. This is done by maintaining a \emphOnly{conflict
   graph}\index{conflict
   graph}.  Each trapezoid $\sigma \in \ArrVD{\bigl.\SegSet_{i-1}}$
maintains a \emphOnly{conflict list}\index{conflict list} $\cl(\sigma)$ of \emph{all} the
segments of $\SegSet$ that intersect its interior.  In particular, the
conflict list of $\sigma$ cannot contain any segment of
$\SegSet_{i-1}$, and as such it contains only the segments of $\SegSet
\setminus \SegSet_{i-1}$ that intersect its interior.  We also
maintain a similar structure for each segment, listing all the
trapezoids of $\ArrVD{\bigl.\SegSet_{i-1}}$ that it currently
intersects (in its interior). We maintain those lists with cross
pointers, so that given an entry $(\Trap, s)$ in the conflict list of
$\sigma$, we can find the entry $(s,\Trap)$ in the conflict list of
$s$ in constant time.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \IncludeGraphics{\File{figs/split}}
    \caption{Splitting a trapezoid by a segment.}
    \figlab{split}
\end{figure}

Thus, given $\seg_i$, we know what trapezoids need to be split (i.e.,
all the trapezoids in $\cl(\seg_i)$).  Splitting a trapezoid $\sigma$
by a segment $\seg_i$ (see \figref{split}) is the operation of computing a set of (at most)
four trapezoids that cover $\sigma$ and have $\seg_i$ on their
boundary.  We compute those new trapezoids, and next we need to
compute the conflict lists of the new trapezoids. This can be easily
done by taking the conflict list of a trapezoid $\sigma \in
\cl(\seg_i)$ and distributing its segments among the $O(1)$ new
trapezoids that cover $\sigma$. Using careful implementation, this
requires a linear time in the size of the conflict list of $\sigma$.

Note that only trapezoids that intersect $\seg_i$ in their interior
get split. Also, we need to update the conflict lists for the segments
(that were not inserted yet).

We next sketch the low-level details involved in maintaining these
conflict lists.  For a segment $\seg$ that intersects the interior of
a trapezoid $\sigma$, we maintain the pair $(\seg,\sigma)$. For every
trapezoid $\sigma$, in the current vertical decomposition, we maintain
a doubly linked list of all such pairs that contain $\sigma$.
Similarly, for each segment $\seg$ we maintain the doubly linked list
of all such pairs that contain $\seg$. Finally, each such pair
contains two pointers to the location in the two respective lists
where the pair is being stored.

It is now straightforward to verify that using this data-structure we
can implement the required operations in linear time in the size of
the relevant conflict lists.

In the above description, we ignored the need to merge adjacent
trapezoids if they have identical floor and ceiling -- this can be
done by a somewhat straightforward and tedious implementation of the
vertical decomposition data-structure, by providing pointers between
adjacent vertical trapezoids and maintaining the conflict list sorted
(or by using hashing) so that merge operations can be done quickly. In
any case, this can be done in linear time in the input/output size
involved, as can be verified.

\subsubsection{Analysis}

\begin{claim}
    The (amortized) running time of constructing $\ArrB_i\,$ from
    $\ArrB_{i-1}$ is proportional to the size of the conflict lists of
    the vertical trapezoids in $\ArrB_i \setminus \ArrB_{i-1}$ (and
    the number of such new trapezoids).
\end{claim}
\begin{proof}
    Observe that we can charge all the work involved in the $i$\th
    iteration to either the conflict lists of the newly created
    trapezoids or the deleted conflict lists. Clearly, the running
    time of the algorithm in the $i$\th iteration is linear in the
    total size of these conflict lists. Observe that every conflict
    gets charged twice -- when it is being created and when it is
    being deleted. As such, the (amortized) running time in the $i$\th
    iteration is proportional to the total length of the newly created
    conflict lists.
\end{proof}


Thus, to bound the running time of the algorithm, it is enough to
bound the expected size of the destroyed conflict lists in $i$\th
iteration (and sum this bound on the $n$ iterations carried out by the
algorithm). Or alternatively, bound the expected size of the conflict
lists created in the $i$\th iteration.

% \myParagraph{Expected size of $\ArrB_i$.}

\begin{lemma}
    \lemlab{expected:tau}%
    % 
    Let $\SegSet$ be a set of $n$ segments (in general
    position\footnote{In this case, no two intersection points of
       input segments are the same, no two intersection points (or
       vertices) have the same $x$-coordinate, no two segments lie on
       the same line, etc. Making the geometric algorithm work
       correctly for all degenerate inputs is a huge task that can
       usually be handled by tedious and careful implementation. Thus,
       we will always assume general position of the input. In other
       words, in theory all geometric inputs are inherently good,
       while in practice they are all evil (as anybody who tried to
       implement geometric algorithms can testify). The reader is
       encouraged not to use this to draw any conclusions on the human
       condition.})  with $k$ intersection points. Let $\ObjSet_i$ be
    the first $i$ segments in a random permutation of $\ObjSet$.  The
    expected size of $\,\ArrB_i =\ArrVD{\bigl.\SegSet_i}$, denoted by
    $\tau(i)$ (i.e., the number of trapezoids in $\ArrB_i$), is
    $O\pth{ i + k (i/n)^2 }$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Consider\footnote{The proof is provided in excruciating detail to
       get the reader used to this kind of argumentation. I would
       apologize for this pain, but it is a minor trifle, not to be
       mentioned, when compared to the other offenses in this book.} %
    an intersection point $\pnt = s \cap s'$, where $s, s' \in
    \SegSet$. The probability that $\pnt$ is present in
    $\ArrVD{\bigl.\SegSet_i}$ is equivalent to the probability that
    both $s$ and $s'$ are in $\SegSet_i$. This probability is
    \begin{equation*}
        \alpha = \frac{\binom{n-2}{i-2}}{\binom{n}{i}}%
        = \frac{(n-2)!}{(i-2)! \, (n-i)!} \cdot \frac{i! \,
           (n-i)!}{n!}%
        = \frac{i(i-1)}{n(n-1)}.
    \end{equation*}
    
    For each intersection point $\pnt$ in $\ArrX{\bigl. \SegSet }$
    define an indicator variable $X_\pnt$, which is $1$ if the two
    segments defining $\pnt$ are in the random sample \index{random
       sample} $\SegSet_i$ and $0$ otherwise. We have that
    $\Ex{X_\pnt}=\alpha$, and as such, by linearity of expectation,
    the expected number of intersection points in the arrangement
    $\Arr\pth{\SegSet_i}$ is
    \begin{equation*}
        \Ex{ \sum_{\pnt \in V} X_{\pnt}} = \sum_{\pnt \in V} \Ex{
           X_{\pnt}} = \sum_{\pnt \in V} \alpha = k \alpha,
    \end{equation*}
    where $V$ is the set of $k$ intersection points of $\ArrX{
       \bigl. \SegSet }$.  Also, every endpoint of a segment of
    $\SegSet_i$ contributed its two endpoints to the arrangement
    $\Arr\pth{\SegSet_i}$. Thus, we have that the expected number of
    vertices in $\Arr\pth{\SegSet_i}$ is
    \begin{equation*}
        2i + \frac{i(i-1)}{n(n-1)}k.
    \end{equation*}
    Now, the number of trapezoids in $\ArrVD{\bigl.\SegSet_i}$ is
    proportional to the number of vertices of $\Arr\pth{\SegSet_i}$,
    which implies the claim.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Backward analysis}
\seclab{backward:analysis}

In the following, we would like to consider the total amount of work
involved in the $i$\th iteration of the algorithm. The way to analyze
these iterations is (conceptually) to run the algorithm for the first
$i$ iterations and then run ``backward'' the last iteration.

So, imagine that the overall size of the conflict lists of the
trapezoids of $\ArrB_i$ is $W_i$ and the total size of the conflict
lists created only in the $i$\th iteration is $C_i$.

We are interested in bounding the expected size of $C_i$, since this
is (essentially) the amount of work done by the algorithm in this
iteration. Observe that the structure of $\ArrB_i$ is defined
independently of the permutation $\SegSet_i$ and depends only on the
(unordered) set $\SegSet_i = \brc{\seg_1, \ldots, \seg_i}$. So, fix
$\SegSet_i$. What is the probability that $\seg_i$ is a specific
segment $\seg$ of $\SegSet_i$?  Clearly, this is $1/i$ since this is
the probability of $\seg$ being the last element in a permutation of
the $i$ elements of $\SegSet_i$ (i.e., we consider a random
permutation of $\SegSet_i$).

Now, consider a trapezoid $\Trap \in \ArrB_i$. If $\Trap$ was created
in the $i$\th iteration, then $\seg_i$ must be one of the (at most
four) segments that define it. Indeed, if $\seg_i$ is not one of the
segments that define $\Trap$, then $\Trap$ existed in the vertical
decomposition before $\seg_i$ was inserted.  Since $\ArrB_i$ is
independent of the internal ordering of $\SegSet_i$, it follows that
$\Prob{ \sigma \in (\ArrB_i \setminus \ArrB_{i-1})} \leq 4/i$. In
particular, the overall size of the conflict lists in the end of the
$i$\th iteration is
\begin{equation*}
    W_i = \sum_{\sigma \in \ArrB_i} {|\cl(\sigma)|}.
    % \eqlab{conflict:lists:1}
\end{equation*}
As such, the expected overall size of the conflict lists created in
the $i$\th iteration is
\begin{equation*}
  \Ex{ C_i \sep{ \ArrB_i }} \leq \sum_{\sigma \in \ArrB_i}
  \frac{4}{i} \cardin{\cl(\sigma)\MakeSBig} \leq \frac{4}{i} W_i.
\end{equation*}
By \lemref{expected:tau}, the expected size of $\ArrB_i$ is $O\pth{i +
   k i^2/n^2}$. Let us guess (for the time being) that on average the
size of the conflict list of a trapezoid of $\ArrB_i$ is about
$O(n/i)$.  In particular, assume that we know that
\begin{equation*}
    \Ex{\bigl. W_i}%
    =%
    O\pth{ \, \Bigl( i + \frac{i^2}{n^2} k \Bigr) \frac{n}{i}}%
    =%
    O\pth{ n + k \frac{i}{n} },
    % \eqlab{conflict:lists:2}
\end{equation*}
by \lemref{expected:tau}, implying
\begin{equation}
    \Ex{\bigl. C_i }%
    =%
    \Ex{  \Bigl. \ExCond{C_i}{ \bigl. \ArrB_i } }%
    \leq%
    \Ex{ \frac{4}{i} W_i}%
    =%
    \frac{4}{i} \Ex{ \bigl. W_i }%
    =%
    O \pth{ \frac{4}{i}
       \, \pth{n + \frac{k i}{n} } \, }%
    =%
    O\pth{\frac{n}{i} +
       \frac{k}{n}},
    \eqlab{conflict:lists:3}
\end{equation}
using \lemref{conditional:expectation}.  In particular, the expected
(amortized) amount of work in the $i$\th iteration is proportional to
$\Ex{\bigl. C_i}$. Thus, the overall expected running time of the
algorithm is
\begin{equation*}
    \Ex{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i }%
    =%
    \sum_{i=1}^{n} O\pth{\frac{n}{i} + \frac{k}{n}}%
    =%
    O\pth{\Bigl. n\log{n} + k }.
\end{equation*}

\begin{theorem}
    \thmlab{v:d}%
    % 
    Given a set $\SegSet$ of $n$ segments in the plane with $k$
    intersections, one can compute the vertical decomposition of
    $\ArrX{\bigl. \SegSet }$ in expected $O(n \log{n} + k )$ time.
\end{theorem}

\myParagraph{Intuition and discussion.} %
What remains to be seen is how we came up with the guess that the
average size of a conflict list of a trapezoid of $\ArrB_i$ is about
$O(n/i)$.  Note that using $\eps$-nets implies that the bound
$O((n/i)\log{i})$ holds with constant probability (see
\thmref{epsilon:net}) for all trapezoids in this arrangement. As
such, this result is only slightly surprising. To prove this, we
present in the next section a ``strengthening'' of $\eps$-nets to
geometric settings.

To get some intuition on how we came up with this guess, consider a
set $\PntSet$ of $n$ points on the line and a random sample
\index{random sample} $\Sample$ of $i$ points from $\PntSet$. Let
$\IntervalSet$ be the partition of the real line into (maximal) open
intervals by the endpoints of $\Sample$, such that these intervals do
not contain points of $\Sample$ in their interior.

Consider an interval (i.e., a one-dimensional trapezoid) of
$\IntervalSet$.  It is intuitively clear that this interval (in
expectation) would contain $O(n/i)$ points. Indeed, fix a point $x$ on
the real line, and imagine that we pick each point with probability
$i/n$ to be in the random sample. The random variable which is the
number of points of $\PntSet$ we have to scan starting from $x$ and
going to the right of $x$ till we ``hit'' a point that is in the
random sample behaves like a geometric variable with probability
$i/n$, and as such its expected value is $n/i$. The same argument
works if we scan $\PntSet$ to the left of $x$. We conclude that the
number of points of $\PntSet$ in the interval of $\IntervalSet$ that
contains $x$ but does not contain any point of $\Sample$ is $O(n/i)$
in expectation.

Of course, the vertical decomposition case is more involved, as each
vertical trapezoid is defined by four input segments. Furthermore, the
number of possible vertical trapezoids is larger.  Instead of proving
the required result for this special case, we will prove a more
general result which can be applied in a lot of other settings.

\subsection{Notation} %
Let $\ObjSet$ be a set of objects.  For a subset
$\Sample \subseteq \ObjSet$, we define a collection of `regions'
denoted by $\RegionsSet{\Sample}$.  For the case of vertical
decomposition of segments (i.e., \thmref{v:d}), the objects are
segments, the regions are trapezoids, and $\RegionsSet{\Sample}$ is
the set of vertical trapezoids in $\ArrVD{\bigl.\Sample}$.  Let
\begin{equation*}
    \AllRegions = \AllRegions\pth{\ObjSet} = \bigcup_{\Sample
       \subseteq \ObjSet} \RegionsSet{\Sample}
\end{equation*}
\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \IncludeGraphics{\File{figs/vertical_2}}
    \caption{$\DefSet{\Trap}= \{b,c, d,e\}$ and $\KillSet{\Trap} = \{ f\}$.}
    \figlab{stopping:set}
\end{figure}

denote the set of \emphic{all possible regions}{moments technique!all
   regions} defined by subsets of $\ObjSet$.


In the vertical trapezoids case, the set $\AllRegions$ is the set of
all vertical trapezoids that can be defined by any subset of the given
input segments.


We associate two subsets $\DefSet{\Trap}$,
$\KillSet{\Trap} \subseteq \ObjSet$ with each region
$\Trap \in \AllRegions$.


\index{set!defining}

The \emphic{defining set}{defining set} $\DefSet{ \Trap }$ of $\Trap$
is the subset of $\ObjSet$ defining the region $\Trap$ (the precise
requirements from this set are specified in the axioms below).  We
assume that for every $\Trap \in \AllRegions$,
$\cardin{\DefSet{\Trap}} \leq d$ for a (small) constant $d$. The
constant $d$ is sometime referred to as the \emphOnly{combinatorial
   dimension}\index{combinatorial
   dimension}. \index{dimension!combinatorial} In the case of
\thmref{v:d}, each trapezoid $\Trap$ is defined by at most four
segments (or lines) of $\ObjSet$ that define the region covered by the
trapezoid $\Trap$, and this set of segments is $\DefSet{\Trap}$.  See
\figref{stopping:set}.

\index{set!stopping}

The \emphOnly{stopping set}\index{stopping set} $\KillSet{ \Trap }$ of $\Trap$ is the set of
objects of $\ObjSet$ such that including any object of $\KillSet{
   \Trap }$ in $\Sample$ prevents $\Trap$ from appearing in
$\RegionsSet{\Sample }$.  In many applications $\KillSet{ \Trap }$ is
just the set of objects intersecting the cell $\Trap$; this is also
the case in \thmref{v:d}, where $\KillSet{\Trap}$ is the set of
segments of $\SegSet$ intersecting the interior of the trapezoid
$\Trap$ (see \figref{stopping:set}).  Thus, the stopping set of a
region $\Trap$, in many cases, is just the conflict list of this
region, when it is being created by an \RIC algorithm.  The
\emphic{weight}{weight!region} of $\Trap$ is $\WeightX{\Trap} =
\cardin{\KillSet{\Trap}}$.

% \medskip

\bigskip

\begin{defn}[Framework axioms]
    \deflab{framework:axioms}%
    %
    Let $\ObjSet, \RegionsSet{\Sample}, \DefSet{\Trap}$, and
    $\KillSet{\Trap}$ be such that for any subset
    $\Sample \subseteq \ObjSet$, the set $\RegionsSet{\Sample}$
    satisfies the following axioms:
    \begin{compactenumi}
        \item \itemlab{axiom:1} For any
        $\Trap \in \RegionsSet{\Sample}$, we have
        $\DefSet{\Trap} \subseteq \Sample$ and
        $\Sample \cap \KillSet{\Trap} = \emptyset$.
    
        \item \itemlab{axiom:2} If $\DefSet{\Trap} \subseteq \Sample$
        and $\KillSet{ \Trap }\cap \Sample=\emptyset$, then
        $\Trap\in \RegionsSet{\Sample}$.
    \end{compactenumi}
\end{defn}


\subsubsection{Examples of the general framework}

\begin{compactenumA}
    \item \textbf{Vertical decomposition.} %
    Discussed above.

    \smallskip
    
    \item \textbf{Points on a line.}  %
    Let $\ObjSet$ be a set of $n$ points on the real line. For a set
    $\Sample \subseteq \ObjSet$, let $\RegionsSet{\Sample}$ be the set
    of atomic intervals of the real lines formed by $\Sample$; that
    is, the partition of the real line into maximal connected sets
    (i.e., intervals and rays) that do not contain a point of
    $\Sample$ in their interior.
    
    Clearly, in this case, an interval $\Interval \in
    \RegionsSet{\Sample}$ the defining set of $\Interval$ (i.e.,
    $\DefSet{ \Interval }$) is the set containing the (one or two)
    endpoints of $\Interval$ in $\Sample$. The stopping set of an
    $\Interval$ is the set $\KillSet{ \Interval }$, which is the set
    of all points of $\ObjSet$ contained in $\Interval$.
    
    \smallskip
    \item \textbf{Vertices of the convex-hull in 2d.}
    % 
    Consider a set $\ObjSet$ of $n$ points in the plane. A vertex on
    the convex hull is defined by the point defining the vertex, and
    the two edges before and after it on the convex hull. To this end,
    a \emphOnly{certified vertex}\index{certified vertex} of the convex hull (say this vertex is
    $\pntA$) is a triplet $(\pnt, \pntA, \pntB)$, such that $\pnt,
    \pntA$ and $\pntB$ are consecutive vertices of $\CHX{\ObjSet}$
    (say, in clockwise order).  Observe, that computing the
    convex-hull of $\ObjSet$ is equivalent to computing the set of
    certified vertices of $\ObjSet$.
    
    For a set $\Sample \subseteq \ObjSet$, let $\RegionsSet{\Sample}$
    denote the set of certified vertices of $\Sample$ (i.e., this is
    equivalent to the set of vertices of the convex-hull of
    $\Sample$. For a certified vertex $\Trap \in
    \RegionsSet{\Sample}$, its defining set is the set of three
    vertices $\pnt, \pntA, \pntB$ that (surprise, surprise) define
    it. Its stopping set, is the set of all points in $\ObjSet$, that
    either on the ``wrong'' side of the line spanning $\pnt \pntA$, or
    on the ``wrong'' side of the line spanning $\pntA
    \pntB$. Equivalently, $\KillSet{\Trap}$ is the set of all points
    $\pntC \in \ObjSet \setminus \Sample$, such that the convex-hull
    of $\pnt, \pntA, \pntB$, and $\pntC$ does not form a convex
    quadrilateral.
    
    \smallskip
    \item \textbf{Edges of the convex-hull in 3d.}  Let $\ObjSet$ be a
    set of points in three dimensions.  An edge $\Edge$ of the
    convex-hull of a set $\Sample \subseteq \ObjSet$ of points in
    $\Re^3$ is defined by two vertices of $\ObjSet$, and it can be
    certified as being on the convex hull $\CHX{\Sample}$, by the two
    faces $\Face, \Face'$ adjacent to $\Edge$. If all the points of
    $\Sample$ are on the ``right'' side of both these two faces then
    $\Edge$ is an edge of the convex hull of $\Sample$.  Computing all
    the certified edges of $\ObjSet$ is equivalent to computing the
    convex-hull of $\ObjSet$.
    
    In the following, assume that each face of any convex-hull of a
    subset of points of $\ObjSet$ is a triangle. As such, a face of
    the convex-hull would be defined by three points.  Formally, the
    \emphOnly{butterfly}\index{butterfly} of an edge $\Edge$ of $\CHX{\Sample}$ is
    $(\Edge, \pnt, \pntA)$, where $pnt, \pntA \in \Sample$, and such
    that all the points of $\Sample$ are on the same side as $\pntA$
    of the plane spanned by $\Edge$ and $\pnt$ (we have symmetric
    condition requiring that all the points of $\ObjSet$ are on the
    same as $\pnt$ of the plane spanned by $\Edge$ and $\pntA$).
    
    For a set $\Sample \subseteq \PntSet$, let $\RegionsSet{\Sample}$
    be its set of butterflies.  Clearly, computing all the butterflies
    of $\ObjSet$ (i.e., $\RegionsSet{\ObjSet}$) is equivalent to
    computing the convex-hull of $\ObjSet$.
    
    For a butterfly $\Trap = \pth{\Edge, \pnt, \pntA} \in
    \RegionsSet{\Sample}$ its defining set (i.e., $\DefSet{\Trap}$) is
    a set of four points (i.e., the two points defining its edge
    $\Edge$, and the to additional vertices defining the two faces
    $Face$ and $\Face'$ adjacent to it). Its stopping set
    $\KillSet{\Trap}$, is the set of all the points of
    $\ObjSet\setminus \Sample$ that of different sides of the plane
    spanned by $\Edge$ and $\pnt$ (resp. $\Edge$ and $\pntA$) than
    $\pntA$ (resp. $\pnt$) [here, the stopping set is the union of
    these two sets].

    \smallskip%
    \item \textbf{Delaunay triangles in 2d.}  For a set of $\ObjSet$
    of $n$ points in the plane. Consider a subset
    $\Sample \subseteq \ObjSet$. A \emphic{Delaunay
       circle}{Delaunay!circle} of $\Sample$ is a disc $\disk$ that
    has three points $\pnt_1, \pnt_2, \pnt_3$ of $\Sample$ on its
    boundary, and no points of $\Sample$ in its interior. Naturally,
    these three points define a \emphic{Delaunay
       triangle}{Delaunay!triangle}
    $\triangle = \triangle \pnt_1 \pnt_2 \pnt_3$. The defining set is
    $\DefSet{\triangle} = \brc{\pnt_1, \pnt_2, \pnt_3}$, and the
    stopping set $\KillSet{\triangle}$ is the set of all points in
    $\ObjSet$ that are contained in the interior of the disk $\disk$.
    
\end{compactenumA}

\subsection{Analysis}

In the following, $\ObjSet$ is a set of $n$ objects complying with
\itemref{axiom:1} and \itemref{axiom:2} of \defref{framework:axioms}.

\bigskip

\myParagraph{The challenge.}
%
What makes the analysis not easy is that there are dependencies
between the defining set of a region and its stopping set (i.e.,
conflict list). In particular, we have the following difficulties
\begin{compactenumA}
    \item The defining set might be of different sizes depending on
    the region $\Trap$ being considered.

    \item Even if all the regions have a defining set of the same size
    $d$ (say, $4$ as in the case of vertical trapezoids), it is not
    true that every $d$ objects define a valid region. For example,
    for the case of segments, the four segments might be vertically
    separated from each other (i.e., think about them as being four
    disjoint intervals on the real line), and they do not define a
    vertical trapezoid together. Thus, our analysis is going to be a
    bit loopy loop -- we are going to assume we know how many regions
    exists (in expectation) for a random sample of certain size, and
    use this to derive the desired bounds.
\end{compactenumA}


\subsubsection{On the probability of a region to be created}


Inherently, to analyze a randomized algorithm using this framework, we
will be interested in the probability that a certain region would be
created. Thus, let
\begin{equation*}
    \CSProbFull{r}{d}{k}{n}
\end{equation*}
denote the probability that a region $\Trap \in \AllRegions$ appears
in $\RegionsSet{\Sample}$, where its defining set is of size $d$, its
stopping set is of size $k$, $\Sample$ is a random sample
\index{random sample} of size $r$ from a set $\ObjSet$, and
$n=\cardin{\ObjSet}$.  \NEW{ Specifically, $\Trap$ is a
   \emph{feasible} region that might be created by an algorithm
   computing $\RegionsSet{\Sample}$.}


\bigskip

\myParagraph{The sampling model.}  %
%
For describing algorithms it is usually easier to work with samples
created by picking a subset of a certain size (without repetition)
from the original set of objects. Usually, in the algorithmic
applications this would be done by randomly permuting the objects and
interpreting a prefix of this permutation as a random
sample. Insisting on analyzing this framework in the ``right''
sampling model creates some non-trivial technical pain.



\begin{lemma}
    \lemlab{c:s:probability}%
    % 
    We have that $\ds\,\CSProbFull{r}{d}{k}{n} \approx
    \pth{1-\frac{r}{n}}^k \pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d$. Formally,
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{1}{2^{2d}} \;
        \pth{ 1 - 4 \cdot \frac{ r}{n} }^{k}
        \pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d
        \;\leq \;
        \CSProbFull{r}{d}{k}{n}
        \;\leq\; 2^{2d} \;
        \pth{ 1 - \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{r}{n} }^{k}
        \,\pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d.
        \eqlab{c:s:probability}
    \end{equation}    
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\Trap$ be the region under consideration that is defined by
    $d$ objects and having $k$ stoppers (i.e.,
    $k=\KillSet{\Trap}$). We are interested in the probability of
    $\Trap$ being created when taking a sample of size $r$ (without
    repetition) from a set $\ObjSet$ of $n$ objects. Clearly, this
    probability is $\CSProbFull{r}{d}{k}{n} = {\binom{n-d-k}{r-d}}
    \;/\; { \binom{n}{r}}$, as we have to pick the $d$ defining
    objects into the random sample and avoid picking any of the $k$
    stoppers. A tedious but careful calculation, delegated to
    \secref{c:s:estimate}, implies \Eqref{c:s:probability}.
    
    Instead, here is an elegant argument for why this estimate is
    correct in a slightly different sampling model. We pick every
    element of $\ObjSet$ into the sample $\Sample$ with probability
    $r/n$, and this is done independently for each object. In
    expectation, the random sample is of size $r$, and clearly the
    probability that $\Trap$ is created is the probability that we
    pick its $d$ defining objects (that is, $(r/n)^d$) multiplied by
    the probability that we did not pick any of its $k$ stoppers (that
    is, $(1-r/n)^k$).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    The bounds of \Eqref{c:s:probability} hold only when $r, d$, and
    $k$ are in certain (reasonable) ranges. For the sake of simplicity
    of exposition we ignore this minor issue. With care, all our
    arguments work when one pays careful attention to this minor
    technicality.
\end{remark}



\subsubsection{On exponential decay}

For any natural number $r$ and a number $t >0$, consider $\Sample$ to
be a random sample \index{random sample} of size $r$ from $\ObjSet$
without repetition.  A region $\Trap \in \RegionsSet{\Sample}$ as
being \emphic{$t$-heavy}{heavy!t@$t$-heavy} if
$\WeightX{\Trap} \geq t \ds \cdot \frac{ n}{r}$. Let
$\RegionsHeavy{t}{\Sample}$ denote all the $t$-heavy regions of
$\RegionsSet{\Sample}$.\footnote{These are the regions that are at
   least $t$ times overweight. Speak about an obesity problem.} %\

\NEW{Intuitively, and somewhat incorrectly, we expect the average
   weight of a region of $\RegionsSet{\Sample}$ to be roughly $n/r$. }
We thus expect the size of this set to drop fast as $t$
increases. Indeed, \lemref{c:s:probability} tells us that a trapezoid
of weight $t\, (n/r)$ has probability
\begin{align*}
    \CSProbFull{r}{d}{t\cdot \frac{n}{r}}{n}%
    &\approx %
    \pth{1-\frac{r}{n}}^{t\,(n/r)} \pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d %
    \approx %
    \exp\pth{-t} \cdot \pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d %
    \approx %
    \exp\pth{\bigl.-t+1} \cdot \pth{1-\frac{r}{n}}^{n/r}
    \pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d%
    \\
    &\approx %
    \exp\pth{-t+1} \cdot \CSProbFull{r}{d}{n/r}{n}
\end{align*}
to be created, since $(1-r/n)^{n/r} \approx 1/e$. Namely, a $t$-heavy
region has exponentially lower probability to be created than a region
of weight $n/r$. We next formalize this argument.




\begin{lemma}
    \lemlab{e:x:p:help}%
    % 
    Let $r \leq n$ and let $t$ be parameters, such that
    $1 \leq t \leq r/d$. Furthermore, let $\Sample$ be a sample of
    size $r$, and let $\SampleA$ be a sample of size $r'=\floor{r/t}$,
    both from $\ObjSet$.  Let $\Trap \in \AllRegions$ be a region with
    weight $\WeightX{\Trap} \geq t\,(n/r)$.  Then,
    \begin{math}
        \Bigl.\ds \Prob{\bigl. \Trap \in \RegionsSet{\Sample}} =
        O\pth{ \exp\pth{-{t}/{2}\bigr.} t^d \,{\Prob{\bigl. \Trap \in
                 \RegionsSet{\SampleA}} } }
\end{math}
.
    % \end{equation*}
\end{lemma}


\begin{proof}
    For the sake of simplicity of exposition, assume that
    $k = \WeightX[]{\Trap} = t\,(n/r)$.  By \lemref{c:s:probability}
    (i.e., \Eqref{c:s:probability}) we have
    \begin{align*}
      \frac{\Prob{\Trap \in \RegionsSet{\Sample}}}{ \Prob{\Trap \in
      \RegionsSet{\SampleA}}}%
      &=%
        \frac{\CSProbFull{r}{d}{k}{n}} {\CSProbFull{r'}{d}{k}{n}}
        % 
          \leq \frac{2^{2d}\pth{ 1 - \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{r}{n} }^{k}
          \,\pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d}{%
          \frac{1}{2^{2d}} \; \pth{ 1 - 4 \frac{ r'}{n} }^{k}
          \pth{\frac{r'}{n}}^d}
          \leq%
          2^{4d}\exp \pth{ - \frac{kr}{2n} } \pth{ 1 + 8 \frac{
          r'}{n} }^{k} \pth{\frac{r}{r'}}^d%
      \\&%
      \leq 2^{4d}\exp
      \pth{ 8 \frac{ kr'}{n}- \frac{kr}{2n} }
      \,\pth{\frac{r}{r'}}^d
      = 2^{4d}\exp \pth{ 8 \frac{ tn \floor{r/t}}{nr}-
      \frac{tnr}{2nr} } \pth{\frac{r}{\floor{r/t}}}^d = O
      \pth{ \exp\pth{-t/2} t^d },
    \end{align*}
    since $1/(1-x) \leq 1+2x$ for $x \leq 1 /2$ and $1+y \leq \exp
    \pth{y}$, for all $y$. (The constant in the above $O(\cdot)$
    depends exponentially on $d$.)
\end{proof}

Let
\begin{equation*}
    \NumOfRegionsZero{r} = \Ex{
       \cardin{\MakesBig\RegionsSet{\Sample}}}%
    \quad \text{and}\quad%
    \NumOfRegions{t}{r} = \Ex{
       \cardin{\RegionsHeavy{t}{\Sample}}},
\end{equation*}
where the expectation is over random subsets $\Sample \subseteq
\ObjSet$ of size $r$. Note that $\NumOfRegionsZero{r} =
\NumOfRegions{0}{r}$ is the expected number of regions created by a
random sample of size $r$. In words, $\NumOfRegions{t}{r}$ is the
expected number of regions in a structure created by a sample of $r$
random objects, such that these regions have weight which is $t$ times
larger than the ``expected'' weight (i.e., $n/r$).  In the following,
we assume that $\NumOfRegionsZero{r}$ is a monotone increasing
function.

\begin{lemma}[The exponential decay lemma]
    \lemlab{exponential:decay}%
    % 
    Given a set $\ObjSet$ of $n$ objects and parameters $r \leq n$ and
    $1 \leq t \leq r/d$, where $d = \max_{\Trap \in \AllRegions\pth
       {\ObjSet}} \cardin{\DefSet{\Trap}}$, if axioms (i) and (ii)
    above hold for any subset of $\ObjSet$, then
    \begin{equation}
        \NumOfRegions{t}{r}  %=
        = O\bigl( t^d
        \exp\pth{-t/2} \,  \NumOfRegionsZero{ r}
        \bigr).
        \eqlab{exponential:decay}
    \end{equation}    
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Let $\Sample$ be a random sample of size $r$ from $\ObjSet$ and
    let $\SampleA$ be a random sample of size $r' = \floor{r/t}$ from
    $\ObjSet$.  Let $H = \bigcup_{X \subseteq \ObjSet, \cardin{X} = r}
    \RegionsHeavy{t}{X}$ denote the set of all $t$-heavy regions that
    might be created by a sample of size $r$.  In the following, the
    expectation is taken over the content of the random samples
    $\Sample$ and $\SampleA$.
    
    For a region $\Trap$, let $X_\Trap$ be the indicator variable that
    is $1$ if and only if $\Trap \in \RegionsSet{\Sample}$. By
    linearity of expectation and since $\Ex{X_\Trap} = \Prob{\Trap\in
       \RegionsSet{\Sample}}$, we have
    \begin{align*}
      \NumOfRegions{t}{r}%
      &=%
        \Ex{\bigl.\cardin{ \RegionsHeavy{t}{\Sample}} }%
        =%
        \Ex{\Bigl. \smash{\sum_{\Trap\in H} X_\Trap}} %
        =%
        \sum_{\Trap\in H} \Ex{\bigl.X_\Trap  } %
        =%
        \sum_{\Trap\in H} \Prob{\Trap\in \RegionsSet{\Sample}
        \MakesBig } %
      \\
      &=%
        O \Bigl( t^d \exp(-t/2) \sum_{\Trap\in H} \Prob{\Trap\in
        \RegionsSet{\SampleA}} \Bigr)%
        % 
        =%
        O \Bigl( t^d \exp(-t/2) \sum_{\Trap\in \AllRegions}
        \Prob{\Trap\in \RegionsSet{\SampleA}}\Bigr)%
      \\&%
      =%
      O \pth{ t^d \exp(-t/2)\, \NumOfRegionsZero{r'}}%
      =%
      O \pth{ t^d \exp(-t/2)\, \NumOfRegionsZero{r}},
    \end{align*}
    by \lemref{e:x:p:help} and since $\NumOfRegionsZero{r}$ is a
    monotone increasing function.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Bounding the moments}

Consider a different randomized algorithm that in a first round
samples $r$ objects, $\Sample \subseteq \ObjSet$ (say, segments),
computes the arrangement induced by these $r$ objects (i.e.,
$\ArrVD{\bigl.\Sample}$), and then inside each region $\Trap$ it
computes the arrangement of the $\WeightX{\Trap}$ objects intersecting
the interior of this region, using an algorithm that takes
\begin{math}
    O\pth{\bigl. {\WeightX{\Trap}}^c }
\end{math}
time, where $c > 0$ is
some fixed constant. The overall expected running time of this
algorithm is
\begin{equation*}
    \Ex{\Bigl. \smash{ \sum_{\Trap \in \RegionsSet{\Sample}} \pth{
             \bigl. \WeightX[]{\Trap} }^c}}.
\end{equation*}
We are now able to bound this quantity.

\begin{theorem}[Bounded moments theorem]
    \thmlab{cs}%
    %
    Let $\Sample \subseteq \ObjSet$ be a random subset of size
    $r$. Let
    $\NumOfRegionsZero{r} = \Ex{\bigl. |\RegionsSet{\Sample}| }$ and
    let $c \geq 1$ be an arbitrary constant. Then,
    \begin{equation*}
        \biggl.
        \Ex{\Bigl. \smash{ \sum_{\Trap \in \RegionsSet{\Sample}}
              \pth{\bigl.\WeightX{\Trap}}^c}}%
        =%
        O \pth{
           \NumOfRegionsZero{r} \; \pth{\frac{n}{r}}^c\,}.
    \end{equation*}    
\end{theorem}
\smallskip%
\begin{proof}
    Let $\Sample \subseteq \ObjSet$ be a random sample \index{random
       sample} of size $r$. Observe that all the regions with weight
    in the range $\ds\, \left[(t-1)\tfrac{n}{r} \,, \, t\cdot
        \frac{n}{r} \MakeSBig\right)\,$ are in the set
    $\RegionsHeavy{t-1}{\Sample} \setminus \RegionsHeavy{t}{\Sample}
    $. As such, we have by \lemref{exponential:decay} that
    \begin{align*}
      W
      &=%      
        \Ex{\Bigl.\smash{\sum_{\Trap \in \RegionsSet{\Sample}}\!\!\! \WeightX{\Trap}^c }}%
        \leq%
        \Ex{\Bigl. \smash{ \sum_{t \ge 1} \pth{ t \frac{n}{r} }^c \pth{
        \cardin{\RegionsHeavy{t-1}{\Sample}} -
        \cardin{\RegionsHeavy{t}{\Sample} }\, } }}%
        \leq%
        \Ex{\Bigl. \smash{ \sum_{t \ge 1} \pth{ t \frac{n}{r} }^c {\MakeSBig
        \cardin{\RegionsHeavy{t-1}{\Sample}} }\, }}
        % 
      \\[2ex]
      &
      %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
        \leq%
        \pth{\frac{n}{r} }^{c} \sum_{t \ge 0} (t+1)^c 
        \Ex{\bigl. \cardin{\RegionsHeavy{t}{\Sample} } }
        =%
        \pth{ \frac{n}{r} }^c \sum_{t \ge 0} (t+1)^c \,
        \NumOfRegions{t}{r}%
        =%
        \pth{ \frac{n}{r} }^c \sum_{t \ge 0}
        O\pth{ \pth{t+1}^{\,c \, + \,d} \, \exp(-t/2) \,
        \NumOfRegionsZero{ r } }
      \\&
      =%
      O\Bigl( \NumOfRegionsZero{r } \, \pth{ \frac{n}{r} }^c
      \sum_{t \ge 0} \pth{t+1}^{\,c\,+\,d} \, \exp(-t/2)
      \Bigr)%
      =%
      O \pth{ \NumOfRegionsZero{r } 
      \pth{{\frac{n}{r}} }^c },
    \end{align*}
    since $c$ and $d$ are both constants.
\end{proof}

\section{Applications}

\subsection{Analyzing the \RIC algorithm for %
   vertical decomposition}

We remind the reader that the input of the algorithm of
\secref{backward:analysis} is a set $\SegSet$ of $n$ segments with $k$
intersections, and it uses randomized incremental construction to
compute the vertical decomposition of the arrangement
$\ArrX{\bigl.\SegSet}$.

\lemref{expected:tau} shows that the number of vertical trapezoids in
the randomized incremental construction is in expectation
$\NumOfRegionsZero{i} = O\pth{ i + k\, (i/n)^2}$.  Thus, by
\thmref{cs} (used with $c=1$), we have that the total expected size
of the conflict lists of the vertical decomposition computed in the
$i$\th step is
\begin{equation*}
    \Ex{\bigl. W_i}%
    =%
    \Ex{ \Bigl. \smash{\sum_{\Trap \in \ArrB_i} \WeightX{\Trap} } }%
    =%
    O \pth{ \NumOfRegionsZero{i}\frac{n}{i}}%
    =%
    O \pth{ n + k \frac{i}{n}}.
\end{equation*}

This is the missing piece in the analysis of
\secref{backward:analysis}. Indeed, the amortized work in the $i$\th
step of the algorithm is $O(W_i/i)$ (see \Eqref{conflict:lists:3}),
and as such, the expected running time of this algorithm is
\begin{equation*}
    \Ex{  O \pth{\Bigl.
          \smash{\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{W_i}{i} }
       }
    } %
    =%
    O \biggl( \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{i} \Bigl({ n + k \frac{i}{n}} \Bigr) \biggr)%
    =%
    O\pth{ n \log n + k }.
\end{equation*}
This implies \thmref{v:d}.



\subsection{Cuttings}

Let $\SegSet$ be a set of $n$ lines in the plane, and let $r$ be an
arbitrary parameter. A \emphic{$(1/r)$-cutting}{cutting} of $\SegSet$
is a partition of the plane into constant complexity regions such that
each region intersects at most $n/r$ lines of $\SegSet$. It is natural
to try to minimize the number of regions in the cutting, as cuttings
are a natural tool for performing ``divide and conquer''.

A neat proof of the existence of suboptimal cuttings follows readily
from the exponential decay lemma.

\begin{lemma}
    Let $\ObjSet$ be a set of $n$ segments in the plane, and let
    $\Sample$ be a random sample from $\ObjSet$ of size
    $\ell = c r \ln r$, where $c$ is a sufficiently large
    constant. Then, with probability $\geq 1-1/r^{O(1)}$, the vertical
    decomposition of $\Sample$ is a cutting of size
    $O( r^2 \log^2 r)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    In our case, the vertical decomposition complexity
    $\NumOfRegionsZero{ \ell} = O(\ell^2)$ -- as $\ell$ segments have
    at most $\binom{\ell}{2}$ intersections.  For $t = c \ln r$, a
    vertical trapezoid $\Trap$ in $\ArrVD{\Sample}$ is bad if
    $\WeightX{\Trap} > r = t(n/\ell)$. But such a trapezoid is
    $t$-heavy.  Let $X$ be the random variable that is the number of
    bad trapezoids in $\ArrVD{\Sample}$. The exponential decay lemma
    (\lemref{exponential:decay}) states that
    \begin{equation*}
        \Ex{X} =
        \NumOfRegions{t}{\ell}  %=
        =%
        O\bigl( t^2
        \exp\pth{-t/2} \,  \NumOfRegionsZero{ \ell}
        \bigr)
        =
        O\bigl( (c \ln r)^2
        \exp\pth{-c\ln r/2} \,  \ell^2
        \bigr)
        =
        O\Bigl(  (\ln^2 r)
        r^{-c /2} \,  (r \log r)^2
        \Bigr)
        <%
        \frac{1}{r^{c/4}},
    \end{equation*}
    if $c$ is sufficiently large.  As such, we have
    $\Prob{X \geq 1} \leq {1}/{r^{c/4}}$ by Markov's inequality.
\end{proof}

We provide an alternative proof to the above using the $\eps$-net
theorem.
\begin{lemma}
    \lemlab{cuttings:bad}%
    % 
    There exists a $(1/r)$-cutting of a set of lines $\SegSet$ in the
    plane of size $O\pth{ (r\log r)^2 }$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Consider the range space having $\SegSet$ as its ground set and
    vertical trapezoids as its ranges (i.e., given a vertical
    trapezoid $\Trap$, its corresponding range is the set of all lines
    of $\SegSet$ that intersect the interior of $\Trap$). This range
    space has a \VC dimension which is a constant as can be easily
    verified. Let $X \subseteq \SegSet$ be an $\eps$-net for this
    range space, for $\eps = 1/r$. By \thmref{epsilon:net} ($\eps$-net
    theorem), there exists such an $\eps$-net $X$ of this range space,
    of size $O((1/\eps) \log (1/\eps)) = O(r \log r)$. In fact,
    \thmref{epsilon:net} states that an appropriate random sample is
    an $\eps$-net with non-zero probability, which implies, by the
    probabilistic method, that such a net (of this size) exists.

    Consider the vertical decomposition $\ArrVD{\bigl.X}$, where $X$
    is as above. We claim that this collection of trapezoids is the
    desired cutting.
    
    The bound on the size is immediate, as the complexity of
    $\ArrVD{\bigl.X}$ is $O\pth{\cardin{X}^2}$ and $\cardin{X} = O( r
    \log r)$.
    
    As for correctness, consider a vertical trapezoid $\Trap$ in the
    arrangement $\ArrVD{\bigl.X}$. It does not intersect any of the
    lines of $X$ in its interior, since it is a trapezoid in the
    vertical decomposition $\ArrVD{\bigl.X}$. Now, if $\Trap$
    intersected more than $n/r$ lines of $\SegSet$ in its interior,
    where $n = \cardin{\SegSet}$, then it must be that the interior of
    $\Trap$ intersects one of the lines of $X$, since $X$ is an
    $\eps$-net for $\SegSet$, a contradiction.
    
    It follows that $\Trap$ intersects at most $\eps n = n/r$ lines of
    $\SegSet$ in its interior.
\end{proof}


\begin{claim}
    Any $(1/r)$-cutting in the plane of $n$ lines contains at least
    $\Omega\pth{r^2}$ regions.
\end{claim}
\begin{proof}
    An arrangement of $n$ lines (in general position) has $M =
    \binom{n}{2}$ intersections. However, the number of intersections
    of the lines intersecting a single region in the cutting is at
    most $m = \binom{n/r}{2}$. This implies that any cutting must be
    of size at least $M / m = \Omega\pth{ n^2 / (n/r)^2 } =
    \Omega\pth{r^2}$.
\end{proof}


\NotAMSMode{\bigskip}

We can get cuttings of size matching the above lower bound using the
moments technique.


\begin{theorem}
    Let $\SegSet$ be a set of $n$ lines in the plane, and let $r$ be a
    parameter. One can compute a $(1/r)$-cutting of $\SegSet$ of size
    $O(r^2)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Let $\Sample \subseteq \SegSet$ be a random sample \index{random
       sample} of size $r$, and consider its vertical decomposition
    $\ArrVD{\bigl.\Sample}$. If a vertical trapezoid $\Trap \in
    \ArrVD{\bigl.\Sample}$ intersects at most $n/r$ lines of
    $\SegSet$, then we can add it to the output cutting. The other
    possibility is that a $\Trap$ intersects $t(n/r)$ lines of
    $\SegSet$, for some $t > 1$, and let $\cl(\Trap) \subset \SegSet$
    be the conflict list of $\Trap$ (i.e., the list of lines of
    $\SegSet$ that intersect the interior of $\Trap$). Clearly, a
    $(1/t)$-cutting for the set $\cl(\Trap)$ forms a vertical
    decomposition (clipped inside $\Trap$) such that each trapezoid in
    this cutting intersects at most $n/r$ lines of $\SegSet$. Thus, we
    compute such a cutting inside each such ``heavy'' trapezoid using
    the algorithm (implicit in the proof) of \lemref{cuttings:bad},
    and these subtrapezoids to the resulting cutting. Clearly, the
    size of the resulting cutting inside $\Trap$ is $O\pth{ t^2 \log^2
       t } = O\pth{ t^4 }$. The resulting two-level partition is
    clearly the required cutting. By \thmref{cs}, the expected size
    of the cutting is
    \begin{align*}
      O\pth{\biggl. \NumOfRegionsZero{r} + \Ex{\Bigl.\smash{ \sum_{\Trap \in
      \RegionsSet{\Sample}}
      \Bigl( 2\frac{\WeightX{\Trap}}{n/r} \Bigr)^4} }}%
      &=%
        O \pth{ \NumOfRegionsZero{r} + \pth{\frac{r}{n}}^4 \Ex{\Bigl.
        \smash{\sum_{\Trap \in \RegionsSet{\Sample}}
        \pth{\WeightX{\Trap}}^4} }}
        \displaybreak[0]
      \\[1ex]
      &\hspace{-1cm} =%
        O \pth{ \NumOfRegionsZero{r} \, + \, \pth{\frac{r}{n}}^4 
        \NumOfRegionsZero{r} \pth{\frac{n}{r}}^4 }%
        =%
        O\pth{ \MakesBig \NumOfRegionsZero{r}}%
        =%
        O\pth{r^2},
    \end{align*}
    since $\NumOfRegionsZero{r}$ is proportional to the complexity of
    $\Arr \pth{\Sample}$ which is $O(r^2)$.
\end{proof}


\section{Bounds on the probability of a %
   region to be created}
\seclab{c:s:estimate}

Here we prove \lemref{c:s:probability} in the ``right'' sampling
model. The casual reader is encouraged to skip this section, as it
contains mostly tedious (and not very insightful) calculations.


Let $\ObjSet$ be a given set of $n$ objects.  Let
$\CSProbFull{r}{d}{k}{n}$ be the probability that a region $\Trap \in
\AllRegions$ whose defining set is of size $d$ and whose stopping set
is of size $k$ appears in $\RegionsSet{\Sample}$, where $\Sample$ is a
random sample from $\ObjSet$ of size $r$ (without repetition).

\begin{lemma}
    \lemlab{c:s:first}%
    % 
    We have $\ds\CSProbFull{r}{d}{k}{n} =%
    \frac{\binom{n-d-k}{r-d}} { \binom{n}{r}} =%
    \frac{\binom{n-d-k}{r-d}}{\binom{n}{r-d}} \cdot
    \frac{\binom{r}{d}}{\binom{n-(r-d)}{d}}%
    =%
    \frac{\binom{n-d-k}{r-d}}{\binom{n-d}{r-d}} \cdot
    \frac{\binom{r}{d}}{\binom{n}{d}}$\,.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    So, consider a region $\Trap$ with $d$ defining objects in
    $\DefSet{\Trap}$ and $k$ detractors in $\KillSet{ \Trap }$.  We
    have to pick the $d$ defining objects of $\DefSet{\Trap}$ to be in
    the random sample \index{random sample} $\Sample$ of size $r$ but
    avoid picking any of the $k$ objects of $\KillSet{\Trap}$ to be in
    $\Sample$.
    
    The second part follows since $\;\ds \binom{n}{r} =
    {\binom{n}{r-d}\, \binom{n-(r-d)}{d}} \;/\; {\binom{r}{d}}$.
    Indeed, for the right-hand side first pick a sample of size $r-d$
    and then a sample of size $d$ from the remaining objects. Merging
    the two random samples, we get a random sample of size
    $r$. However, since we do not care if an object is in the first
    sample or second sample, we observe that every such random sample
    is being counted $\binom{r}{d}$ times.
    
    The third part is easier, as it follows from $\,\ds \binom{n}{r-d}
    \, \binom{n-(r-d)}{d} = \binom{n}{d} \, \binom{n-d}{r-d}$. The two
    sides count the different ways to pick two subsets from a set of
    size $n$, the first one of size $d$ and the second one of size
    $r-d$.
\end{proof}


\begin{lemma}%
    \lemlab{b:fraction}%
    % 
    For $M \geq m \geq t\geq 0$, we have $\;\ds
    \pth{\frac{m-t}{M-t}}^t\leq \frac{\binom{m}{t}}{\binom{M}{t}}
    \leq \pth{\frac{m}{M}}^t$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    % (i)
    We have that $\ds \alpha = \frac{\binom{m}{t}}{\binom{M}{t}} =
    \frac{m!}{(m-t)!t!}  \frac{(M-t)!t!}{M!} = \frac{m}{M} \cdot
    \frac{m-1}{M-1} \cdots \frac{m-t+1}{M-t+1} $. Now, since $M \geq
    m$, we have that $\ds \frac{m-i}{M-i} \leq \frac{m}{M}$, for all
    $i \geq 0$. As such, the maximum (resp. minimum) fraction on the
    right-hand size is $m/M$ (resp. $\frac{m-t+1}{M-t+1}$). As such,
    we have $\,\pth{\frac{m-t}{M-t}}^t \leq
    \pth{\frac{m-t+1}{M-t+1}}^t \leq \alpha \leq \pth{m/M}^t$.
\end{proof}


\begin{lemma}
    \lemlab{exchange:powers}%
    % 
    Let $0 \leq X, Y \leq N$. We have that
    $\,\ds\pth{1-\frac{X}{N}}^Y \leq \pth{1-\frac{Y}{2N}}^X$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Since $1-\alpha \leq \exp(-\alpha) \leq (1-\alpha/2)$, for $0 \leq
    \alpha \leq 1$, it follows that
    \begin{equation*}
        \pth{1-\frac{X}{N}}^Y %
        \leq%
        \exp \pth{ -\frac{XY}{N}}%
        =%
        \pth{\exp \pth{ -\frac{Y}{n}} }^X%
        \leq%
        \pth{ 1 -\frac{Y}{2n}}^X.
    \end{equation*}
\end{proof}


\begin{lemma}
    \lemlab{b:fraction:2}%
    % 
    For $2d \leq r \leq n/8$ and $k \leq n/2$, we have that
    \begin{equation*}
        \ds \frac{1}{2^{2d}} \; \pth{ 1 - 4 \cdot \frac{ r}{n} }^{k}
        \pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d \;\leq \; \CSProbFull{r}{d}{k}{n} \;\leq\;
        2^{2d} \; \pth{ 1 - \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{r}{n} }^{k}
        \,\pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d.
    \end{equation*}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    By \lemref{c:s:first}, \lemref{b:fraction}, and
    \lemref{exchange:powers} we have
    \begin{align*}
        \CSProbFull{r}{d}{k}{n}%
      & =%
        \frac{\binom{n-d-k}{r-d}} {\binom{n-d}{r-d}} \cdot
        \frac{\binom{r}{d}} {\binom{n}{d}}%
        \leq%
        \pth{ \frac{n-d-k}{n-d} }^{r-d} \pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d \;\leq\;
        \pth{ 1 - \frac{k}{n} }^{r-d} \pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d \leq 2^{d}
        \; \pth{ 1 - \frac{k}{n} }^{r} \,\pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d
        \\
        & \leq 2^{d} \; \pth{ 1 - \frac{r}{2 n} }^{k}
        \,\pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d,
    \end{align*}
    since $k \leq n/2$. As for the other direction, by similar
    argumentation, we have
    \begin{align*}
        \CSProbFull{r}{d}{k}{n}%
        &=%
        \frac{\binom{n-d-k}{r-d}}{\binom{n}{r-d}} \cdot
        \frac{\binom{r}{d}}{\binom{n-(r-d)}{d}}%
        \;\geq\;
        \pth{ \frac{n - d-k - (r-d)}{n-(r-d)} }^{r-d}
        \pth{\frac{r-d}{n-(r-d)-d}}^d\\
        %---------------------------------------------------------
        & =%
        \pth{ 1 - \frac{d+k}{n-(r-d)} }^{r-d}
        \pth{\frac{r-d}{n-r}}^d \;\geq\; \pth{ 1 - \frac{d+k}{n/2}
        }^{r} \pth{\frac{r/2}{n}}^d \\%
        & \geq%
        \frac{1}{2^d} \pth{ 1 - \frac{4 r}{n} }^{d+k}
        \pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d%
        \geq%
        \frac{1}{2^{2d}} \pth{ 1 - \frac{4 r}{n} }^{k}
        \pth{\frac{r}{n}}^d,
    \end{align*}
    by \lemref{exchange:powers} (setting $N=n/4$, $X=r$, and $Y=d+k$)
    and since $r \geq 2d$ and $4r/n \leq 1/2$.
\end{proof}




\section{Bibliographical notes}

The technique described in this chapter is generally attributed to the
work by Clarkson and Shor \cite{cs-arscg-89}, which is historically
inaccurate as the technique was developed by Clarkson
\cite{c-arscg-88}. Instead of mildly confusing the matter by referring
to it as the Clarkson technique, we decided to make sure to really
confuse the reader and refer to it as the \emphOnly{moments
   technique}\index{moments
   technique}. The Clarkson technique \cite{c-arscg-88} is in fact
more general and implies a connection between the number of ``heavy''
regions and ``light'' regions. The general framework can be traced
back to the earlier paper \cite{c-narsc-87}. This implies several
beautiful results, some of which we cover later in the book.
% The interested reader is referred to the work by
% Sharir %on this aspect
% \cite{s-cstre-01}.

For the full details of the algorithm of
\secref{vertical:decomposition}, the interested reader is refereed to
the books \cite{bcko-cgaa-08, by-ag-98}. Interestingly, in some cases
the merging stage can be skipped; see \cite{h-cctp-00}.

Agarwal \etal \cite{ams-cmfal-98} presented a slightly stronger
variant than the original version of Clarkson \cite{c-arscg-88} that
allows a region to disappear even if none of the members of its
stopping set are in the random sample.  This stronger setting is used
in computing the vertical decomposition of a single face in an
arrangement (instead of the whole arrangement). Here an insertion of a
faraway segment of the random sample \index{random sample} might cut
off a portion of the face of interest. In particular, in the settings
of Agarwal \etal{} Axiom (ii) is replaced by the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(ii)] \itemlab{axiom:2:x} %
    If $\Trap \in \RegionsSet{\Sample}$ and $\SampleA$ is a subset of
    $\Sample$ with $\DefSet{\Trap } \subseteq \SampleA$, then $\Trap
    \in \RegionsSet{\SampleA}$.
\end{enumerate}


Interestingly, Clarkson \cite{c-arscg-88} did not prove \thmref{cs}
using the exponential decay lemma but gave a direct proof. In fact,
his proof implicitly contains the exponential decay lemma.  We chose
the current exposition since it is more modular and provides a better
intuition of what is really going on and is hopefully slightly
simpler.  In particular, \lemref{c:s:probability} is inspired by the
work of Sharir \cite{s-cstre-01}.


The exponential decay lemma (\lemref{exponential:decay}) was proved by
Chazelle and Friedman \cite{cf-dvrsi-90}. The work of Agarwal \etal
\cite{ams-cmfal-98} is a further extension of this result. Another
analysis was provided by Clarkson \etal \cite{cms-frric-93}.

Another way to reach similar results is using the technique of
Mulmuley \cite{m-cgitr-94}, which relies on a direct analysis on
`stoppers' and `triggers'. This technique is somewhat less convenient
to use but is applicable to some settings where the moments technique
does not apply directly.  Also, his concept of the omega function
might explain why randomized incremental algorithms perform better in
practice than their worst case analysis \cite{m-eahsr-89}.

Backwards analysis in geometric settings was first used by Chew
\cite{c-bvdcp-86} and was formalized by Seidel \cite{s-barga-93}. It
is similar to the ``leave one out'' argument used in statistics for
cross validation. The basic idea was probably known to the Greeks (or
Russians or French) at some point in time.

(Naturally, our summary of the development is cursory at best and not
necessarily accurate, and all possible disclaimers apply. A good
summary is provided in the introduction of \cite{s-barga-93}.)

\myParagraph{Sampling model.}  As a rule of thumb all the different
sampling approaches are similar and yield similar results.  For
example, we used such an alternative sampling approach in the
``proof'' of \lemref{c:s:probability}.  It is a good idea to use
whichever sampling scheme is the easiest to analyze in figuring out
what's going on. Of course, a formal proof requires analyzing the
algorithm in the sampling model its uses.


\myParagraph{Lazy randomized incremental construction.}  %
If one wants to compute a single face that contains a marking point in
an arrangement of curves, then the problem in using randomized
incremental construction is that as you add curves, the region of
interest shrinks, and regions that were maintained should be
ignored. One option is to perform flooding in the vertical
decomposition to figure out what trapezoids are still reachable from
the marking point and maintaining only these trapezoids in the
conflict graph. Doing it in each iteration is way too expensive, but
luckily one can use a lazy strategy that performs this cleanup only a
logarithmic number of times (i.e., you perform a cleanup in an
iteration if the iteration number is, say, a power of $2$). This
strategy complicates the analysis a bit; see \cite{bds-lric-95} for
more details on this \emphOnly{lazy randomized incremental construction}\index{lazy randomized incremental construction}
\index{random incremental construction!lazy} technique. An alternative
technique was suggested by the author for the (more restricted) case
of planar arrangements; see \cite{h-twpa-00}. The idea is to compute
only what the algorithm really needs to compute the output, by
computing the vertical decomposition in an exploratory online
fashion. The details are unfortunately overwhelming although the
algorithm seems to perform quite well in practice.

\myParagraph{Cuttings.}  The concept of cuttings was introduced by
Clarkson. The first optimal size cuttings were constructed by Chazelle
and Friedman \cite{cf-dvrsi-90}, who proved the exponential decay
lemma to this end. Our elegant proof follows the presentation by d{}e
Berg and Schwarzkopf \cite{bs-ca-95}. The problem with this approach
is that the constant involved in the cutting size is awful%
\footnote{This is why all computations related to cuttings should be
   done on a waiter's bill pad. As Douglas Adams put it: ``On a
   waiter's bill pad, reality and unreality collide on such a
   fundamental level that each becomes the other and anything is
   possible, within certain parameters.''}. %
\Matousek \cite{m-ocfcp-98} showed that there $(1/r)$-cuttings with
$8r^2 + 6r + 4$ trapezoids, by using level approximation. A different
approach was taken by the author \cite{h-cctp-00}, who showed how to
get cuttings which seem to be quite small (i.e., constant-wise) in
practice. The basic idea is to do randomized incremental construction
but at each iteration greedily add all the trapezoids with conflict
list small enough to the cutting being output. One can prove that this
algorithm also generates $O(r^2)$ cuttings, but the details are not
trivial as the framework described in this chapter is not applicable
for analyzing this algorithm.

Cuttings also can be computed in higher dimensions for hyperplanes. In
the plane, cuttings can also be computed for well-behaved curves; see
\cite{sa-dsstg-95}.

Another fascinating concept is \emphOnly{shallow cuttings}\index{shallow cuttings}. These are
cuttings covering only portions of the arrangement that are in the
``bottom'' of the arrangement. \Matousek came up with the concept
\cite{m-rph-92}.  See \cite{aes-vdsl3-99, cch-smcpg-09} for extensions
and applications of shallow cuttings.

\myParagraph{Even more on randomized algorithms in geometry.}  We have
only scratched the surface of this fascinating topic, which is one of
the cornerstones of ``modern'' computational geometry. The interested
reader should have a look at the books by Mulmuley \cite{m-cgitr-94},
Sharir and Agarwal \cite{sa-dsstg-95}, \Matousek{} \cite{m-ldg-02},
and Boissonnat and Yvinec \cite{by-ag-98}.
