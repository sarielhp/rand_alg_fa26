\section{Bi-tension}

Our construction of good expanders, would use the idea of composing
graphs together. To this end, in our analysis, we will need the notion
of bi-tension. Let $\DEdges(\Graph)$ be the set of \emph{directed}
edges of $\Graph$; that is, every edge $xy \in E(\Graph)$ appears
twice as $\dirEdge{x}{y}$ and $\dirEdge{y}{x}$ in $\DEdges$.

\begin{defn}
    For a graph $\Graph$, let $\BiDialX{\Graph}$ denote the
    \emphi{bi-tension} of $\Graph$; that is, the smallest constant,
    such that for any two function $f,g :V(\Graph) \rightarrow \Re$,
    we have that
    \begin{align}
        \ExExt{x,y \in V}{\cardin{f(x)- g(y)}^2} \leq
        \BiDialX{\Graph} \ExExt{ \dirEdge{x}{y} \in \DEdges\;
        }{\cardin{f(x)- g(y)}^2}.
        \eqlab{b:i:tension}
    \end{align}
\end{defn}



The proof of the following lemma is similar to the proof of
\lemref{expansion}. The proof is provided for the sake of
completeness, but there is little new in it.

\begin{fragment}{\deflabel{b_i_expansion}}
\begin{lemma}
    Let $\Graph=(V,E)$ be a connected $d$-regular graph with $n$
    vertices. Then $\ds \BiDialX{\Graph} = \frac{1}{1-\EVal}\,$, where
    $\EVal = \EVal(\Graph)$, where $\EVal(\Graph) = \max\pth{\TEV{2},
       -\TEV{n} }$, where $\TEV{i}$ is the $i$th largest eigenvalue of
    the random walk matrix associated with $\Graph$.

    \lemlab{b:i:expansion}
\end{lemma}
\end{fragment}

\begin{proof}
    We can assume that $\Ex{f(x)}=0$. As such, we have that
    \begin{align}
        \eqlab{reduction:2} &\hspace{-1cm} \ExExt{x,y \in
           V}{\cardin{f(x)- g(y)}^2} =%
        \ExExt{x,y \in V}{\pth[]{f(x)}^2} -2 \ExExt{x,y \in V}{ f(x)
           g(y)} + \ExExt{y \in V}{\pth[]{g(y)}^2} =%
        \ExExt{x,y \in V}{\pth[]{f(x)}^2} + \ExExt{y \in
           V}{\pth[]{g(y)}^2}.
    \end{align}

    Let $\TMtrx$ be the matrix associated with the random walk on
    $\Graph$ (each entry is either zero or $1/d$), we have
    \begin{align*}
        \rho = \ExExt{\dirEdge{x}{y} \in \DEdges\;}{
           \cardin{f(x)-g(y)}^2 } & = \frac{1}{nd}
        \sum_{\dirEdge{x}{y} \in \DEdges\;}\pth{ f(x) -g(y) }^2 =
        \frac{1}{n} \sum_{x,y \in V} \TMtrx_{xy} \pth{f(x) - g(y)}^2\\
        &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in V} \pth{\pth{f(x)}^2 +\pth{ g(x)}^2}
        - \frac{2}{n} \sum_{x,y \in V} \TMtrx_{xy} f(x)g(y).
    \end{align*}
    Let $\Basis(\TMtrx)= \permut{v_1,\ldots, v_n}$ be the orthonormal
    eigenvector basis defined by $\TMtrx$ (see
    \defref{orthonormal:basis}), with eigenvalues $\TEV{1}
    \geq \TEV{2} \geq \cdots \geq \TEV{n}$, respectively. Write
    $f=\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i v_i$ and $g=\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i
    v_i$. Since $\Ex{f(x)} = 0$, we have that $\alpha_1 = 0$. Now,
    $\TMtrx_{xy}= \TMtrx_{yx}$, and we have
    \begin{align*}
        \sum_{x,y \in V} \TMtrx_{xy} f(x) g(y) &= \sum_{x,y \in V}
        \TMtrx_{yx} \pth{\sum_{i} \alpha_iv_i(x)} \pth{\sum_{j}
           \beta_jv_j(y)} = \sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \beta_j \sum_{y\in V}
        v_j(y) \sum_{x \in V} \TMtrx_{yx} v_i(x) %
        \\ & =%
        \sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \beta_j \sum_{y\in V} v_j(y) \pth{\TEV{i}
           v_i \pth{y} \MakeBig} =%
        \sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \beta_j \TEV{i} \DotProd{v_j}{v_i}
        %
        =%
        \sum_{i=2}^n \alpha_i \beta_i \TEV{i} \sum_{y\in
           V}\pth{v_i(y)}^2 \\%
        &\leq %
        \EVal \sum_{i=2}^n \frac{\alpha_i^2 + \beta_i^2}{2}\sum_{y\in
           V}\pth{v_i(y)}^2 \leq \frac{\EVal}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n
        \sum_{y\in V}\pth{ \pth{\alpha_iv_i(y)}^2 +
           \pth{\beta_iv_i(y)}^2 }\\
        %
        &=%
        \frac{\EVal}{2} \sum_{y\in V} \pth{ \pth{f(y)}^2 + \pth{g(y)}^2
        }
    \end{align*}
    As such,
    \begin{align*}
        \ExExt{\dirEdge{x}{y} \in \DEdges\;}{ \cardin{f(x)-g(y)}^2 }
        &= \frac{1}{nd}\sum_{\dirEdge{x}{y} \in \DEdges\;}
        \cardin{f(x)-g(y)}^2 
        %
        = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V} \pth{\pth{f(y)}^2 +\pth{ g(y)}^2} -
        \frac{1}{n} \sum_{x,y \in V} \frac{2 f(x)g(y)}{d}\\
        &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V} \pth{\pth{f(y)}^2 +\pth{ g(y)}^2} -
        \frac{2}{n} \sum_{x,y \in V} \TMtrx_{xy} f(x)g(y)\\
        & \geq \pth{ \frac{1}{n}- \frac{2}{n} \cdot \frac{\EVal}{2} }
        \sum_{y\in V} \pth{\pth{f(y)}^2 +\pth{ g(y)}^2} =
        \pth{1-\EVal} \pth{ \ExExt{y \in V}{\pth[]{f(y)}^2} +
           \ExExt{y \in V}{\pth[]{g(y)}^2} }\\
        &=%
        \pth{1-\EVal} \ExExt{x,y \in V}{\cardin{f(x)- g(y)}^2},
    \end{align*}
    by \Eqref{reduction:2}.  This implies that $\BiDialX{\Graph} \leq
    {1}/\pth[]{1-\EVal\,}$. Again, by trying either $f=g= v_2$ or $f =
    v_n$ and $g= -v_n$, we get that the inequality above holds with
    equality, which implies $\displaystyle \BiDialX{\Graph} \geq {1} /
    \pth[]{1-\EVal\,}$. Together, the claim now follows.
\end{proof}

We state the following result but do not prove it since we do not need
it for our nafarious purposes (however, we did prove the left side of
the inequality).
\begin{theorem}
    Let $\Graph$ be a $\delta$-expander with adjacency matrix $\Mtrx$
    and let $\lambda_2 = \lambda_2(\Graph)$ be the second-largest
    eigenvalue of $\Mtrx$. Then 
    \[
    \frac{1}{2} \pth{1- \frac{\lambda_2}{d}} \leq \delta\leq
    \sqrt{2\pth{1 - \frac{\lambda_2}{d}}}.
    \]
\end{theorem}

What the above theorem says, is that the expansion of a
\Expander{n}{d}{\delta} is a function of how far is its second
eigenvalue (i.e., $\lambda_2$) from its first eigenvalue (i.e.,
$d$). This is usually referred to as the \emphi{spectral gap}.

We will start by explicitly constructing an expander that has ``many''
edges, and then we will show to reduce its degree till it become a
constant degree expander.



\subsection{Explicit construction of a small expander}

\subsubsection{A quicky reminder of fields}

A \emphi{field} is a set $\Field$ together with two operations, called
addition and multiplication, and denoted by $+$ and $\cdot$,
respectively, such that the following axioms hold:
\begin{compactenumi}
    \item Closure: $\forall x,y \in \Field$, we have $x+y \in \Field$
    and $x\cdot y \in \Field$.

    \item Associativity: $\forall x,y,z \in \Field$, we have $x+(y+z)
    = (x+y)+z$ and $(x \cdot y ) \cdot z =x \cdot (y\cdot z)$.

    \item Commutativity: $\forall x,y \in \Field$, we have $x+y = y+x$
    and $x\cdot y = y\cdot x$.

    \item Identity: There exists two distinct special elements $0,1
    \in \Field$. We have that $\forall x \in \Field$ it holds $x+0 =
    a$ and $x\cdot 1 = x$.

    \item Inverse: There exists two distinct special elements $0,1
    \in \Field$, and we have that $\forall x \in \Field$ there exists an
    element $-x \in \Field$, such that $x+ (-x)=0$.

    Similarly, $\forall x \in \Field$, $x \ne 0$, there exists an
    element $y= x^{-1} = 1/x \in \Field$ such that $x \cdot y = 1$.

    \item Distributivity: $\forall x,y,z \in \Field$ we have
    $x\cdot(y+z) = x\cdot y + x\cdot z$.
\end{compactenumi}


Let $q = 2^t$, and $r>0$ be an integer. Consider the finite field
$\Field_{q}$. It is the field of polynomials of degree at most $t-1$,
where the coefficients are over $\mathbb{Z}_2$ (i.e., all calculations
are done modulus 2).  Formally, consider the polynomial 
\[
p(x) = x^t + x +1.
\]
It it irreducible over $\Field_2 =\brc{0,1}$ (i.e., $p(0) = p(1) \ne
0$). We can now do polynomial arithmetic over polynomials (with
coefficients from $\Field_2$), where we do the calculations modulus
$p(x)$. Note, that any irreducible polynomial of degree $n$ yields the
same field up to isomorphism. Intuitively, we are introducing the $n$
distinct roots of $p(x)$ into $\Field$ by creating an extension field
of $\Field$ with those roots.


An element of $\Field_q=\Field_{2^t}$ can be interpreted as a binary
string $b = b_0b_1\ldots,b_{t-1}$ of length $t$, where the
corresponding polynomial is 
\[
\mathrm{poly}(b) =\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}
b_i x^i.
\]
The nice property of $\Field_q$ is that addition can be interpreted as
a {\ttfamily x{o}r} operation. That is, for any $x,y \in \Field_q$, we have
that $x+y+y = x$ and $x-y -y = x$. The key properties of $\Field_q$ we
need is that multiplications and addition can be computed in it in
polynomial time in $t$, and it is a field (i.e., each non-zero element
has a unique inverse).

\paragraph{Computing multiplication in $\Field_q$.} Consider two
elements $\alpha, \beta \in \Field_q$. Multiply the two polynomials
$\mathrm{poly}(\alpha)$ by $\mathrm{poly}(\beta)$, let
$\mathrm{poly}(\gamma)$ be the resulting polynomial (of degree at most
$2t-2$), and compute the remainder $\mathrm{poly}(\beta)$ when
dividing it by the irreducible polynomial $p(x)$. For this remainder
polynomial, normalize the coefficients by computing their modules base
$2$. The resulting polynomial is the product of $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

\bigskip


For more details on this field, see any standard text on abstract
algebra.


\subsubsection{The construction}


Let $q = 2^t$, and $r>0$ be an integer. Consider the linear space
$\LSpace = \Field_q^{\,r}$.  Here, a member $\alpha =
(\alpha_0,\ldots, \alpha_r) \in \LSpace$ can be thought of as being a
string (of length $r+1$) over $\Field_q$, or alternatively, as a
binary string of length $n=t(r+1)$.


For $\alpha  = (\alpha_0,\ldots, \alpha_r) \in \LSpace$, and $x,y \in
\Field_q$, define the operator
\[
\rho(\alpha,x,y) = \alpha + y \cdot\pth{ 1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^r} =
\pth{\alpha_0 + y, \alpha_1 + y x, \alpha_2 + y x^2, \ldots, \alpha_r+
   y x^r} \in \LSpace.
\]
Since addition over $\Field_q$ is equivalent to a x{o}r operation we
have that 
\begin{align*}
    \rho\pth{\MakeBig \rho(\alpha,x,y), x, y} &= \pth{\alpha_0 + y +
       y, \alpha_1 + y x + yx, \alpha_2 + y x^2+yx^2, \ldots,
       \alpha_r+ y
       x^r+yx^r}\\
    &= \pth{\alpha_0, \alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots, \alpha_r} = \alpha.
\end{align*}
Furthermore, if $(x,y) \ne (x',y')$ then $\rho(\alpha,x,y) \ne
\rho(\alpha,x',y')$.

We now define a graph $\LD{q}{r}=\pth{\LSpace, E}$, where
\[
E= \brc{ \alpha \beta \sep{ \begin{array}{l}
          \alpha \in \LSpace, x, y \in \Field_q\\
          \beta = \rho(\alpha,x,y)
          \end{array}}}
\]
Note, that this graph is well defined, as $\rho(\beta,x,y)
=\alpha$. The degree of a vertex of $\LD{q}{r}$ is
$\cardin{\Field_q}^2 = q^2$, and $\LD{q}{r}$ has $N = \cardin{\LSpace}
= q^{r+1} = 2^{t(r+1)}=2^n$ vertices.

\begin{fragment}{\deflabel{explicit}}
\begin{theorem}
    For any $t > 0, r>0$ and $q = 2^t$, where $r<q$, we have that
    $\LD{q}{r}$ is a graph with $q^{r+1}$ vertices. Furthermore,
    $\lambda_1\pth{\LD{q}{r}} = q^2$, and $\lambda_i\pth{\LD{q}{r}}
    \leq rq$, for $i=2,\ldots, n$.

    In particular, if $r \leq q/2$, then $\LD{q}{r}$ is a
    \Expander{\MakeBig q^{r+1}}{q^2}{\frac{1}{4}}.

    \thmlab{explicit}
\end{theorem}
\end{fragment}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\Mtrx$ be the $N \times N$ adjacency matrix of
    $\LD{q}{r}$. Let $L:\Field_q \rightarrow \brc{0,1}$ be a linear
    map which is onto. It is easy to verify that $\cardin{L^{-1}(0)} =
    \cardin{L^{-1}(1)}$\footnote{Indeed, if $Z = L^{-1}(0)$, and $L(x)
       = 1$, then $L(y)=1$, for all $y \in U = \brc{x+z \sep{z \in
             Z}}$. Now, its clear that $\cardin{Z} = \cardin{U}$.}

    We are interested in the eigenvalues of the matrix $\Mtrx$. To
    this end, we consider vectors in $\Re^N$. The $i$th row an $i$th
    column of $\Mtrx$ is associated with a unique element $b_i \in
    \LSpace$. As such, for a vector $v \in \Re^N$, we denote by
    $\Entry{v}{b_i}$ the $i$th coordinate of $v$. In particular, for
    $\alpha =\pth{\alpha_0, \ldots, \alpha_r} \in \LSpace$, let
    $v_\alpha\in \Re^N$ denote the vector, where its $\beta
    =\pth{\beta_0, \ldots, \beta_r} \in \LSpace$ coordinate is
    \[
    \Entry{v_{\alpha}}{\beta} = \pth{-1}^{L\pth[]{\sum_{i=0}^r
          \alpha_i\, \beta_i}}.
    \]
    Let $V = \brc{v_\alpha \sep{ \alpha \in \LSpace}}$. For $\alpha
    \ne \alpha' \in V$, observe that
    \[
    \DotProd{v_{\alpha}}{v_{\alpha'}} = \sum_{\beta\in \LSpace}
    \pth{-1}^{L\pth[]{\sum_{i=0}^r \alpha_i\, \beta_i}} \cdot
    \pth{-1}^{L\pth[]{\sum_{i=0}^r \alpha_i'\, \beta_i}} =
    \sum_{\beta\in \LSpace} \pth{-1}^{L\pth[]{\sum_{i=0}^r
          \pth{\alpha_i+\alpha_i'}\, \beta_i}}
    = \sum_{\beta \in \LSpace} \Entry{v_{\alpha+\alpha'}}{\beta}.
    \]
    So, consider $\psi=\alpha+\alpha' \ne 0$. Assume, for the
    simplicity of exposition that all the  coordinates of $\psi$ are
    non-zero. We have, by the linearity of $L$ that 
    \[
    \DotProd{v_{\alpha}}{v_{\alpha'}} = \sum_{\beta \in \LSpace}
    \pth{-1}^{L\pth[]{\sum_{i=0}^r \alpha_i\, \beta_i}} =
    \sum_{\beta_0 \in \Field_q, \ldots, \beta_{r-1} \in \Field_q} \;\;
    \pth{-1}^{L\pth[]{ \psi_0\, \beta_0 + \cdots +
          \psi_{r-1}\,\beta_{r-1}}} \sum_{\beta_r \in \Field_q}
    \pth{-1}^{L\pth[]{ \psi_r\, \beta_r}}.
    \]
    However, since $\psi_r \ne 0$, the quantity $\brc{\psi_r
       \beta_r\sep{ \beta_r \in \Field_q}} = \Field_q$.  Thus, the
    summation $\sum_{\beta_r \in \Field_q} \pth{-1}^{L\pth[]{ \psi_r\,
          \beta_r}}$ gets $\cardin{L^{-1}(0)}$ terms that are $1$, and
    $\cardin{L^{-1}(0)}$ terms that are $-1$. As such, this summation
    is zero, implying that $\DotProd{v_{\alpha}}{v_{\alpha'}} =
    0$. Namely, the vectors of $V$ are orthogonal.

    Observe, that for $\alpha,\beta,\psi \in \LSpace$, we have
    $\Entry{v_\alpha}{\beta + \psi} =\Entry{v_\alpha}{\beta}
    \Entry{v_\alpha}{\psi}$. For $\alpha \in \LSpace$, consider the
    vector $\Mtrx v_\alpha$. We have, for $\beta \in \LSpace$, that
    \begin{align*}
        \Entry{\pth{\Mtrx v_\alpha}}{\beta} = \sum_{\psi \in \LSpace}
        \Mtrx_{\beta \psi} \cdot \Entry{v_\alpha}{\psi} %
        &=%
        \sum_{\substack{x,y\; \in\; \Field_q\\ \psi =\rho(\beta,x,y)}}
        \Entry{v_\alpha}{\psi} =%
        \sum_{x,y\; \in\; \Field_q}
        \Entry{v_\alpha}{\beta+ y(1,x,\ldots, x^r)}\\
        &=%
        \pth{\sum_{x,y\; \in\; \Field_q}
           \Entry{v_\alpha}{y(1,x,\ldots, x^r)}} \cdot
        \Entry{v_\alpha}{\beta}.
    \end{align*}
    Thus, setting $\lambda(\alpha) = \sum_{x,y\; \in\; \Field_q}
    \Entry{v_\alpha}{y(1,x,\ldots, x^r)} \in \Re$, we have that $\Mtrx
    v_\alpha = \lambda(\alpha) \cdot v_\alpha$. Namely, $v_\alpha$ is
    an eigenvector, with eigenvalue $\lambda(\alpha)$.

    Let $p_\alpha(x) =\sum_{i=0}^r \alpha_i x^i$, and let
    \begin{align*}
         \lambda(\alpha)&= \sum_{x,y\; \in\; \Field_q}
        \Entry{v_\alpha}{y(1,x,\ldots, x^r)} \in \Re %
        = \sum_{x,y \in \Field_q} \pth{-1}^{L(y p_\alpha(x))}\\ %
        &= \sum_{\substack{x,y \in \Field_q\\p_\alpha(x)=0}}
        \pth{-1}^{L(y\; p_\alpha(x))} %
        + \sum_{\substack{x,y \in \Field_q\\p_\alpha(x)\ne 0}}
        \pth{-1}^{L(y\; p_\alpha(x))} .
    \end{align*}
    If $p_\alpha(x) = 0$ then $(-1)^{L(y\;p_\alpha(x))} = 1$, for all
    $y$. As such, each such $x$ contributes $q$ to $\lambda(\alpha)$.

    If $p_\alpha(x) \ne 0$ then $y\;p_\alpha(x)$ takes all the values
    of $\Field_q$, and as such, $L(y\;p_\alpha(x))$ is $0$ for half of
    these values, and $1$ for the other half. Implying that these kind
    terms contribute $0$ to $\lambda(\alpha)$. But $p_\alpha(x)$ is a
    polynomial of degree $r$, and as such there could be at most $r$
    values of $x$ for which the first term is taken. As such, if
    $\alpha \ne 0$ then $\lambda(\alpha) \leq rq$. If $\alpha = 0$
    then $\lambda(\alpha)=q^2$, which implies the theorem.
\end{proof}


\bigskip

This construction provides an expander with constant degree only if
the number of vertices is a constant.  Indeed, if we want an expander
with constant degree, we have to take $q$ to be as small as possible.
We get the relation $n = q^{r+1}\leq q^{q}$, since $r \leq r$, which
implies that $q = \Omega( \log n /\log \log n)$. Now, the expander of
\thmref{explicit} is $q^2$-regular, which means that it is not going
to provide us with a constant degree expander.

However, we are going to use it as our building block in a
construction that would start with this expander and would inflate it
up to the desired size.
